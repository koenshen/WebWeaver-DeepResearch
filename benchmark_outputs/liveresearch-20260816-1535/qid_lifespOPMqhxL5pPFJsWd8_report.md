

# High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE) in Ski Mountaineers at ≥3,000 m: A Comprehensive Research Report

## 1. Introduction

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a life-threatening, non-cardiogenic form of pulmonary edema that afflicts otherwise healthy individuals after rapid ascent to altitudes typically above 2,500–3,000 m. For ski mountaineers—who frequently ascend from low altitude to ski resorts or high-altitude camps in a matter of hours—the risk is particularly acute. This report synthesizes the current understanding of HAPE pathophysiology, reviews documented cases from the literature (with emphasis on the 2020–2026 period), evaluates acute and long-term treatment outcomes, and outlines evidence-based prevention and acclimatization protocols.

---

## 2. Predominant Pathophysiological Mechanisms

### 2.1 Exaggerated and Uneven Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction (HPV)

The central pathophysiological event in HAPE is an excessive, heterogeneous hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Hypobaric hypoxia at altitude triggers the pulmonary vasculature to constrict—a normal reflex that diverts blood from poorly ventilated lung regions. In HAPE-susceptible individuals, this response is both **exaggerated** (leading to mean pulmonary artery pressures of 35–50 mmHg, versus 20–30 mmHg in controls) and **uneven** across the vascular bed. The result is regional overperfusion: areas with less vasoconstriction receive a disproportionately high blood flow, raising capillary hydrostatic pressure beyond the threshold for edema formation (17–24 mmHg) [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-overview); [Physiological Reviews, 2012](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.01167.2004).

### 2.2 Capillary Stress Failure

When capillary pressure exceeds the tensile strength of the alveolar-capillary membrane, structural breaks occur—a mechanism termed **capillary stress failure**. This leads to a high-permeability leak of plasma proteins and erythrocytes into the alveolar space, even in the absence of inflammation. In early HAPE, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid shows high protein concentrations without elevated inflammatory markers, confirming that hydrostatic stress, not inflammation, is the primary driver [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-overview); [West et al., Thieme, 1991](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-2007-1024594).

### 2.3 Impaired Alveolar Fluid Clearance

Hypoxia directly inhibits epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in alveolar type I and II cells, reducing the active transport of sodium and thus the osmotic gradient driving water reabsorption. Hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia further impairs fluid clearance. This diminished clearance capacity compounds the hydrostatic leak, accelerating alveolar flooding [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-overview); [PMC, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3617508).

### 2.4 Endothelial Dysfunction and Nitric Oxide Deficiency

HAPE-susceptible individuals exhibit a reduced bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO) in exhaled air and in the pulmonary vasculature, along with elevated endothelin-1 (a potent vasoconstrictor). This imbalance promotes exaggerated HPV and further elevates pulmonary artery pressure. Genetic factors (e.g., polymorphisms in the eNOS and ACE genes) and mitochondrial DNA mutations have been linked to this susceptibility [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-overview); [Gatterer et al., Nat Rev Dis Primers, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41572-024-00526-w).

### 2.5 The Overarching Model

The current consensus model (2024 WMS guidelines) is that HAPE is a **hydrostatic, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema** driven by:
- Exaggerated, uneven hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
- Regional overperfusion and elevated capillary pressure
- Capillary stress failure with high-permeability leak
- Impaired alveolar fluid clearance
- A secondary inflammatory response in late/untreated cases

## 3. Documented Cases in the High-Altitude Medicine Literature (Focus on Ski Mountaineers, 2020–2026)

### 3.1 Classic Ski Resort Case: Sierra Nevada, Spain (2018, published 2020)

A landmark case report in *Archivos de Bronconeumología* describes a 40-year-old male, low-altitude resident, who ascended to Sierra Nevada (max. 3,300 m) in under 3 hours and skied intensively for 48 hours. He developed sudden dyspnea, cyanosis, tachycardiac (120 bpm), tachypnea (>30/min), and pink frothy sputum. Oxygen saturation was ~90% on FiO₂ 0.6. Chest auscultation revealed bilateral moist rales. ECG showed sinus tachycardia. This case illustrates the classic ski-mountaineering HAPE scenario: rapid ascent, heavy exertion, and symptom onset within 2–5 days [Arch Bronconeumol, 2020](https://www.archbronconeumol.org/en-high-altitude-acute-pulmonary-edema-after-articulo-S1579212918304233).

### 3.2 Delayed-Onset HAPE at Everest Base Camp (2023)

A case series published in *Health Science Reports* (2023) presented a 41-year-old male with a prior HAPE history who ascended from Lukla (2,860 m) to Everest Base Camp (5,364 m) over 4 days. On day 11 at base camp, he developed dyspnea and productive cough. POCUS confirmed HAPE. This case highlights that HAPE can occur beyond the typical 2–5 day window, especially in susceptible individuals [PMC, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10126756).

### 3.3 HAPE in a Himalayan Trekker with Concurrent Respiratory Infection (2014, widely cited)

A 55-year-old female trekker developed HAPE at 4,410 m in Nepal. She had a preceding sore throat and took antibiotics but continued ascending. Symptoms worsened at Tengboche (3,860 m), requiring emergency airlift to Kathmandu. This case underscores the synergistic risk of respiratory infection, exertion, and rapid ascent [PMC, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3984695).

### 3.4 HAPE in a Native Highlander Porter (2023)

A case report from Nepal described a 22-year-old porter who developed HAPE at 4,920 m with SpO₂ of 40% and a Lake Louise AMS score of 8. He was managed with oxygen, nifedipine, acetazolamide, and descent. This rare case demonstrates that HAPE is not exclusive to lowlanders; re-entry HAPE in altitude residents is well documented [JAMSA, 2023](https://jamsa.amsa-international.org/index.php/main/article/view/520).

### 3.5 Severe HAPE at a Remote Antarctic Research Station (2026)

A case report in *Life* (MDPI, 2026) describes acute severe HAPE at a remote Antarctic station. Management included descent, oxygen, and nifedipine with full recovery, illustrating that HAPE can occur in extreme cold, high-altitude environments beyond traditional mountain settings [MDPI, 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-1729/16/2/313).

### 3.6 Colorado Ski Resort Data (2025)

A retrospective analysis of 64 pediatric HAPE cases in Colorado mountain children (published 2025) found that the most prominent characteristics were rapid onset of symptoms at altitude with marked hypoxia that improved within hours on oxygen/descent. This mirrors the adult ski-mountaineering population [Authorea, 2025](https://www.authorea.com/doi/10.22541/au.173809433.31101165).

### 3.7 HAPE with COVID-19 Coinfection (2022)

A case report documented HAPE in an alpinist with concurrent COVID-19 pneumonia, demonstrating that infectious respiratory illness can lower the threshold for HAPE development [Frontiers, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1735083/full).

### 3.8 The HAPER-CAPER Randomized Controlled Trial (2025)

This landmark trial compared CPAP + oxygen vs. high-flow oxygen alone in HAPE treatment. Mean time to clinical resolution was ~158 minutes with CPAP + oxygen and ~178 minutes with high-flow oxygen alone, demonstrating that both modalities are highly effective [PMC, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12611343).

## 4. Acute and Long-Term Treatments with Best Outcomes

### 4.1 Acute Treatment: The Hierarchy of Interventions

**Immediate descent** remains the single most effective treatment. The 2024 WMS guidelines recommend descending at least 1,000 m (or until symptoms resolve) with minimal exertion (use motorized transport, animal transport, or carry by rescuers) [WMS 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013).

**Supplemental oxygen** (goal SpO₂ > 90%) is the first-line adjunct. High-flow oxygen alone can achieve clinical resolution in ~178 minutes per the HAPER-CAPER trial. CPAP or EPAP can be added when available [PMC, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12611343).

**Nifedipine** (30 mg extended-release every 12 hours, or 20 mg every 8 hours) is the recommended pharmacologic treatment when descent is delayed or oxygen is unavailable. It works by reducing pulmonary artery pressure. In the landmark placebo-controlled trial, nifedipine reduced HAPE incidence from 64% to 10% in susceptible individuals [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-treatment).

**Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors** (tadalafil 10 mg twice daily, sildenafil 50 mg every 8 hours) are alternatives when nifedipine is unavailable. Evidence for treatment is limited, but physiologic rationale is strong [WMS 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013).

**Dexamethasone** is **not routinely recommended** for isolated HAPE, as the pathophysiology is hydrostatic, not inflammatory. However, it should be added if neurologic dysfunction persists despite oxygen (suggesting concurrent HACE or hypoxic encephalopathy) [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-treatment).

**Acetazolamide** is **not recommended** for HAPE treatment, as it has no proven role in reducing pulmonary artery pressure [WMS 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013).

**Portable hyperbaric chambers** (e.g., Gamow bag) are effective when descent and oxygen are unavailable.

### 4.2 Long-Term Outcomes and Recovery

**Prognosis with prompt treatment is excellent.** Clinical recovery can be remarkably rapid—within minutes of descent or oxygen. In the HAPER-CAPER trial, mean time to resolution was ~2.5–3 hours. Complete clinical recovery may take 1–2 weeks, and imaging (CT) evidence of lung damage typically resolves within 1–6 months [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-overview); [UIAA Case Study](https://www.theuiaa.org/case-study-what-does-hape-feel-like).

**Long-term complications are rare** but include:
- **Pulmonary hypertension**: Prolonged or repeated HAPE episodes may cause persistent pulmonary vascular remodeling. In most individuals, PAP normalizes after descent, but some may develop chronic high-altitude pulmonary hypertension (HAPH) [StatPearls, 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK555925).
- **Recurrence**: The recurrence rate with rapid ascent (4,500 m in 1–2 days) is approximately **60%** among individuals with prior HAPE [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/high-altitude-pulmonary-edema); [American Lung Association](https://www.lung.org/blog/environmental-pulmonary-edema).
- **Right ventricular strain**: Persistent or severe pulmonary hypertension can lead to right heart strain, though this is reversible with descent in most cases.

**Follow-up**: After an episode, evaluation at a hospital is recommended. For recurrent HAPE, further diagnostic workup (echocardiography, lung function tests, and evaluation for patent foramen ovale or congenital heart disease) is warranted [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-treatment).

## 5. Acclimatization and Prevention Protocols

### 5.1 Gradual Ascent: The Foundation

The primary method for preventing HAPE is **gradual ascent**, allowing time for physiologic acclimatization:
- Above 2,500 m, increase sleeping altitude by no more than **300–500 m per day**
- Add a **rest day every 600–1,200 m** of altitude gain
- Do not sleep more than 300–500 m higher than the previous night
- Staged ascent: spending **6–7 days at moderate altitude (2,200–3,000 m)** before ascending to higher elevations blunts the pulmonary artery pressure response and reduces HAPE risk [WMS 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013); [AFP, 2020](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p505).

### 5.2 Preacclimatization

Preacclimatization via **intermittent hypoxic exposure** (normobaric hypoxia in a tent, >8 hours/day for several days before travel) or **living at moderate altitude** before ascent can reduce HAPE risk, although evidence for the optimal protocol remains uncertain [WMS 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013).

### 5.3 Pharmacologic Prophylaxis (Reserved for HAPE-Susceptible Individuals)

| Agent | Dose | Evidence Level | Notes |
|-------|------|----------------|-------|
| **Nifedipine (first-line)** | 30 mg ER every 12 hours, starting day before ascent, continue 4–7 days after reaching target altitude or until descent | Strong (RCT): reduced HAPE incidence from 64% to 10% | Preferred for those with prior HAPE |
| **Tadalafil (second-line)** | 10 mg every 12 hours | Moderate (RCT): reduced incidence in susceptible individuals | Alternative when nifedipine is contraindicated |
| **Dexamethasone (third-line)** | 8 mg every 12 hours | Moderate | May be used when other agents are unavailable; caution with rebound upon discontinuation [WMS 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013) |
| **Sildenafil** | 50 mg every 8 hours | Limited | Alternative PDE5 inhibitor |
| **Salmeterol** | Inhaled, 125 µg twice daily | Moderate (single RCT: reduced incidence from 74% to 33%) | Not recommended as sole agent by 2024 WMS; may be used as adjunct [Medscape, 2024](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300716-treatment) |
| **Acetazolamide** | Not recommended for HAPE prophylaxis | Insufficient evidence | May be considered for re-entry HAPE only |

### 5.4 Behavioral Prevention for Ski Mountaineers

Specific recommendations for ski mountaineers and backcountry skiers:
- **Avoid rapid ascent**: Many ski resorts have base elevations >2,500 m (e.g., Breckenridge, CO at 2,926 m; Val Thorens, France at 2,300 m). Sleeping at such elevations on the first night after a sea-level departure is a major risk factor.
- **Limit exertion on days 1–3**: Heavy skiing or mountaineering during the first 2–5 days at altitude increases cardiac output and pulmonary artery pressure, precipitating HAPE. The Sierra Nevada case report specifically highlights intensive skiing over 48 hours as a trigger [Arch Bronconeumol, 2020](https://www.archbronconeumol.org/en-high-altitude-acute-pulmonary-edema-after-articulo-S1579212918304233).
- **Stay warm**: Cold exposure increases sympathetic tone and pulmonary artery pressure.
- **Hydrate and avoid alcohol/sedatives**: These impair respiratory drive and fluid balance.
- **Recognize early symptoms**: Unusual breathlessness, dry cough, and decreased exercise tolerance are early warning signs. Do not ascend further.
- **Monitor SpO₂**: Values <70% at rest or >10 points below age-matched norms at the same altitude warrant immediate descent.

### 5.5 Risk Stratification for Recurrence

The 2024 WMS guidelines and a dedicated review (Luks & Grissom, 2024) recommend the following for individuals with **recurrent HAPE**:
- Thorough evaluation for underlying conditions: echocardiography (to rule out pulmonary hypertension, PFO, congenital heart disease), pulmonary function tests, and cardiopulmonary exercise testing
- If no underlying pathology is found, a **multipronged approach** is indicated: slow ascent, staged acclimatization, and pharmacologic prophylaxis (nifedipine first-line)
- In rare cases, if predisposing factors (e.g., severe pulmonary hypertension, absent pulmonary artery) are found, future high-altitude travel may be contraindicated [PubMed, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38682380).

## 6. Summary of Evidence Gaps and Future Directions (2020–2026)

The 2020–2026 period has seen important advances:
- **HAPER-CAPER trial** (2025) established CPAP as a viable adjunct to oxygen
- **2024 WMS guidelines** clarified the role of PDE5 inhibitors and dexamethasone in HAPE treatment
- **New pathophysiologic insights** (mitochondrial DNA mutations, oxidative stress, metabolic reprogramming) were identified (Sharma et al., 2021)
- **Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)** has emerged as a valuable tool for early detection and monitoring (Lahham et al., 2023)

Key remaining uncertainties:
- Optimal duration and protocol for preacclimatization
- Long-term pulmonary vascular outcomes after repeated HAPE
- The role of PDE5 inhibitors in established HAPE treatment (lack of RCTs)
- Whether combination pharmacotherapy (e.g., nifedipine + salmeterol) is superior to monotherapy

---

## 7. Conclusion

HAPE in ski mountaineers at ≥3,000 m is a **hydrostatic, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema** driven by exaggerated, uneven hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, capillary stress failure, and impaired alveolar fluid clearance. The literature documents numerous cases across ski resorts and high-altitude expeditions, with the Sierra Nevada case (2020) serving as a prototypical example of the ski-mountaineering scenario. **Immediate descent and supplemental oxygen** are the cornerstones of treatment, with nifedipine as the primary pharmacologic adjunct. **Recovery is excellent** with prompt intervention, but the **recurrence rate is ~60%** with rapid re-ascent, making prevention paramount. **Gradual ascent, staged acclimatization, and pharmacologic prophylaxis (nifedipine first-line)** are the most effective strategies for reducing recurrence. The 2024 WMS guidelines and the 2025 HAPER-CAPER trial provide the most current evidence for optimizing outcomes in this high-risk population.
