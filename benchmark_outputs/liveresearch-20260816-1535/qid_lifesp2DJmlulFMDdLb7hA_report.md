

# Comparative Efficacy of Training and Nutrition Interventions for Body Composition in Recreationally Active Adults (18–35): A Comprehensive Evidence Synthesis (2015–2026)

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes the international peer-reviewed literature and consensus guidelines (2015–2026) on the comparative efficacy of training and nutrition interventions for reducing adiposity while preserving or increasing lean body mass in recreationally active adults aged 18–35. The evidence base demonstrates that (1) **protein intake of ~1.6 g/kg/day** is sufficient for lean mass gain during energy balance, with **2.3–3.1 g/kg/day** recommended during caloric deficits; (2) **resistance training (RT) is the primary exercise modality for lean mass preservation**, and the 2026 ACSM Position Stand de-emphasizes periodization in favor of volume ≥10 sets/muscle group/week; (3) **HIIT and MICT produce comparable body composition improvements**, with HIIT offering modest advantages in percent body fat reduction and time efficiency; (4) a **caloric deficit of ~500 kcal/day** represents the threshold at which RT's lean mass protective effect is neutralized; (5) **meal timing has negligible independent effects** when total protein and calories are controlled, though **even protein distribution (~0.4 g/kg/meal across 3–4 meals)** enhances muscle protein synthesis; and (6) **long-term maintenance requires sustained RT, higher protein intake, ongoing behavioral support, and self-monitoring**.

## 1. Protein Intake Targets

### 1.1 Lean Mass Gain During Energy Balance

The landmark systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression by **Morton et al. (2018)** in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* analyzed 49 studies with 1,863 participants and found that dietary protein supplementation augments resistance training-induced gains in fat-free mass (FFM). Biphasic regression identified a **break point of ~1.62 g/kg/day** (95% CI: 1.03–2.20), beyond which additional protein provided no further benefit for FFM gains. The authors recommended up to **2.2 g/kg/day** for those seeking to maximize muscle hypertrophy. Notably, the effect was greater in younger and resistance-trained individuals and diminished with age. This study excluded energy-restricted participants, so the threshold applies to weight maintenance or surplus conditions.

**Source:** https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376

A subsequent systematic review and meta-analysis by **Tagawa et al. (2022)** in the *Journal of Nutrition* examined 65 studies with 2,907 participants and confirmed that protein supplementation significantly improved lean body mass (mean difference: 0.62 kg; 95% CI: 0.36–0.88) in adults. Subgroup analysis found that younger adults (<65 years) required **≥1.6 g/kg/day** to achieve significant gains (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.17–0.43, moderate certainty).

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8978023

### 1.2 Lean Mass Preservation During Caloric Deficit

The **International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) Position Stand (2017)** provides the most authoritative consensus on protein requirements during energy restriction. The ISSN recommends:

- **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising individuals during energy balance
- **2.3–3.1 g/kg/day** to maximize retention of lean body mass in resistance-trained subjects during hypocaloric periods
- Preliminary evidence suggests **>3.0 g/kg/day** may further promote fat mass loss

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153

A systematic review of protein during caloric restriction in resistance-trained athletes by **Helms et al. (2014)** made a case for higher intakes, and subsequent work by **Hector and Phillips (2018)** in the *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* recommended **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** during weight loss for athletes, with severity of the caloric deficit determining where within this range an individual should aim.

**Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182451

The **BASES Expert Statement on protein recommendations for athletes (2022)** similarly recommends **≥1.6 g/kg/day** during energy balance and **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** during energy deficit for lean mass retention.

**Source:** https://www.cases.org.uk/imgs/bases_tses_spring_2022_online_expert_statement683.pdf

A **2026 scoping review** of international consensus statements, position stands, and practice guidelines (PMC12513969) found that recommendations for daily protein intake during body composition manipulation have converged over time. Post-2019, all six included papers consistently recommended **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day**. Protein distribution across **3–5 meals** or every **3–4 hours**, with per-meal portions of **0.25–0.50 g/kg body mass** (or 20–40 g absolute), was commonly advised. The review also identified a consistent minimum energy availability threshold of **30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day** to support athlete health.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513969

### 1.3 Protein During Concurrent High-Volume Training in a Deficit

A systematic review by **Roth, Schoenfeld, and Behringer (2022)** in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* examined lean mass sparing in resistance-trained athletes during caloric restriction. Mean protein consumption in the included studies was **3.35 g/kg FFM in females and 3.06 g/kg FFM in males** (range: 2.12–4.00 g/kg FFM). The authors noted that these high intakes may have allowed maintained RT performance, indirectly helping to spare lean mass. Importantly, the review found that **studies increasing RT volume over time during caloric restriction appeared to demonstrate no-to-low lean mass loss** compared to studies reducing volume.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9012799

## 2. Resistance Training Periodization and Volume

### 2.1 The 2026 ACSM Position Stand

The **American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) Position Stand on Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults (2026)** represents the most comprehensive evidence-based guidance to date. This landmark document synthesized findings from **137 systematic reviews** involving more than **30,000 participants**, updating the previous 2009 guidelines.

**Key findings for hypertrophy:**

- **Volume is the primary driver:** Muscle hypertrophy was enhanced by **≥10 sets per muscle group per week** and by eccentric overload
- **Periodization is not superior to non-periodized programs:** The Position Stand concluded that "with appropriate progressive overload, periodization is not significantly superior to nonperiodized programs; thus, periodization is less important than previously hypothesized for healthy adults to improve muscle function and hypertrophy"
- **Load range is broad:** Hypertrophy was not meaningfully changed by loads ranging from low to high when effort was sufficient
- **Training to failure, equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, and blood flow restriction** did not consistently impact training outcomes
- **Consistency beats complexity:** The most meaningful gains come from regular participation in any form of resistance training

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823

A **2026 systematic review and meta-analysis** by Zhang et al. in *Frontiers in Public Health* comparing linear and undulating periodization found that both approaches demonstrated similar effects on enhancing athletic capacity, improving body composition, and regulating metabolic health markers.

**Source:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1707627/full

### 2.2 Practical Recommendations for Recreationally Active Adults

Based on the 2026 ACSM guidelines, for hypertrophy, the recommended prescription is:
- **Volume:** ≥10 sets per muscle group per week
- **Load:** Wide range (30–100% 1RM) with sufficient effort
- **Frequency:** ≥2 sessions per week
- **Progressive overload:** Essential; periodization is optional
- **Key lifts:** Perform earlier in the session

**Source:** https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026

## 3. HIIT vs. MICT for Body Composition

### 3.1 Meta-Analytic Evidence

A comprehensive **systematic review and meta-analysis (2023)** published in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* analyzed 29 RCTs and found that both HIIT and MICT significantly improved body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness, with the exception of fat-free mass (neither modality significantly increased FFM). Between-group analyses found that **HIIT provided greater benefits for waist circumference, percent fat mass, and VO₂peak** compared to MICT. The advantage was influenced by age (18–45 years), obesity status, intervention duration (>6 weeks), and HIIT interval characteristics. The authors concluded that the low time cost makes HIIT a more suitable exercise prescription for young and middle-aged adults.

**Source:** https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/6/4741

A **2026 network meta-analysis** published in *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation* specifically examined overweight/obese adults and found that **HIIT ≥75 min/week ranked first** for reducing body fat percentage (SUCRA: 84.74%) and waist circumference (SUCRA: 74.63%), outperforming both MICT ≥150 min/week and HIIT + resistance training. The analysis also found that HIIT ≥75 min/week was associated with greater reductions in body fat percentage than MICT ≥150 min/week (MD = −0.89, 95% CrI: −1.71 to −0.07).

**Source:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-026-01686-w

A **2025 meta-analysis** in *Frontiers in Physiology* focusing on overweight/obese adolescents found that HIIT and MICT produced comparable effects on most outcomes, with HIIT showing a modest, borderline significant advantage in reducing body fat percentage, while MICT appeared more effective for waist circumference reduction in running-based protocols.

**Source:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1636792/full

### 3.2 Key Distinctions and Practical Implications

The earlier **Wewege et al. (2017)** meta-analysis in *Obesity Reviews* found no significant differences between HIIT and MICT for any body composition measure, but HIIT required **~40% less training time**. This time efficiency advantage has been consistently replicated across subsequent meta-analyses.

**Source:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/obr.12532

**Consensus:** HIIT and MICT both improve body composition in recreationally active adults. HIIT offers modest advantages for percent body fat and abdominal fat reduction with greater time efficiency. Neither modality alone significantly increases FFM; resistance training is required for hypertrophy.

## 4. Caloric Deficit Magnitude and Lean Mass Preservation

### 4.1 The ~500 kcal/Day Threshold

The critical **meta-analysis and meta-regression by Murphy and Koehler (2022)** in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* examined the impact of energy deficiency on resistance training outcomes. The key finding was that **an energy deficit of ~500 kcal/day completely ablated the accretion of lean mass** in response to resistance training. At deficits below this threshold, resistance training could still produce lean mass gains (though attenuated); at deficits above ~500 kcal/day, lean mass loss occurred despite resistance training. Importantly, strength gains were not significantly impaired by energy deficits (ES = −0.31, p = 0.28), indicating that strength can be maintained even when muscle growth is not possible.

**Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696

The meta-regression also revealed that a 500 kcal/day deficit and aging 30 years produced a similar effect on the predicted change in lean mass in response to RT (ES = −0.15), contextualizing the magnitude of the impairment.

### 4.2 Other Evidence on Deficit Magnitude

The systematic review by **Roth, Schoenfeld, and Behringer (2022)** reported that estimated energy deficits in the included studies ranged from **−250 kcal/day to −882 kcal/day** (mean: −347 kcal/day in females, −398 kcal/day in males). The authors emphasized that once the deficit becomes too severe, even anabolic stimuli such as high-volume RT may be unable to counteract diet-induced anabolic resistance.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9012799

The **scoping review of consensus statements (2026)** found that the recommended calorie deficit for athletes seeking body composition manipulation ranges from **250–1,000 kcal/day**, with a fat loss rate of **0.5–1.0 kg/week**, and a minimum energy availability of **30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day**.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513969

### 4.3 Practical Recommendations

| Deficit Magnitude | Expected Outcome with RT + Adequate Protein |
|---|---|
| <500 kcal/day | Possible lean mass gain (attenuated), fat loss |
| ~500 kcal/day | Lean mass maintenance, fat loss |
| >500 kcal/day | Lean mass loss likely despite RT |

**Higher protein intake (2.3–3.1 g/kg/day) is essential for lean mass preservation during deficits.** The **Longland et al. (2016)** study demonstrated that in young men under ~40% energy deficit, those consuming **2.4 g/kg/day** protein gained 1.2 ± 1.0 kg lean mass, while those consuming 1.2 g/kg/day only maintained lean mass, despite both groups performing intensive concurrent training.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9012799 (citing Longland et al., 2016, *Am J Clin Nutr*)

## 5. Meal-Timing Protocols

### 5.1 Protein Timing and the "Anabolic Window"

A systematic review with meta-analysis published in *Nutrients* (2025) examined the effects of protein ingestion timing on exercise-induced adaptations. The authors concluded that the evidence for a narrow "anabolic window" is supported by **low to very low certainty**, and the timing of protein intake relative to exercise (before vs. after) does not significantly affect strength or body composition outcomes when total daily protein intake is adequate.

**Source:** https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/13/2070

A 2026 evidence synthesis examining seven controlled studies across five distinct meal-timing variables (eating windows, meal frequency, breakfast timing, late-night eating, and fasted exercise) found **high certainty convergence**: total daily calorie and protein intake are the primary determinants of body composition outcomes. The anabolic window was found to be effectively **total protein intake in disguise**.

**Source:** https://fitchef.com/claims/meal-timing-total-intake

### 5.2 Protein Distribution Across Meals

The **Mamerow et al. (2014)** study in *The Journal of Nutrition* demonstrated that an even protein distribution (~30 g per meal across three meals) produced a **25% higher 24-hour muscle protein synthesis rate** compared to a skewed distribution (10 g breakfast, 15 g lunch, 65 g dinner), despite identical total daily protein intake.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4018950

The **Areta et al. (2013)** study found that **20 g doses every 3 hours** (four doses) were most effective for stimulating myofibrillar protein synthesis after resistance exercise, compared to 10 g every 1.5 hours or 40 g every 6 hours.

**Source:** https://massresearchreview.com/2023/05/22/protein-distribution-matters-to-an-extent

The 2026 scoping review of consensus statements found that **3–5 meals per day** with protein intervals of **3–5 hours** and per-meal portions of **0.25–0.50 g/kg body mass** (or 20–40 g) represent the evidence-based consensus for optimizing muscle protein synthesis.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513969

### 5.3 Time-Restricted Eating

A 2025 RCT in the *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* found that combining high-protein time-restricted eating with resistance training reduced adipose tissue while preserving fat-free mass in women with overweight. However, the primary driver appeared to be the high protein intake and caloric restriction rather than the eating window per se.

**Source:** https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/35/6/article-p493.xml

### 5.4 Practical Recommendations

- **Total daily protein intake is the primary determinant**, not timing
- **Even protein distribution** across 3–4 meals at ~0.4 g/kg/meal enhances muscle protein synthesis
- **Post-exercise protein** (20–40 g or 0.25–0.4 g/kg) is beneficial but not uniquely superior to other meals if total intake is adequate
- **Pre-sleep protein** (≥40 g) may enhance overnight muscle protein synthesis
- **Meal frequency** beyond 3–4 meals per day does not provide additional benefits when total intake is controlled

## 6. Evidence-Based Maintenance Strategies

### 6.1 The Challenge of Long-Term Weight Maintenance

The long-term maintenance of weight loss and body composition improvements is recognized as the most challenging phase of obesity management. Physiological adaptations, including reduced resting metabolic rate and increased appetite, promote weight regain. The **National Weight Control Registry (NWCR)**, the longest-running study of successful weight loss maintainers, reports that participants maintained an average weight loss of 33 kg for more than 5 years.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5764193

### 6.2 Key Behavioral Strategies

A comprehensive review in *PMC* (2018) identified the following evidence-based strategies for weight loss maintenance:

| Strategy | Evidence Strength |
|---|---|
| **Frequent self-monitoring and self-weighing** | Strong |
| **High levels of physical activity** (200–300 min/week) | Strong |
| **Consistent meal patterns** (including breakfast) | Moderate |
| **Reduced calorie intake** with continued attention to portion control | Strong |
| **More frequent at-home meals** vs. restaurant/fast food | Moderate |
| **Reduced screen time** | Moderate |
| **Use of portion-controlled meals or meal substitutes** | Moderate |
| **Ongoing clinical or group support** (≥1 year) | Strong |

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5764193

### 6.3 The Role of Resistance Training in Maintenance

A 2026 review in *PMC* on resistance training as a key strategy for high-quality weight loss concluded that **RT enhances weight loss quality by maximizing fat mass reduction while preserving or increasing FFM**. Incorporating RT into weight loss programs may improve long-term weight maintenance and mitigate FFM loss. The preservation of lean mass through RT helps maintain resting metabolic rate, counteracting the metabolic adaptation that typically drives weight regain.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12851882

The **2026 ACSM Position Stand** emphasizes that the most meaningful gains come from regular participation in any form of resistance training, and that "consistency beats complexity" for long-term adherence.

**Source:** https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026

### 6.4 Dietary Strategies for Maintenance

A 2026 review in *News-Medical* on lifestyle interventions for long-term weight maintenance identified:

- **High-protein, high-fiber dietary patterns** as among the strongest evidence-based approaches
- **Protein intake of 25–30 g per meal** promotes satiety and reduces hunger through hormonal modulation (GLP-1, CCK)
- Diets providing **20–25% of daily energy from protein** lead to an additional 1.6 kg of weight loss while preserving fat-free mass
- **Structured exercise** can lead to an additional 1.5–3.5 kg of weight loss maintenance

**Source:** https://www.news-medical.net/health/Lifestyle-interventions-to-support-long-term-weight-maintenance.aspx

### 6.5 Behavioral and Psychological Strategies

The comprehensive review in *PMC* (2018) detailed several evidence-based counseling strategies:

1. **Relapse prevention training:** Anticipating high-risk situations, self-weighing, identifying weight thresholds signaling need for re-engagement
2. **Cognitive restructuring:** Addressing maladaptive thought patterns and coping strategies
3. **Developing cognitive flexibility:** Moving from rigid "all-or-none" rules to flexible, sustainable habits
4. **Appeal to deeper motivations:** Finding enjoyable physical activities and connecting to intrinsic values
5. **Managing expectations:** Realistic goal-setting (5–10% weight loss is clinically meaningful)
6. **Escalating treatment as needed:** Referral to dietitians, obesity medicine specialists, or behavioral psychologists

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5764193

### 6.6 Clinical Guidelines for Maintenance

The **2013 AHA/ACC/TOS Guideline for the Management of Overweight and Obesity in Adults** (still referenced in the 2018 review) codified that weight loss interventions should include **long-term comprehensive weight loss maintenance programs that continue for at least 1 year**. This recommendation has been reinforced by subsequent evidence.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5764193

## 7. Integrated Practical Recommendations

### 7.1 For Adiposity Reduction with Lean Mass Preservation

1. **Caloric deficit:** 250–500 kcal/day (avoid >500 kcal/day deficit to preserve lean mass)
2. **Protein intake:** 2.3–3.1 g/kg/day (or 2.0–2.4 g/kg/day as a more conservative target); distribute evenly across 3–4 meals at ~0.4 g/kg/meal
3. **Resistance training:** ≥2 sessions/week, ≥10 sets per muscle group per week; progressive overload; periodization is optional
4. **Aerobic training:** HIIT (≥75 min/week) or MICT (≥150 min/week); HIIT offers modest fat loss advantages with time efficiency
5. **Minimum energy availability:** >30 kcal/kg FFM/day

### 7.2 For Long-Term Maintenance

1. **Continue resistance training** ≥2×/week to preserve lean mass and metabolic rate
2. **Maintain higher protein intake** (1.6–2.4 g/kg/day)
3. **Engage in regular physical activity** (200–300 min/week of moderate activity)
4. **Self-monitor** weight, food intake, and activity regularly
5. **Eat consistent meals** with high protein and fiber content
6. **Seek ongoing support** (clinical, group, or community-based)
7. **Develop cognitive flexibility** and realistic expectations

## 8. Research Gaps and Future Directions

1. **Sex-specific responses:** The 2026 ACSM Position Stand and the protein consensus literature both note that far less work has been done in women than men. Emerging evidence suggests sex differences in protein metabolism and training adaptations that warrant further investigation.
2. **Optimal protein distribution during deficit:** While even distribution enhances MPS, the precise interaction with caloric deficit magnitude and training volume requires further study.
3. **HIIT + RT combinations:** The optimal integration of HIIT and RT for simultaneous fat loss and lean mass gain in recreationally active adults is not fully characterized.
4. **Long-term adherence:** Most evidence comes from short-term (8–16 week) interventions; strategies for sustaining body composition improvements beyond 1–2 years are understudied.
5. **Individual variability:** Genetic, epigenetic, and microbiome factors that determine individual responses to these interventions are not well understood.
