

# Chronic Gastrointestinal Distress in Masters (40+) Endurance Runners: A Comprehensive Evidence Review

## 1. Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) distress during or after running—often called "runner's gut" or "runner's stomach"—is one of the most common performance-limiting problems in endurance sports. Depending on the methodology and population studied, **30%–90% of distance runners** report exercise-related GI symptoms, and up to **96% of ultramarathon participants** experience some form of GI complaint during competition (GSSI, 2024; de Oliveira et al., 2014). For masters athletes (40+ years old), the problem is compounded by age-related changes in gut function, slower adaptation to fueling protocols, and reduced thermoregulatory capacity (NEVERSECOND, 2026; Outside Run, 2026).

This report synthesizes peer-reviewed studies, case reports, clinical guidelines, and expert consensus to address the most common causes of chronic GI distress in masters runners (10K to ultramarathon distances), how similar cases have been described in the sports-medicine literature, which medical and behavioral treatments work best, and which fueling, hydration, and training adjustments most reliably prevent recurrence.

---

## 2. Prevalence and Scope of the Problem

- **General runners:** 30–90% of distance runners experience GI problems (GSSI, 2024; de Oliveira et al., 2014).
- **Marathoners:** Up to 71% report cramping or diarrhea; 16% experience bloody diarrhea on at least one occasion (AJMC, 2023; Sanchez et al., 2006).
- **Ultramarathoners:** 96% of 161-km race participants reported GI symptoms; 35.6% attributed failure to finish to these symptoms (Stuempfle et al., 2013).
- **Masters athletes:** A 2020 study of 805 recreational runners (mean age 39.7 ± 10.0) found a 26.1% race-day symptom prevalence, with bloating (18.6%), urge to defecate (17.8%), and stomach pain (16.5%) most common (PMC12325075, 2024). Runners aged 35–54 (approximating the masters group) had *lower* prevalence than younger runners, but the study noted that a history of GI disorders—more common with age—was the strongest predictor.
- **Masters-specific data:** A 2020 dietary-restrictions study found that masters males (≥40 years) reported significantly lower rates of stomach pain/cramps (33% vs. 48% in young males) and side ache/stitch (13% vs. 35%), but similar rates of diarrhea (15% vs. 10%) and urge to defecate (15% vs. 17%) (Parnell et al., 2020, PMC7288429).

## 3. Most Common Causes of Chronic GI Distress in Endurance Runners

### 3.1 Splanchnic Hypoperfusion and Ischemia (The Dominant Mechanism)

The single most important physiological cause is **exercise-induced splanchnic hypoperfusion**. At 70% VO₂max, gut blood flow can drop by nearly 80% as blood is shunted to working muscles and skin (GSSI, 2024; NEVERSECOND, 2026). This ischemia leads to:

- **Intestinal epithelial injury** and increased intestinal permeability ("leaky gut")
- **Endotoxemia** (translocation of luminal endotoxins into the systemic circulation)
- **Local and systemic inflammation**
- **Mucosal erosions and ischemic colitis** in severe cases

> **Key evidence:** Otte et al. (2001) demonstrated gastric ischemia in healthy volunteers during exercise using tonometry (J Appl Physiol, 2001). Kolkman et al. (1999) showed increased gastric PCO₂ during exercise as a marker of ischemia (Gut, 1999). The "exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome" (Ex-GIS) model formalizes this as a cascade beginning with hypoperfusion, progressing to epithelial injury, impaired barrier function, and systemic inflammation (AJMC, 2023; MDPI, 2024).

### 3.2 Mechanical Jostling (The "Running" Factor)

Running produces **repetitive mechanical trauma** to the GI tract. The bouncing motion causes:

- Gastric sloshing leading to nausea and bloating
- Intestinal wall microtrauma, particularly in the colon
- Higher incidence of lower-GI symptoms (diarrhea, urgency) compared with cycling (GSSI, 2024; Houston Methodist, 2021)

This mechanical stress is thought to explain why **runners have more GI symptoms than cyclists** (GSSI, 2024).

### 3.3 Nutritional Triggers

Specific foods and feeding patterns are well-established triggers:

- **High-fat, high-fiber, and high-protein foods** delay gastric emptying and increase symptom risk (de Oliveira et al., 2014; PMC12325075, 2024)
- **Concentrated carbohydrate solutions** (osmolality >500 mOsm/L) shift fluid into the intestinal lumen, causing cramping and diarrhea (GSSI, 2024)
- **Fermentable carbohydrates (FODMAPs)** increase gas production and bloating (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022)
- **Eating within 30 minutes before a race** significantly increases bloating, urge to defecate, and flatulence (PMC12325075, 2024)
- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, aspirin)** increase intestinal permeability and may exacerbate GI symptoms and bleeding (GSSI, 2024; MySportsScience, 2024)

### 3.4 Dehydration and Heat Stress

Dehydration compounds every other mechanism:

- **Plasma volume losses of 10–15%** worsen splanchnic hypoperfusion (AJMC, 2023)
- **Heat stress** shifts blood to the skin for cooling, further reducing gut blood flow
- **4% body weight loss from sweat** increases risk of bowel movements during running (Without Limits, 2024)
- **Masters athletes** have reduced cardiovascular cooling capacity and decreased sweating efficiency, making them more vulnerable to heat-induced GI distress (NEVERSECOND, 2026)

### 3.5 Age-Related Changes in Masters Athletes

While the literature on age-specific mechanisms is limited, several factors are relevant:

- **Slower gastric emptying** with aging may increase risk of upper-GI symptoms
- **Reduced gut adaptability** – masters athletes may need longer to "train the gut" due to lower baseline carbohydrate intake and slower upregulation of glucose transporters (SGLT1, GLUT5) (Outside Run, 2026; NEVERSECOND, 2026)
- **Higher prevalence of underlying GI conditions** (e.g., diverticulosis, IBS, gastritis, celiac disease) that may be unmasked or exacerbated by exercise (Henry Ford Health, 2025)
- **Polypharmacy** – medications for hypertension, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia may affect GI function
- **Reduced thermoregulatory efficiency** – masters athletes have lower sweat rates and skin blood flow, increasing core temperature and gut ischemia risk (NEVERSECOND, 2026)

### 3.6 Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO) – A Ruled-Out Cause

A 2011 study specifically tested whether SIBO causes GI symptoms in runners. Seven long-distance runners (mean age 29.1 years) with GI complaints underwent glucose hydrogen breath testing. **No evidence of SIBO** was found in any subject. The authors concluded that the daily duration of reduced mesenteric blood flow and mechanical GI concussion in runners is insufficient to cause SIBO (PMC3155897, 2011). However, one runner had lactose intolerance, suggesting this alternative diagnosis is worth considering.

## 4. Case Descriptions in the Sports-Medicine Literature

### 4.1 Exercise-Induced Ischemic Colitis ("Runner's Colitis")

**Most common severe GI pathology reported in runners.** It is a rare but serious consequence of long-distance running.

**Case 1 (1998):** A 42-year-old elite marathoner presented with severe abdominal pain and hematochezia after a marathon. Colonoscopy showed ischemic colitis. Extensive workup (including magnetic resonance angiography) revealed no vascular occlusion; the etiology was deemed **non-occlusive ischemia from splanchnic vasoconstriction, intravascular volume depletion, and possibly hypercoagulability** (Lucas & Schroy, 1998, Am J Gastroenterol; ScienceDirect, 1998).

**Case 2 (2017):** A 41-year-old longtime runner presented with cramping lower abdominal pain and hematochezia after increasing her run distance. Colonoscopy confirmed ischemic colitis. She was previously misdiagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (Faress et al., 2017, PMC5675973).

**Case 3 (2006):** A series of marathon runners with ischemic colitis were reviewed by Sanchez et al., emphasizing that the condition is often misdiagnosed as infectious colitis or IBD (J Emerg Med, 2006).

**Key point:** Ischemic colitis typically presents with **acute cramping abdominal pain, diarrhea, and hematochezia** during or immediately after intense running. Most cases resolve with conservative management (hydration, bowel rest), but severe cases can require surgery (AJMC, 2023).

### 4.2 Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia Exacerbated by Exercise

A 2015 case report described a patient with a high-grade superior mesenteric artery (SMA) stenosis that was asymptomatic at rest but caused severe **exercise-induced abdominal pain** during running. The ischemia was unmasked by the increased oxygen demand of the gut during digestion combined with exercise-induced splanchnic vasoconstriction. This case highlights that **underlying vascular stenoses** can cause chronic, reproducible exercise-related pain (Annals of Vascular Surgery, 2015).

### 4.3 Gastrointestinal Bleeding in Marathon Runners

- Up to **12% of ultramarathon participants** experience occult GI bleeding (AJMC, 2023)
- **16% of runners** report bloody diarrhea on at least one occasion (Sanchez et al., 2006)
- A 2024 case report detailed a 41-year-old runner with acute melena after a marathon; workup was negative, and the bleeding was attributed to exercise-induced ischemic injury of the small bowel (Bin Hameed et al., 2024, Beaumont Scholarly Works)

### 4.4 Lactose Intolerance Mimicking Runner's Gut

In the SIBO study (PMC3155897, 2011), one runner tested positive for lactose intolerance. This runner was asymptomatic at rest but developed GI symptoms during running, suggesting that **running may unmask subclinical food intolerances** by reducing the threshold for symptoms.

### 4.5 Functional GI Disorders (IBS, Functional Dyspepsia)

Many runners have underlying **irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)** or **functional dyspepsia** that is exacerbated by exercise. The Chinese study (PMC12325075, 2024) found that a history of GI disorders (including IBS, gastritis, functional dyspepsia) was the strongest predictor of race-day symptoms. The symptoms of Ex-GIS (bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhea) overlap significantly with IBS (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022).

## 5. Medical and Behavioral Treatments

### 5.1 Dietary Strategies (First-Line, Most Evidence)

| Strategy | Evidence Level | Key Details |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| **Dietary fiber reduction** | Strong (multiple studies) | Most common self-reported strategy (15.2% of athletes). Implement 1–3 days before exercise. Reduces luminal contents and GI transit time. Median success score 4/5 (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022) |
| **Low-FODMAP diet** | Strong (RCT-level for IBS, observational for athletes) | 5.4% of athletes used it; median success 4/5. A 24-hour low-FODMAP diet before exercise reduces Ex-GIS and malabsorption. Should not be used long-term without rechallenge (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022; PMC12258207, 2025) |
| **Dairy/lactose avoidance** | Moderate | 5.8% of athletes used dairy-free diet; median success 4/5. May reflect undiagnosed lactose intolerance (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022) |
| **Increasing carbohydrate intake** | Strong | Improves splanchnic blood flow, reduces intestinal injury. 30–60 g/h for events ≤2h; 60–90 g/h for longer events. Must be paired with gut training (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022; GSSI, 2024) |
| **Gluten-free diet** | Weak (not supported by RCT) | Popular but no evidence of benefit in non-celiac athletes (MDPI, 2024; Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022) |
| **Low-fat, low-protein pre-exercise** | Moderate | Fat and protein delay gastric emptying; should be minimized in the pre-exercise meal (de Oliveira et al., 2014) |

### 5.2 Gut Training (Periodized Carbohydrate Conditioning)

**Gut training** is the deliberate practice of consuming higher carbohydrate doses during training to improve gastric emptying and intestinal absorption capacity. Key evidence:

- **Two weeks of repetitive carbohydrate intake during running** reduces GI symptom severity by **60–63%** (RunnersConnect, 2024; PMC12258207, 2025)
- **Protocol:** Start with 30 g/h of multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose + fructose) and gradually increase to 60–90 g/h over several weeks
- **Masters athletes** may need a longer adaptation window, especially if they have been on a low-carb diet or train infrequently (Outside Run, 2026; NEVERSECOND, 2026)
- Elite athletes now consume **120–150 g/h** successfully, but this requires months of consistent training (NEVERSECOND, 2026)

### 5.3 Hydration and Electrolyte Management

- **Euhydration** before and during exercise reduces Ex-GIS and improves gastrointestinal integrity compared with dehydration (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022)
- **Goal:** Drink 16–20 oz of fluid per hour during exercise; adjust based on sweat rate, heat, and humidity (Houston Methodist, 2021)
- **Dilute carbohydrate drinks** (6–8% carbohydrate concentration) optimize gastric emptying (GSSI, 2024)
- **Avoid hypertonic drinks** (>500 mOsm/L) – they delay gastric emptying and cause fluid shifts into the lumen (GSSI, 2024)
- **Electrolyte replacement** is critical; both too little and too much sodium can cause symptoms (Eat for Endurance, 2024)

### 5.4 Pharmacological Treatments

| Medication | Indication | Evidence | Considerations |
|------------|-----------|----------|----------------|
| **Loperamide (Imodium)** | Diarrhea-predominant symptoms | Anecdotal/small studies | Trade-offs: slower gut transit may impair fueling; may cause bloating. Not recommended for routine prevention (RunnersConnect, 2024; Hellemans, 2024) |
| **Nifedipine (low dose, 2.5–5 mg)** | Refractory runner's diarrhea | Clinical experience | A calcium-channel blocker that reduces colonic motility. Prescription only. Low dose minimizes side effects (Hellemans, 2024) |
| **Anti-nausea medications** (e.g., motion sickness drugs) | Nausea/vomiting | Anecdotal | May cause drowsiness; test in training (Hellemans, 2024) |
| **PPIs / H2 blockers** | GERD/heartburn | Standard GERD guidelines | First-line: lifestyle modifications; second-line: medications. Avoid PPIs long-term without indication (Musculoskeletal Key, 2024) |
| **Peppermint oil / ginger** | Nausea, cramping | Mild supportive evidence | Low risk; may help settle stomach (Hellemans, 2024) |

### 5.5 Behavioral and Mind-Body Strategies

- **Relaxation/meditation:** 4% of athletes used it; rated as moderately successful. Given the overlap between Ex-GIS and IBS, gut-directed hypnotherapy and cognitive behavioral therapy may be effective, though research in athletes is limited (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022)
- **Pre-race anxiety management:** Stress and anxiety contribute to GI symptoms via the gut-brain axis (Patrick B. Wilson study, cited in MDPI, 2024)
- **Pacing adjustments:** Slowing down (76.2% of symptomatic runners) and walking/stopping (41.0%) were the most common relief strategies during races (PMC12325075, 2024)

### 5.6 When to Seek Medical Evaluation

Persistent stomach issues despite optimized nutrition and training warrant evaluation for:

- **IBS, celiac disease, IBD** (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis)
- **Gastritis, peptic ulcer disease**
- **Chronic mesenteric ischemia** (especially in older athletes with vascular risk factors)
- **Diverticulitis** (more common with age)
- **Colon cancer** (age-appropriate screening recommended)

**Red flags:** bloody diarrhea, severe or progressive pain, fever, unintentional weight loss, nocturnal symptoms, family history of colon cancer (Henry Ford Health, 2025; Medical News Today, 2024).

## 6. Prevention Strategies: Fueling, Hydration, and Training Adjustments

### 6.1 Pre-Run/Race Nutrition (24–72 Hours Before)

| Timing | Recommendation | Evidence |
|--------|---------------|----------|
| **48–72 hours pre-race** | Carbohydrate loading: 10–12 g/kg/day of easily digestible carbs. Reduce fiber, fat, and protein | RunnersConnect, 2024 |
| **24–48 hours pre-race** | Low-fiber diet (<10 g/day) to reduce colonic bulk. Avoid high-FODMAP foods | Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022; PMC12258207, 2025 |
| **3–4 hours pre-run** | Low-fat, low-fiber, moderate-protein meal (e.g., white rice, banana, toast) | GSSI, 2024 |
| **30–60 min pre-run** | Avoid solid food. If needed, small amount of easily digestible carbs (e.g., gel, sports drink) | PMC12325075, 2024 |
| **Morning of race** | Avoid eating within 30 minutes of start; this significantly increases bloating, urgency, and flatulence | PMC12325075, 2024 |

### 6.2 During-Run Fueling

- **Carbohydrate intake:** 30–60 g/h for runs 1–2.5 hours; 60–90 g/h for runs >2.5 hours; **multiple transportable carbohydrates** (glucose + fructose) are better tolerated (GSSI, 2024; ACSM guidelines)
- **Hydration with fuel:** Always consume water with gels/chews – concentrated carbs without water increase osmolality and GI distress (Henry Ford Health, 2025)
- **Frequency:** Small, frequent amounts (every 15–20 minutes) rather than large boluses (RunnersConnect, 2024)
- **Avoid:** High-fat foods, high-fiber foods, dairy, caffeine (if sensitive), and NSAIDs during exercise (GSSI, 2024; MySportsScience, 2024)

### 6.3 Hydration Protocol

- **Daily hydration:** 0.5 oz per pound of body weight as baseline (Without Limits, 2024)
- **Pre-run:** 16–20 oz of water or electrolyte drink 2–3 hours before; 8–10 oz 20–30 minutes before
- **During run:** 16–20 oz per hour, adjusted for heat and sweat rate
- **Post-run:** Replace 150% of sweat losses (weigh before and after run)
- **Electrolytes:** Use isotonic or hypotonic sports drinks; avoid hypertonic drinks that delay gastric emptying

### 6.4 Training Adjustments

**Gut Training Protocol (adapted from RunnersConnect, GSSI, and Outside Run):**

1. **Start low:** Begin with 30 g/h of carbohydrate during runs; use a mix of glucose + fructose
2. **Progress gradually:** Increase by 5–10 g per week as tolerated
3. **Consistency:** Practice on 2–3 runs per week minimum; daily is better
4. **Masters adaptation:** Expect a slower timeline. If you've been low-carb, start at 20 g/h and increase over 4–8 weeks (Outside Run, 2026)
5. **Heat adaptation:** Train in heat to improve thermoregulatory efficiency; masters athletes need extra caution (NEVERSECOND, 2026)
6. **Run intensity:** Symptoms are more common at higher intensities. Periodize fueling to match workout intensity
7. **Consider probiotics:** Emerging evidence suggests certain strains (e.g., *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium* species) may improve run-to-fatigue time and reduce GI symptoms, but evidence is not yet conclusive (PMC9734205, 2022)

### 6.5 Specific Adjustments for Masters Athletes

- **Allow more time for gut adaptation** – 6–8 weeks vs. 4 weeks for younger athletes (Outside Run, 2026)
- **Be extra cautious in heat** – masters athletes have reduced thermoregulatory capacity; plan hot-weather runs with more hydration and slower pace (NEVERSECOND, 2026)
- **Ensure adequate daily energy availability** – chronic underfueling (low energy availability) worsens GI function (Eat for Endurance, 2024)
- **Review all medications** with a physician – anticholinergics, calcium channel blockers, metformin, and others can affect GI motility
- **Consider screening for age-related GI conditions** – colonoscopy at age 45 (USPSTF); evaluation for diverticulosis, gastritis, and celiac disease if symptoms persist

## 7. Summary of Evidence-Based Recommendations

| Domain | Strongest Evidence | Moderate Evidence | Low/Insufficient Evidence |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| **Causes** | Splanchnic hypoperfusion/ischemia; mechanical jostling; high-fat/fiber/FODMAP foods; dehydration; NSAIDs | Heat stress; age-related reduced thermoregulation; underlying IBS | SIBO; gluten sensitivity |
| **Treatment** | Dietary fiber reduction; low-FODMAP diet; increasing carbohydrate intake; gut training; euhydration | Dairy/lactose avoidance; relaxation/meditation; peppermint/ginger | Loperamide; nifedipine; probiotics; hypnotherapy |
| **Prevention** | Pre-race low-fiber diet; carbohydrate loading; gut training; 30–60 g/h fueling; avoiding eating <30 min pre-race | Heat-acclimation; periodized carbohydrate intake; electrolyte balance | Probiotic supplementation; gluten-free diet |

---

## 8. Conclusion

Chronic GI distress in masters endurance runners is predominantly caused by **exercise-induced splanchnic hypoperfusion and ischemia**, compounded by the **mechanical trauma of running**, **nutritional triggers**, **dehydration/heat stress**, and **age-related physiological changes**. The sports-medicine literature describes several well-documented cases of exercise-induced ischemic colitis, chronic mesenteric ischemia unmasked by running, and occult GI bleeding, particularly in marathoners and ultramarathoners.

The most effective treatment and prevention strategies are **dietary fiber reduction, low-FODMAP diets, progressive gut training, euhydration, and careful pre-race nutrition timing**. Masters athletes may require a slower, more gradual approach to gut training and should be especially vigilant about heat management and medication interactions. For persistent symptoms, evaluation for underlying GI disorders (IBS, celiac disease, ischemic colitis, mesenteric ischemia) is warranted.
