

# Standardized Testing in K-12 Education: A Comprehensive Research Report for Parents

## Executive Summary

Standardized testing is one of the most debated topics in American education. For parents, the core question is whether these assessments—from state-mandated tests in elementary school to the SAT/ACT in high school—ultimately help or hinder a child's learning and development. This report synthesizes evidence from peer-reviewed research, government datasets, expert analysis, and student/parent perspectives to provide a balanced, evidence-based examination of how standardized testing affects children across four key domains: stress and love of learning, curriculum narrowing and critical thinking, prediction of future success, and alternative approaches.

---

## 1. How Standardized Testing Affects Stress Levels and Love of Learning

### The Physiological Evidence: Cortisol and Stress

The most direct evidence of testing's impact on children comes from biological research. A landmark study published by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) measured cortisol—a primary stress hormone—in the saliva of students in grades 3–8 in New Orleans charter schools. The findings were striking: students' cortisol levels rose by approximately **15% on average** during weeks when high-stakes standardized tests were administered, compared to non-testing weeks. Male students experienced an even sharper increase of **35%** on average. Students from neighborhoods with higher poverty rates and more violent crime showed the largest cortisol spikes. Critically, students whose cortisol levels shifted significantly (more than 10% up or down) performed about **0.4 standard deviations worse** on tests than expected based on their classroom performance—equivalent to roughly 80 points on the SAT scale. This suggests that the stress response itself undermines test performance, creating a vicious cycle for students who are already anxious. (Source: [NBER, Heissel et al., 2019](https://www.nber.org/digest/mar19/test-related-stress-and-student-scores-high-stakes-exams))

### Psychological and Emotional Impact

A 2005 study cited in the Edutopia review of high-stakes testing found that young elementary students experienced "anxiety, panic, irritability, frustration, boredom, crying, headaches, and loss of sleep" during testing periods. Researchers concluded that "high-stakes testing causes damage to children's self-esteem, overall morale, and love of learning." In a 2011 study by Laura-Lee Kearns at St. Francis Xavier University, high school students who failed a state literacy test reported feeling "degraded, humiliated, stressed, and shamed by the test results," even though many were successful students who had thought of themselves as academically advanced. (Source: [Edutopia, "The Psychological Toll of High-Stakes Testing"](https://www.edutopia.org/article/psychological-toll-high-stakes-testing))

A research review from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) confirmed that test anxiety negatively impacts reading comprehension on high-stakes tests, independent of a student's actual reading ability. This means that the test format itself may be systematically underestimating what many students truly know and can do. (Source: [PMC, "Test anxiety and a high-stakes standardized reading comprehension test," 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5487000))

### Impact on Love of Learning and Intrinsic Motivation

Research on Self-Determination Theory (SDT)—which identifies autonomy, mastery, and purpose as the three pillars of intrinsic motivation—shows that high-stakes testing environments can undermine all three. A 2016 Edutopia article by educator Valencia Clay argued that "low standardized test scores are a reflection of being uninspired, not unknowledgeable," and that focusing on test preparation crowds out the deeper engagement that fuels genuine love of learning. (Source: [Edutopia, "Intrinsic Motivation vs. Standardized Tests," 2016](https://www.edutopia.org/blog/intrinsic-motivation-vs-standardized-tests-valencia-clay))

However, the evidence is not one-sided. A 2025 longitudinal study published in *Learning and Individual Differences* (Muth & Lüftenegger, 2025) of 1,855 secondary school students found that while students perceived increased "teaching to the test" from grades 11 to 12, this actually predicted *increases* in some aspects of motivation, including how important and useful students found the material. The researchers caution that this does not necessarily mean teaching to the test is beneficial—it may reflect students' pragmatic recognition that test performance matters for college admissions—but it does challenge the simple narrative that test preparation inevitably destroys motivation. (Source: [ScienceDirect, "Teaching to the test: Unraveling the consequences for student motivation," 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041608025000834))

### Survey Data on Student Well-Being

The Challenge Success 2024 Student Voice Report, analyzing data from over 270,000 high school students across 14 years, found that pressure to succeed—much of it tied to testing and grades—remains a top stressor for American adolescents. The report recommends that schools prioritize both physical and mental health, provide students with relevant and meaningful academic experiences, and nurture a positive, respectful, and caring school culture. (Source: [Challenge Success, "2024 Student Voice Report"](https://www.challengesuccess.org/2024-student-voice-report))

A 2024 survey from the Michigan Student Voice project, which gathered responses from over 70,000 students, found that student engagement and sense of belonging—both critical for intrinsic motivation—decline as students progress through grade levels, a pattern that may be exacerbated by the increasing focus on high-stakes testing in middle and high school. (Source: [MI Student Voice, Spring 2024 Survey Results](https://mistudentvoice-cdn.fxbrt.com/downloads/about_us/survey-results/mi_student_voice_perception_survey_-_final_results_spring_2024.pdf))

## 2. "Teaching to the Test" vs. Real Education and Critical Thinking

### The Evidence on Curriculum Narrowing

A comprehensive synthesis of research by Wayne Au (2007), examining 49 studies on high-stakes testing, found a strong and consistent relationship between high-stakes testing and changes in curriculum and pedagogy. More than **80% of the studies** found that testing led to narrowing of curriculum content and increases in teacher-centered instruction. In other words, the more high-stakes a test is, the more likely teachers are to focus classroom time on tested subjects and tested formats, at the expense of untested subjects like art, music, social studies, and science. (Source: [ASCD, "High-Stakes Testing Narrows the Curriculum," 2007](https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/high-stakes-testing-narrows-the-curriculum))

A Washington State Board of Education report documented that high-poverty schools and schools with higher percentages of minority students are disproportionately affected by curriculum narrowing. The report found that "students from low income households at high poverty schools are delivered a narrowed curriculum" and that "schools with greater percentages of minority students are more likely to provide a narrowed curriculum for teachers to 'teach to the test.'" This means that the children who most need a rich, engaging education are often the ones receiving the most rote, test-focused instruction. (Source: [Washington State Board of Education, "Some of the Impacts of a Narrowed Curriculum," 2018](https://sbe.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-08/Impacts%20of%20a%20Narrowed%20Curriculum_010418.pdf))

### "Curriculum Teaching" vs. "Item Teaching"

Assessment expert W. James Popham draws a critical distinction between "curriculum-teaching"—teaching a broad set of knowledge and skills aligned to standards—and "item-teaching"—drilling students on the specific types of questions that will appear on the test. The latter, which is the common pejorative meaning of "teaching to the test," provides students with a narrow scope of information, often overlooking important concepts, context, and deeper understanding. (Source: [MasteryPrep, "Teaching to the Test Is Dead," 2023](https://www.masteryprep.com/blog/teaching-to-the-test-is-dead))

### The Authentic Instruction Alternative

A study by Newmann, Bryk, and Nagaoka (cited on ReadingRockets.org) analyzed the effects of "authentic intellectual instruction"—teaching that emphasizes deep knowledge, higher-order thinking, and substantive conversation rather than test-prep drills. The results were striking: in classrooms where teachers employed more authentic instruction, students logged test-score gains on the Iowa Test of Basic Skills that **exceeded the national average by 20%**. This finding suggests that there is no inherent trade-off between teaching for deep understanding and performing well on standardized tests. (Source: [Reading Rockets, "Teach to the Test? Just Say No"](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/teach-test-just-say-no))

### Critical Thinking and Standardized Tests

Research on the relationship between standardized tests and critical thinking is complex. The Insight Assessment group notes that "standardized critical thinking tests are commonly used to identify prospective students who can and will complete rigorous educational programs," and that "more than three decades of educational research shows the strength of this relationship" between critical thinking skills and academic success. However, the *type* of standardized test matters enormously. Tests that reward only memorization and speed do not measure critical thinking; tests designed to assess reasoning and problem-solving skills can. The challenge is that most state accountability tests fall into the former category, not the latter. (Source: [Insight Assessment, "Standardized Critical Thinking Tests"](https://insightassessment.com/standardized-critical-thinking-tests-powerful-predictors-in-high-stakes-situations))

## 3. How Test Scores Relate to Future Success in College and Career

### The Predictive Validity Debate: What the Research Says

The relationship between standardized test scores and future college success is one of the most studied—and most contested—questions in educational research. The evidence points in nuanced directions.

**The case for tests as predictors:** A major 2025 study from Opportunity Insights (Friedman, Sacerdote, Staiger, & Tine) analyzed data from Ivy-Plus colleges (Ivy League plus Chicago, Duke, MIT, and Stanford) and found that SAT/ACT scores have substantial predictive power for first-year college GPA. Students with the highest possible scores (1600 SAT/36 ACT) earned a first-year GPA **0.43 points higher** on a 4.0 scale than students with scores around 1200/25, and were **five times less likely** to receive a grade of C+ or lower. The study also found that high school GPA had relatively little predictive power in this context—standardized test scores were **four times more predictive** of first-year academic achievement than high school grades. The College Board's own research, across 171 institutions and over 220,000 students, found that SAT scores add **15% more predictive power** than high school GPA alone for first-year college performance. (Source: [Opportunity Insights, 2025](https://opportunityinsights.org/paper/test-scores); [College Board, "Predictive Validity of the SAT"](https://international.collegeboard.org/toolkit/sat-policy/understanding-sat/predictive-validity))

**The case against over-reliance on tests:** A major study from the University of Chicago Consortium on School Research (Allensworth & Clark, 2020) examined 55,084 Chicago Public Schools students who enrolled in four-year colleges. The researchers found that **high school GPA was five times stronger than ACT scores** at predicting college graduation. Moreover, the predictive power of GPA was consistent across all high schools, while the relationship between ACT scores and graduation varied dramatically depending on which high school a student attended. At many high schools, there was **no connection at all** between ACT scores and eventual college graduation. The authors concluded that GPA captures not just academic knowledge but also critical non-cognitive skills like motivation, persistence, and time management—qualities that standardized tests do not measure but that are essential for long-term success. (Source: [AERA, "High School GPAs and ACT Scores as Predictors of College Graduation," 2020](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Research-Finds-that-High-School-GPAs-Are-Stronger-Predictors-of-College-Graduation-than-ACT-Scores))

### Reconciling the Evidence

The apparent contradiction between these findings can be partially resolved by considering the context. At highly selective colleges, where grade inflation is common and most applicants have high GPAs, test scores may provide additional differentiation. At less selective institutions, GPA may be a stronger predictor because it captures a broader range of student characteristics. Both measures have value, and using them together provides the most complete picture—but neither is perfect.

### College Admissions in the Test-Optional Era

The COVID-19 pandemic led hundreds of colleges to adopt test-optional admissions policies, and the evidence on their effects is mixed. Research from the National Association for College Admission Counseling (NACAC) found that test-optional policies **did not uniformly increase racial or socioeconomic diversity**. A study of 180 selective liberal arts colleges found that adopting test-optional policies increased the number of applications (thereby decreasing acceptance rates) but did not increase diversity. A University of California, Davis study (2025) confirmed that "test-optional admissions policies are often adopted with the assumption that they will broaden access to underrepresented minority groups," but the effectiveness depends on "existing admissions values and institutional priorities." (Source: [NACAC, "Defining Access: How Test-Optional Works"](https://www.nacacnet.org/defining-access-how-test-optional-works); [Davis Vanguard, "Study Finds Test-Optional Policies Can Boost Diversity," 2025](https://davisvanguard.org/2025/08/campus-diversity-admissions-policies))

An NBER study (Sacerdote, Staiger, & Tine, 2025) examining Dartmouth College's transition to test-optional found that the policy **disproportionately harmed high-achieving applicants from disadvantaged backgrounds**, because these students were less likely to submit their scores even when they had strong ones. The researchers also found that test scores predict academic success similarly regardless of student background, while other application components like guidance counselor recommendations tend to favor higher-income students. (Source: [NBER, "How Test-Optional Policies Disproportionately Harm High-Achieving Applicants from Disadvantaged Backgrounds," 2025](https://www.nber.org/digest/202504/test-optional-policies-and-disadvantaged-students))

### What About Career Outcomes?

The relationship between K-12 test scores and long-term career success is even more tenuous. Research consistently shows that while test scores correlate with educational attainment, which in turn correlates with earnings, the direct predictive power of test scores for career outcomes—especially when controlling for other factors like socioeconomic background, personality traits, and network effects—is modest. The World Economic Forum and other organizations increasingly emphasize that "durable skills" like critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, and communication—which are poorly measured by most standardized tests—are the strongest predictors of career success in the 21st-century economy.

## 4. Alternatives to Standardized Testing: What Works?

### Performance-Based Assessment (PBA)

Performance-based assessment asks students to demonstrate their knowledge and skills through authentic tasks—writing essays, conducting scientific investigations, building models, creating portfolios, or delivering presentations. The Institute of Education Sciences (IES) reports that research shows performance assessments help teachers develop instructional strategies that strengthen students' problem-solving and critical thinking skills, and increase student engagement as well as academic and interpersonal skill development. (Source: [IES, "Performance Assessment: An Engaging Alternative," 2023](https://ies.ed.gov/learn/blog/performance-assessment-engaging-alternative-traditional-test-taking))

The National Education Association (NEA) strongly advocates for PBA, arguing that it "allows students to demonstrate knowledge and skill through critical-thinking, problem-solving, collaboration, and the application of knowledge to real-world situations" and "helps students prepare for college, career, and life." Performance assessment can range from short constructed responses to multi-week research projects and portfolios. (Source: [NEA, "Standardized Testing is Still Failing Students," 2025](https://www.nea.org/nea-today/all-news-articles/standardized-testing-still-failing-students))

### Case Study: The New York Performance Standards Consortium

The New York Performance Standards Consortium is a network of 38 public high schools serving over 12,000 students. These schools have a waiver that allows them to graduate students using performance-based assessments (called "PBATs"—Performance-Based Assessment Tasks) in place of most Regents exams. PBATs require students to complete extended written assignments, conduct original research, and defend their work orally before a panel of judges. Consortium schools serve a diverse student population—including many low-income students and students of color—and research shows that they produce graduates who are well-prepared for college. Linda Darling-Hammond, a leading education scholar, has called the Consortium "some of the most sophisticated performance assessments for graduating high school students in the country." (Source: [New York Performance Standards Consortium](http://www.performanceassessment.org))

### Case Study: New Hampshire's PACE Program

New Hampshire's Performance Assessment of Competency Education (PACE) is a first-in-the-nation accountability system that replaces most standardized tests with locally developed performance assessments. In PACE districts, student proficiency is determined through a combination of teacher judgments, classroom-based assessments, and common performance tasks administered across participating schools. Statewide standardized tests are only given in grades 3 (ELA), 4 (math), 8 (ELA and math), and 11 (ELA, math, and science)—primarily for auditing purposes. Research on PACE has found "small positive effects" on student achievement in all examined grades and subjects, with "lower achieving students" tending to show the greatest benefits. (Source: [NH Department of Education, PACE Program](https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-learner-support/bureau-of-instructional-support/performance-assessment-competency-education); [Center for Assessment, "NH PACE Program Student Achievement"](https://www.nciea.org/blog/keeping-up-the-pace))

### Case Study: Massachusetts Consortium for Innovative Education Assessment (MCIEA)

MCIEA is a partnership between eight Massachusetts school districts and their local teachers unions, working to create a fair and effective accountability system. The consortium uses teacher-created, classroom-embedded performance assessments rather than externally created standardized tests. It measures school quality through multiple indicators: student engagement, sense of belonging, performing arts, civic competencies, and school climate—not just test scores. As MCIEA co-founder Jack Schneider explains, "The current data used to measure student success closely correlates to race, income, and family educational attainment. It really only tells us about advantage and disadvantage outside school, not what schools are actually doing." (Source: [NEA, "Standardized Testing is Still Failing Students," 2025](https://www.nea.org/nea-today/all-news-articles/standardized-testing-still-failing-students))

### The Mastery Transcript Consortium (MTC)

The Mastery Transcript Consortium (now Mastery.org) is a network of nearly 400 private and public schools that have moved beyond traditional letter grades and GPAs to a "mastery transcript" that documents what students know and can do across multiple competencies. The transcript reflects students' work, projects, and experiences, and is accepted by 823 colleges and universities, including all eight Ivy League schools. Nearly 3,000 students have been admitted to college using a Mastery Transcript. This approach shifts the focus from test scores to demonstrated competencies, allowing students to be "seen for who they really are, not distilled down to a GPA." (Source: [Mastery.org](https://www.mastery.org))

### Other Promising Approaches

- **Through-year assessment models:** Several states are piloting "through-year" assessments that administer multiple shorter tests throughout the school year and aggregate results into a single summative score, reducing the pressure of a single high-stakes testing day and providing more timely feedback for instruction. (Source: [Center for American Progress, "3 Trends in K-12 Assessments," 2023](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/fact-sheet-3-trends-in-k-12-assessments-across-the-country))

- **Sampling:** Rather than testing every student every year, testing a statistically representative sample of students can provide system-level data without burdening all students. This is the approach used by the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), which has provided reliable national data on student achievement for decades without the high-stakes consequences that distort instruction.

- **Multiple measures:** Anya Kamenetz, author of *The Test*, recommends using multiple measures including social and emotional skills surveys, game-based assessments, portfolio evaluations, and performance tasks—rather than relying on a single test score to evaluate students, teachers, or schools. (Source: [Otus, "Measuring Student Progress Without Standardized Tests"](https://otus.com/resources/blog/measure-student-progress-without-standardized-testing))

- **Project-Based Learning (PBL) assessment:** Schools using PBL assess student learning through authentic products, presentations, and performances rather than traditional tests. Research from the Buck Institute for Education (PBLWorks) shows that PBL assessment can effectively measure both content knowledge and success skills like critical thinking, collaboration, and communication. (Source: [PBLWorks](https://www.pblworks.org/PBL-curriculum-and-assessment))

## Summary and Recommendations for Parents

The evidence on standardized testing presents a complex picture. There is no simple yes-or-no answer to whether testing helps or hurts children—the effects depend heavily on the *type* of test, the *stakes* attached to it, the *age* of the child, and the *implementation* in the classroom.

**What the research clearly shows:**

1. **High-stakes testing causes measurable physiological stress** in children, particularly in those from disadvantaged backgrounds, and this stress can undermine test performance and a love of learning.

2. **Teaching to the test is real and harmful** in its most extreme forms, narrowing the curriculum and reducing opportunities for deep learning, especially in the schools that serve the most vulnerable students. However, authentic instruction that teaches for deep understanding can *improve* test scores while also developing critical thinking.

3. **Standardized tests have real but limited predictive value** for college success. They are better predictors of first-year college grades (especially at selective institutions) than of college graduation or long-term career success. High school GPA captures important non-cognitive skills that tests miss.

4. **Test-optional admissions policies are not a panacea** for equity—they can benefit some students but may also harm high-achieving, disadvantaged students. The most effective approach to college admissions likely uses multiple measures in combination.

5. **Proven alternatives exist** and are working in schools and districts across the country. Performance-based assessment, portfolio systems, through-year assessments, and competency-based transcripts offer more holistic, equitable, and instructionally useful ways to measure student learning.

**What parents can do:**

- **Advocate for balance** in your child's school. Ask about the ratio of test preparation time to authentic learning time. Support teachers who integrate performance assessments alongside required tests.

- **Learn about your state's assessment system** and whether it allows for innovations like through-year assessments or performance-based alternatives. Many states have flexibility under the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) that they are not fully utilizing.

- **Consider the test-optional landscape** when researching colleges, but also recognize that strong test scores can still be an asset, especially for students from less advantaged backgrounds.

- **Monitor your child's stress levels** around testing. If your child experiences significant anxiety, consider talking to the school about accommodations, relaxation techniques, or—in extreme cases—opting out of state tests if your state allows it.

- **Support your child's love of learning** by emphasizing growth, effort, and curiosity over test scores. Research consistently shows that intrinsic motivation is the strongest predictor of long-term success and well-being.
