
# GDPR Privacy Risk Assessment Report: Enforcement Analysis 2023–2026

## Executive Summary

Since the GDPR took effect in May 2018, cumulative fines have exceeded **€7.1 billion** across more than 2,500 enforcement actions, with **€1.2 billion issued in 2025 alone** — matching the record set in 2024. Over **60% of all fine value** has been imposed since January 2023, confirming that enforcement has entered a sustained high-volume, high-value phase. The average fine stands at approximately **€2.4 million**, and more than 3,200 cases are now tracked across all EU/EEA member states. Ireland accounts for **€4.04 billion** (57% of the global total), but enforcement is widening: Spain has issued over 1,000 fines, and Italy, Romania, Germany, France, and the Netherlands are all imposing substantial penalties. This report identifies the ten most frequent violation types, their typical penalty ranges, and the specific compliance gaps enterprises must address based on enforcement actions exceeding €10 million from a broad cross-section of member states.

The CMS GDPR Enforcement Tracker, the DLA Piper GDPR Fines and Data Breach Survey, and the Uniconsent enforcement database consistently group GDPR violations into nine formal categories. ## Section 2: Standard Penalty Range Typically Imposed for Each Violation Type

Under **Article 83 GDPR**, penalties operate on a two-tier system. Regulators apply a contextual assessment using ten crtieria (Art. 83(2)), including the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement; intentional or negligent character; categories of personal data affected; degree of responsibility; previous infringements; and cooperation with authorities.

### Upper Tier — Up to €20 Million or 4% of Global Annual Turnover

| Violation Type | Tolling Art. 83(5) | Typical Penalty Range | Example Cases |
|---------------|----------------------|----------------------|---------------|
| Insifficient legal basis for processing | Art. 6, 7 | €100,000 – €1.2 billion | Meta (€1.2B, IE); TikTok (€530M, IE); LinkedIn (€310M, IE); Google (€325M, FR) |
| Non-compliance with general processing principles | Art. 5 | €50,000 – €405 million | Meta/Instagran (€405M, IE); Uber (€290M, NL); Vodafone (€45M, DE) |
| Insufficient technical and organizational security | Art. 32 | €50,000 – €265 million | Meta (€265M, IE); Meta (€251M, IE); AWS-related enforcement|
| Insufficient data subject rights | Art. 12–22 | €10,000 – €100 million | Various national DPA enforcement; multiple TikTok children's data cases|
| Insufficient information obligations (transparency) | Art. 13, 14 | €10,000 – €225 million | WhatApp (€225M, IE); various cookie-wall enforcement|

### Lower Tier — Up to €10 Million or 2% of Global Annual Turnover

| Violation Type | Triggering Art. 83(4) | Typical Penalty Range | Examples |
|----------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|----------|
| Insufficient cooperation with supervisory authority | Art. 31 | €5,000 – €5 million | Rare; failures to respond to DPA investigations|
| Insufficient data breach notification | Art. 33, 34 | €5,000 – €20 million | Late notifications; failure to document breaches|
| Insufficient data processing agreement | Art. 28 | €1,000 – €2 million | Missing processor contracts; inadequate sub-processor controls|
| Lack of appointment of DPO | Art. 37–39 | €1,000 – €10 million | Small to medium enterprise cases; public sector enforcement|

**Key Insight:** While the upper tier carries the highest theoretical maximum, most actual fines fall well below it. The average fine across all categories is approximately **€2.4 million**, held down by a long tail of small administrative penalties. However, when major technology companies are involved with systematic failures affecting millions of users, fines reach hundreds of millions or billions of euros. The **€530 million TikTok fine** (2025), the **€310 million LinkedIn fine** (2024), and the **€1.2 billion Meta fine** (2023) all involved deliberate or negligent violations of fundamental data processing principles affecting large user bases.

**Sources:** Art. 83 GDPR text (gdpr-info.eu); Uniconsent.com GDPR Enforcement and Fines 2026; CMS Enforcement Tracker Report 2026.

## Section 3: Specifc Compliance Gaps Enterprises Should Avoid

### Gap 1: Using an Invalid Legal Basis for Behavioural Advertising and Profiling
**The largest fines consistently stem from this single gap.** Meta was fined €1.2 billion for unlawful EU-US data transfers under Art. 46, €390 million for relying on the "contractual necessity" legal basis for personalised ads (which the DPC and EDPB rejected), and €310 million for LinkedIn's similar invalid legitimate interest claims for behavioural advertising. TikTok received a €530 million fine in 2025 for unlawful EU-China data transfers. Google was fined €325 million by the French CNIL in 2025 for serving personalised ads in Gmail without valid consent.

**Remediation:** Audit all processing activities to ensure a documented, valid legal basis exists. For non-essential cookies and behavioural advertising, obtain **freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent** (Art. 7) with proof of opt-in. Do not bundle consent for unrelated processing purposes.

### Gap 2: Dark Patterns and Cookie Banner Non-Compliance
Regulators across multiple member states — France, Italy, Spain, Germany, and the Netherlands — have fined companies for making it as easy to **refuse** cookies as to accept them. The French CNIL fined Google LLC and Google Ireland a combined €150 million for this violation. *noyb*-style coordinated complaints have produced hundreds of decisions across the EU.

**Remediation:** Design cookie banners with symmetrical choice architecture — a "Reject All" button must be as prominent and easy to click as "Accept All." Do not use pre-ticked boxes or layered consent walls. Document consent receipts.

### Gap 3: Weak Technical and Organisational Security Measures
Meta was fined €265 million (Nov 2022) and €251 million (Dec 2024) for insufficient security measures that led to widespread data scraping and data exposure. The €251 million fine specifically cited "insufficient technical and organisational measures to ensure information security." Amazon France Logistique was fined €32 million by the CNIL for disproportionate employee surveillance through scanner tracking.

**Remediation:** Implement and document **data protection by design and default** (Art. 25). Enforce multi-factor authentication, role-based access controls, encryption at rest and in transit, regular vulnerability assessments, and a formal incident response plan. Ensure any employee monitoring is proportionate, transparent, and justified by a legitimate purpose with a documented DpIA.

### Gap 4: Incomplete or Inaccurate Records of Processing Activities (RoPA)
A recurring finding in DPA audits is that enterprises lack accurate, up-to-date inventories of all personal data processing activities. This makes it impossible to fulfil DSARs, identify lawful bases, or respond to breaches within 72 hours. Spain's AEPD routinely fines public and private entities for incomplete RoPAs.

**Remediation:** Maintain a living, automated Register of Processing Activities covering all processing operations. Map data flows from collection through storage, processing, sharing, and deletion. Update within one month of any new processing activity.

### Gap 5: Delayed or Restricted Responses to Data Subject Access Requests (DSARs)
The EDPB's Coordinated Enforcement Framework (CEF) made the right of access (Art. 15) its 2024 focus, and enforcement is intensifying. Common failures include exceeding the one-month response deadline, requesting excessive identity verification (passport copies, notarised forms), and claiming disproportionate effort without justification.

**Remediation:** Implement a DSAR automation tool with searchable data mapping. Respond within one month (extendable by two months only with clear justification). Limit identity verification to what is strictly necessary and proportionate.

### Gap 6: Unlawful International Data Transfers
The Meta €1.2 billion fine and the TikTok €530 million fine both centred on unlawful transfers of EU user data to the US and China, respectively. Uber was fined €290 million by the Dutch AP in 2024 for unlawful EU-US data transfers of driver data. The invalidation of the Privacy Shield (Schrems II, 2020) and continuing controversy over the Data Privacy Framework make this a high-risk area.

**Remediation:** Map all cross-border data flows. Implement **Standard Contractual Clauses (SSCs)** supplemented with a **Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA)** and, where required, supplementary measures (encryption, pseudonymisation). Monitor the Data Privacy Framework certification closely.

### Gap 7: Inadequate Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)
Failing to conduct a DPIA before introducing high-risk processing — such as large-scale monitoring, biometrics, profiling, AI/ML processing, or children's data — is a common finding in enforcement actions. The Italian DPA fined Enel Energia €79.1 million for processing customer data acquired through illicit lists without adequate risk assessment or security controls.

**Remediation:** Integrate DPIAs into the project lifecycle for any new technology or processing activity likely to result in high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Maintain a register of all DPIAs and revisit them at least annually or upon a material change.

### Gap 8: Children's Data Protection Failures
TikTok was fined €345 million in 2023 and €530 million in 2025 in part for failing to adequately protect children's data — including allowing children's accounts to be public by default and failing to verify parental consent. Meta (Instagram) was fined €405 million in 2022 for mishandling children's data and exposing children's contact information.

**Remediation:** Obtain **verifiable parental consent** before processing data of children under the age of digital consent (13–16, depending on member state). Enable maximum privacy settings by default for minors. Do not use children's data for behavioural advertising.

### Gap 9: Failure to Appoint a Data Protection Officer When Required
Though fewer fines are issued for this violation (Art. 37–39), some notable actions have targeted public authorities and enterprises that process large-scale sensitive data. The requirement applies to all public authorities, organisations engaged in large-scale systematic monitoring, and those processing special category data on a large scale.

**Remediation:** Assess whether your organisation is required to appoint a DPO under Art. 37. If so, ensure the DPO is designated in writing, registered with the relevant DPA, and involved **early and properly** in all data protection matters.

### Gap 10: Ignoring Vendor and Processor Compliance
Several enforcement actions have highlighted the controller's liability for processor non-compliance. Insufficient data processing agreements (Art. 28), failure to audit sub-processors, and lack of contractual requirements for breach notification have all been cited in fines.

**Remediation:** Review and update all Data Processing Agreements with current Art. 28 requirements. Require third-party vendor risk assessments, audit processor compliance annually, and include contractual clauses governing sub-processors, data deletion, and breach notification.

**Sources:** Scrut.io GDPR Fines and Penalties Guide 2025–2026; Secure Privacy Blog "Common Data Protection Gaps"; Compunnel Top 10 GDPR Compliance Mistakes; Sprinto GDPR Violations and Lessons Learned.
