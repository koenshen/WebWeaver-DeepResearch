

# Ultra-Processed Foods and Modern Health: A Comprehensive Research Report

## Executive Summary

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) now constitute a dominant share of the global food supply—approximately 70% of items in grocery stores in the United States and nearly 60% of total dietary energy consumed in the U.S. and other high-income countries. A rapidly growing body of evidence, including multiple large-scale systematic reviews and umbrella meta-analyses published in 2024–2026, links high UPF consumption to at least 32 distinct adverse health outcomes, ranging from cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes to depression, anxiety, and all-cause mortality. This report synthesizes the international research literature across four dimensions: (1) the biological mechanisms by which processing technologies and additives affect human physiology, (2) the role of food industry marketing and product design in shaping eating behavior, (3) individual genetic and neurobiological susceptibility to food addiction, and (4) the structural environmental factors—including food deserts and economic inequality—that constrain individual choice. The report concludes with a review of emerging public health policy approaches, with particular attention to front-of-pack warning labels, taxation, marketing restrictions, and school-based interventions, and offers evidence-informed recommendations for future research and policy action.

## 1. How Food Processing Technologies and Additives Affect the Body and Brain

### 1.1 The NOVA Classification System

The scientific study of food processing and health has been transformed by the NOVA classification system, developed by researchers at the University of São Paulo. NOVA categorizes foods into four groups based on the extent and purpose of industrial processing:

- **Group 1:** Unprocessed or minimally processed foods (fresh fruits, vegetables, meat, milk, eggs)
- **Group 2:** Processed culinary ingredients (oils, butter, sugar, salt)
- **Group 3:** Processed foods (canned vegetables, salted nuts, cheese, smoked fish)
- **Group 4:** Ultra-processed foods (UPFs)—industrial formulations made predominantly from substances extracted from foods, with little to no whole food content, and containing additives such as colors, flavors, emulsifiers, and non-sugar sweeteners

UPFs are operationally defined by the presence of ingredients of "no culinary use"—including modified starches, hydrogenated oils, protein isolates, high-fructose corn syrup, and a wide array of cosmetic additives—and by their ready-to-eat or ready-to-heat nature. The NOVA system has been adopted by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), and the dietary guidelines of multiple countries including Brazil, Uruguay, Canada, and Israel.

### 1.2 Epidemiological Evidence of Health Harm

The most comprehensive synthesis of the evidence to date is the 2024 umbrella review published in *The BMJ* by Lane et al., which analyzed 45 distinct pooled analyses encompassing nearly 10 million participants. The review found that greater exposure to UPFs was directly associated with:

- **Convincing evidence (Class I):** 50% increased risk of cardiovascular disease-related mortality (RR 1.50, 95% CI 1.37–1.63), 12% increased risk of type 2 diabetes (dose-response RR 1.12, 1.11–1.13), 48% higher risk of anxiety (OR 1.48, 1.37–1.59), and 53% higher risk of common mental disorders (OR 1.53, 1.43–1.63)
- **Highly suggestive evidence (Class II):** 21% increased risk of all-cause mortality (RR 1.21, 1.15–1.27), 66% higher risk of heart disease mortality (HR 1.66, 1.51–1.84), 40% higher risk of type 2 diabetes (OR 1.40, 1.23–1.59), 22% increased risk of depressive outcomes (HR 1.22, 1.16–1.28), and 55% higher risk of obesity (OR 1.55, 1.36–1.77)

A 2025 updated systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis by Liang et al., published in *Systematic Reviews*, confirmed a linear dose-response relationship: each 10% increment in the proportion of UPFs in the diet was associated with a 10% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.16), and the highest versus lowest consumption group showed a 15% increased risk (HR 1.15, 1.09–1.22).

An umbrella review by Dai et al. (2024) in *Clinical Nutrition* added further granularity, demonstrating convincing evidence for links between UPF consumption and renal function decline (OR 1.25, 1.18–1.33) and wheezing in children and adolescents (OR 1.42, 1.34–1.49), and highly suggestive evidence for diabetes, overweight, obesity, depression, and common mental disorders. Notably, no study to date has reported beneficial health outcomes associated with UPF consumption.

### 1.3 Biological Mechanisms: Beyond Nutrient Composition

The mechanisms by which UPFs cause harm extend beyond their well-known poor nutritional profiles (high in added sugars, saturated fats, and sodium, low in fiber, protein, and micronutrients). Emerging research points to several distinct pathways:

#### 1.3.1 Disruption of the Gut Microbiome

A 2024 review in *Nature Reviews Gastroenterology & Hepatology* documented that food additives commonly added to UPFs—including emulsifiers, non-sugar sweeteners, colors, and microparticles/nanoparticles—have been shown in preclinical studies to affect the gut microbiome, intestinal permeability, and intestinal inflammation. Emulsifiers such as carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) and polysorbate-80 (P80) have been shown to reduce microbial diversity, promote the erosion of the protective mucus layer, and allow bacteria to move closer to the gut wall, triggering chronic low-grade inflammation. A randomized controlled feeding study by Chassaing et al. (2022) demonstrated that dietary emulsifier CMC consumption induced detrimental impacts on the gut microbiota and metabolome in humans.

The 2025 systematic review by Liang et al. noted that food additives "affect the gut microbiome, leading to reduced diversity and shifts in bacterial populations, such as more harmful *Shigella* and fewer beneficial *Lachnospira* and *Roseburia*." These microbial shifts may impair gut barrier function, promote metabolic endotoxemia, and contribute to the pathogenesis of obesity, insulin resistance, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

#### 1.3.2 Alterations to the Food Matrix and Satiety Signaling

The intensive processing of UPFs fundamentally alters the physical structure of food—a phenomenon known as "dietary reconstitution" or "food matrix disruption." These alterations affect digestion, nutrient absorption, and orosensory properties. UPFs are typically soft, hyper-palatable, and rapidly consumed, leading to faster eating rates and delayed satiety signals. The 2025 systematic review by Liang et al. explains that "the health effects of UPFs could be linked to their organoleptic properties, which may lead to an increased eating speed and a delay in satiety signals, resulting in higher overall food intake and poor health outcomes."

A landmark randomized controlled trial by Hall et al. (2019) at the National Institutes of Health demonstrated that individuals consuming an ultra-processed diet ad libitum ate approximately 500 kcal more per day and gained significant weight compared to those consuming a matched unprocessed diet, despite the meals being matched for macronutrients, sugar, sodium, and fiber. This finding strongly implicates processing itself—beyond nutrient composition—as a driver of overconsumption.

#### 1.3.3 Neurobiological Effects and Brain Reward Circuitry

A growing body of evidence suggests that UPFs may exert addictive-like effects on the brain's reward circuitry. The combination of refined carbohydrates and fats, which rarely occurs in nature, appears to have a supra-additive effect on brain reward systems, exceeding the response to either macronutrient alone. A 2023 review of 281 studies across 36 countries found that 14% of adults and 12% of children met criteria for food addiction as measured by the Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS), with prevalence rates comparable to those for legal addictive substances such as alcohol and tobacco.

The neurobiological mechanisms involve:

- **Dopamine signaling:** UPFs, particularly those high in refined sugars and saturated fats, trigger rapid and amplified dopamine release in the ventral striatum, nucleus accumbens, and other reward-related brain regions. A 2025 review in *Pharmacological Research* by Hough et al. demonstrates that repeated consumption of hyper-palatable foods leads to dopamine receptor downregulation (particularly D2 receptors), reduced dopamine signaling, and compulsive reward-seeking behavior—a pattern mirroring substance use disorders.
- **Speed of reward:** UPFs are absorbed rapidly through the gastrointestinal tract, delivering glucose and other substrates to the brain with a speed that mimics the pharmacokinetics of addictive drugs. As researchers at the University of Texas Medical Branch note, "UPFs are absorbed quicker and hit the brain releasing dopamine quicker," producing a "quick fix" that reinforces cravings and habitual consumption.
- **Opioid and endocannabinoid systems:** Beyond dopamine, hyper-palatable foods activate endogenous opioid pathways and the endocannabinoid system, contributing to the hedonic experience of eating and the development of compulsive consumption patterns.

A 2024 study published in *Clinical Nutrition* provided mechanistic evidence linking artificial sweeteners (such as aspartame) to depression, noting that aspartame "was shown to inhibit the synthesis and release of neurotransmitters, dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin, which are important in the development of depression." Certain food colorings (tartrazine, carmine) have been shown to induce pro-inflammatory cytokines and hypersensitivity reactions, with implications for both mental and physical health.

#### 1.3.4 Inflammation and Oxidative Stress

High UPF consumption promotes a pro-inflammatory state through multiple pathways: the direct effects of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) formed during high-temperature processing, the disruption of the gut barrier leading to endotoxemia, the displacement of anti-inflammatory nutrients (fiber, polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids), and the presence of neo-formed contaminants such as acrylamide and heterocyclic amines. Chronic low-grade inflammation is a well-established pathway linking diet to cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and mood disorders.

## 2. How the Food Industry's Marketing and Product Design Influence Eating Behaviors and Food Choices

### 2.1 The Scale and Scope of Food Marketing

Food marketing is a multi-billion-dollar global industry designed to shape consumer preferences, drive purchasing decisions, and maximize consumption. The World Health Organization has identified food marketing as a major commercial determinant of health, particularly for children and adolescents who are developmentally vulnerable to persuasive messaging. A 2025 review in *Obesity Reviews* by the Rudd Center for Food Policy and Health documents that the overwhelming majority of food marketing expenditures promote ultra-processed products high in sugar, fat, and sodium.

### 2.2 Marketing Strategies and Their Mechanisms of Influence

#### 2.2.1 Portion Distortion and Supersizing

The practice of "supersizing"—offering larger portions at minimal additional cost—became widespread in the 1990s and has fundamentally altered social norms around appropriate serving sizes. A 2024 narrative review on food marketing and obesity published in *Frontiers in Public Health* explains:

> "Portion control is an essential but frequently overlooked aspect of food marketing that greatly aids in creating obesogenic environments. The practice of 'supersizing' encourages overconsumption while boosting unit sales volume. Packaging design is also essential; 'portion distortion' refers to how visual signals and container proportions frequently skew perceptions of proper serving quantities. Research shows that regardless of hunger or satiety signs, people consistently eat more when given larger quantities."

Controlled trials have demonstrated that exposure to larger portion sizes can result in long-term increases in daily calorie intake, contributing directly to positive energy balance and weight gain. This effect is particularly pronounced in children and adolescents, who have limited capacity for self-regulation of intake.

#### 2.2.2 Digital Marketing and Social Media

The rise of digital platforms has transformed food marketing, enabling unprecedented levels of personalization, interactivity, and reach. A 2024 systematic review in the *International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity* found that children and adolescents are heavily exposed to food marketing on social media platforms (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube), with exposure increasing with age. Adolescents may view more than 9,000 instances of food marketing per year on social media alone.

Key digital marketing tactics include:

- **Influencer marketing:** Brands partner with social media personalities, including "kidfluencers," to promote products in ways that appear authentic and organic. A 2024 review in *Frontiers in Public Health* notes that "influencer relationships, culturally relevant narratives, and geodemographic profiling" are used "to promote ultra-processed meals to low-income ethnic groups and metropolitan areas."
- **Behavioral targeting:** Personal data and online activity patterns are used to deliver targeted advertisements. Children's data is collected through games, apps, and social media, enabling the delivery of personalized food marketing that is difficult to distinguish from organic content.
- **Engagement-driven techniques:** Brands encourage user interaction through likes, shares, comments, and user-generated content campaigns, leveraging peer networks to amplify marketing reach. The EuroHealthNet Magazine notes that "most of our eating behavior is automatic, guided by subconscious processes," and that constant exposure to digital food marketing shapes preferences, attitudes, and eating habits in ways that consumers may not consciously recognize.

#### 2.2.3 Targeted Marketing to Vulnerable Populations

A major concern documented in the 2024 *Frontiers in Public Health* review is the disproportionate targeting of Black and Hispanic youth in the United States with marketing for fast food, sugar-sweetened beverages, and snack products. The Rudd Center's 2021 research found that:

> "Ads for fast food, sugar-sweetened beverages, and snack goods continue to be disproportionately shown to Black and Hispanic kids in the US on digital and television media. These advertisements frequently use language, music, and imagery that are culturally appropriate and intended to appeal to particular ethnic groups to boost brand engagement and purchase intention."

Multinational food companies in Brazil and South Africa have similarly used culturally relevant narratives and geodemographic profiling to promote UPFs to low-income ethnic groups. These marketing strategies, while presented as "inclusive" or "culturally sensitive," systematically worsen health inequities by pushing nutrient-poor, energy-dense products on populations already at elevated risk for obesity and diet-related diseases.

#### 2.2.4 Product Design and the "Bliss Point"

The food industry employs food scientists and sensory psychologists to engineer products with optimal levels of sugar, fat, salt, and texture—the "bliss point"—that maximize palatability and consumption. Research from internal tobacco industry documents, analyzed by Fazzino et al. at the University of California, San Francisco, revealed that tobacco companies that owned major food corporations (Philip Morris owned Kraft-General Foods; R.J. Reynolds owned Nabisco) applied the same principles of product design and addiction engineering to processed foods. As described by Ashley Gearhardt, these companies "engineered processed food to hit what is known as our 'bliss' point and elicit craving."

The combination of refined carbohydrates and fats in UPFs is particularly potent. A 2023 review in *The BMJ* by Gearhardt et al. proposes that "the combination of refined carbohydrates and fats often found in UPFs seems to have a supra-additive effect on brain reward systems, above either macronutrient alone, which may increase the addictive potential of these foods."

### 2.3 The "Health Halo" and Front-of-Package Deception

Food marketing frequently employs "health halos"—implicit claims that a product is nutritious or beneficial based on selective attributes such as "low fat," "high protein," "contains whole grains," or "natural." A 2024 review in *Appetite* found that front-of-package claims are often misleading, leading consumers to perceive ultra-processed products as healthier than they actually are. The use of brand logos, cartoon characters, and sports endorsements further exploits children's developmental vulnerabilities, making it difficult for them to distinguish between entertainment and advertising.

## 3. Individual Susceptibility: Why Some People Are More Vulnerable to Processed Food Addiction and Health Effects

### 3.1 Genetic Factors

Substantial evidence indicates that genetic variation contributes to individual differences in susceptibility to food addiction and the metabolic consequences of UPF consumption. Twin studies estimate that approximately 40–60% of the risk for addictive behaviors, including food addiction, is attributable to genetic factors.

#### 3.1.1 Dopamine Pathway Genes

The dopamine system is centrally implicated in reward processing, and polymorphisms in dopamine-related genes have been associated with food addiction and compulsive eating. A 2024 study in *Frontiers in Nutrition* examining Chilean adults found that genotypes associated with low dopaminergic signaling were linked to snack food choice, emotional eating, and uncontrolled eating. The study concluded that "these results provide evidence for the involvement of genotypes associated with low dopaminergic signaling in eating behavior."

A 2023 study published in *Appetite* demonstrated that a genetic risk score reflecting reduced dopamine signaling in the ventral striatum was associated with food addiction symptoms and higher body mass index, even in lean individuals. The authors state that "emerging symptoms of food addiction and risk for obesity may have identifiable biological correlates in lean individuals," suggesting that genetic risk is detectable before the onset of obesity.

#### 3.1.2 The Melanocortin-4 Receptor (MC4R) Gene

A 2025 study in the *Journal of Eating Disorders* investigated the contribution of MC4R polymorphisms to food addiction in 126 individuals with obesity. The study found a direct association between the rs17782313 variant and food addiction, with carriers of the C allele showing higher risk. Among those with food addiction, C allele carriers exhibited significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression than T allele carriers. The MC4R gene is a critical regulator of energy homeostasis and appetite; its variants have been consistently linked to obesity, hyperphagia, and food reward sensitivity.

#### 3.1.3 The FTO Gene and Gene-Environment Interactions

The fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) gene is among the most robustly replicated genetic loci for obesity. Genome-wide association studies have identified overlap between FTO variants and food addiction traits. The FTO gene influences appetite regulation, satiety signaling, and the neural response to food cues. Cross-sectional studies suggest that the effect of FTO on obesity risk is amplified in obesogenic environments—those with high availability of UPFs—and attenuated in environments with healthier food access, illustrating the critical importance of gene-environment interactions.

### 3.2 Neurobiological Differences

#### 3.2.1 Dopamine Receptor Density and Reward Sensitivity

Individual differences in baseline dopamine receptor density (particularly D2 receptors) in the striatum have been linked to differing vulnerability to food addiction. Individuals with lower D2 receptor availability may require greater stimulation from food to achieve the same level of reward, predisposing them to overconsumption of hyper-palatable UPFs. A 2024 study in *Pharmacological Research* notes that "repeated dopamine overstimulation leads to receptor downregulation and compulsive reward-seeking, reinforcing craving patterns." This neuroadaptation is more pronounced in some individuals, creating a vicious cycle of increasing consumption and diminishing reward.

#### 3.2.2 Functional Brain Connectivity

A 2024 fMRI study in *NeuroImage* found that individuals with food addiction symptoms showed altered functional connectivity between the nucleus accumbens, prefrontal cortex, and insula—networks involved in reward valuation, inhibitory control, and interoceptive awareness. Reduced prefrontal cortex activity during exposure to food cues was associated with poorer self-regulation and greater UPF consumption. These differences may have both genetic and developmental origins, with early-life exposure to UPFs potentially shaping brain circuitry in ways that increase future vulnerability.

### 3.3 Psychological and Behavioral Factors

#### 3.3.1 Impulsivity and Sensation-Seeking

Personality traits such as impulsivity, sensation-seeking, and negative emotionality are well-established risk factors for both substance use disorders and food addiction. Individuals high in impulsivity show stronger neural responses to food cues, greater difficulty resisting temptation, and a higher likelihood of binge eating. The Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS), which has been validated in 36 countries, assesses symptoms including loss of control over consumption, persistent desire or failed attempts to cut down, and continued use despite negative consequences—all of which parallel substance use disorder criteria.

#### 3.3.2 Childhood Adversity and Stress

A 2023 review in *Nutrients* found that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), including trauma, neglect, and household dysfunction, are associated with a two- to three-fold increase in the risk of food addiction in adulthood. The mechanistic pathway involves dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, altered dopamine signaling, and increased reliance on highly palatable foods as a source of comfort or emotional regulation. Chronic stress upregulates the reward value of calorie-dense foods while simultaneously impairing prefrontal executive function, creating a "perfect storm" for compulsive eating.

### 3.4 Sex Differences

Epidemiological studies consistently find that food addiction prevalence is higher in women than in men. A 2024 systematic review reported weighted mean prevalence of 12.2% in women versus 6.4% in men. Sex differences in hormonal regulation (including estrogen's modulation of dopamine signaling), differential socialization around eating and body image, and higher rates of mood disorders in women may contribute to this disparity.

## 4. Environmental Factors: Food Deserts, Economic Inequality, and the Structural Constraints on Individual Choice

### 4.1 Food Deserts and Limited Food Access

The term "food desert" refers to geographic areas where residents have limited access to affordable, nutritious food—particularly fresh fruits, vegetables, and whole foods. According to 2025 analysis of the USDA Food Access Research Atlas, nearly 39.5 million people in the United States were living in low-income, low-access areas. The states with the highest proportion of residents in such areas are concentrated in the South: Mississippi (30% of residents), New Mexico (29%), and Arkansas (26%).

Food deserts are characterized by:

- **Absence of supermarkets:** Residents in low-income neighborhoods have fewer supermarkets per capita than residents in higher-income neighborhoods, even after controlling for population density. Instead, these areas are dominated by convenience stores, corner stores, and fast-food outlets that primarily stock shelf-stable, ultra-processed products.
- **Transportation barriers:** Low-income families are less likely to have reliable personal vehicles or access to public transportation that would enable travel to supermarkets in other neighborhoods. This creates a "food swamp" environment where unhealthy options are abundant and healthy options are scarce.
- **Higher prices:** Even when healthy foods are available in low-income neighborhoods, they are often priced higher than in affluent areas. A 2025 Annie E. Casey Foundation report notes that "low-income families are less likely to have reliable transportation (either a personal vehicle or the ability to pay for transportation), which can prevent residents from traveling where needed to buy nutritious groceries."

### 4.2 Food Insecurity and UPF Consumption

Food insecurity—defined by the USDA as lack of consistent access to enough food for an active, healthy life—is strongly associated with higher UPF consumption. The USDA's 2024 Household Food Security report revealed that 13.7% of U.S. households (47.9 million people) experienced food insecurity, including 14.1 million children.

A 2022 study in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* found that food-insecure adults consume nearly 6% more ultra-processed foods compared to those with high food security. A 2023 study in *PLoS ONE* examining SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) participants found that "food-insecure adults and adults receiving SNAP have greater consumption of ultra-processed foods than their counterparts, suggesting that ultra-processed food consumption may help to explain the diet-related health disparities observed in these populations."

The mechanisms linking food insecurity to UPF consumption are:

- **Lower cost:** UPFs are energy-dense but nutrient-poor, providing the most calories per unit cost. When household budgets are constrained, calorie density becomes the primary driver of food choice.
- **Shelf stability:** UPFs require no refrigeration and have long shelf lives, making them practical for households with unreliable housing, limited storage, or frequent moves.
- **Time constraints:** Food-insecure households often face significant time poverty, relying on convenience foods that require minimal preparation time.
- **Psychosocial stress:** Food insecurity is a chronic stressor that increases cortisol levels and promotes cravings for calorie-dense comfort foods.

### 4.3 Economic Inequality and Nutritional Inequality

Socioeconomic status is one of the strongest predictors of diet quality. A 2024 study by the Global Food Research Program at the University of North Carolina found stark disparities in UPF purchases across socioeconomic groups:

> "Households with lower incomes and lower education levels purchased a higher proportion of UPFs compared to higher-income, higher-education households. Race and ethnicity also played a role, with non-Hispanic White households purchasing a significantly higher proportion of UPFs than Black, Hispanic, or other race/ethnic groups."

This finding is counterintuitive—non-Hispanic White households purchased more UPFs overall—but reflects the fact that UPF consumption is high across all groups, with the most nutritionally vulnerable populations (lower-income, less educated, and racial/ethnic minorities) facing the greatest disproportionate health burden. The study found that 90% of carbonated soft drinks, 81% of mixed dishes and soups, and 71% of sweets and snacks were ultra-processed, while minimally processed foods (fruits, vegetables, nuts, oils) made up only 12% of total purchases.

A 2025 study in *The Nation's Health* reported that ultra-processed foods now account for nearly two-thirds of calories in the American diet, with consumption even higher among lower-income groups. The World Health Organization has identified this pattern as a key driver of the "double burden of malnutrition"—the coexistence of undernutrition and obesity—in low- and middle-income countries.

### 4.4 The Commercial Determinants of Health

The concept of "commercial determinants of health" has gained prominence in public health discourse, recognizing that corporate practices—including product formulation, marketing, lobbying, and supply chain management—are primary drivers of diet-related disease. A 2025 policy brief from the BMA (British Medical Association) documented that in the UK government between January 2020 and June 2024, "ministers met with food businesses and their trade associations 1,408 times—40 times more than with food Non-Governmental Organisations and ministers." This disproportionate access has been leveraged to delay or dilute public health regulations.

The BMA report further notes that "in May 2025 industry was involved in influencing yet another delay to junk food advertising restrictions being implemented as well as a watering down of the industry guidance on what can be advertised." Such corporate influence operates globally, with transnational food corporations employing similar strategies to resist regulation, shape scientific discourse, and maintain market share across countries.

### 4.5 Food Environments in Low- and Middle-Income Countries

The nutritional transition in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) has been accompanied by a rapid expansion of UPF availability and marketing. A 2024 review in *Frontiers in Public Health* documents that:

> "Food marketing strategies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) increasingly take advantage of urbanization, growing digital access, and lax regulatory monitoring. The widespread use of mobile devices has made it possible for aggressive digital advertising to target children and teenagers with highly processed and energy-dense meals, particularly through social media and gaming platforms."

In Brazil, the concentration of UPF retailers in lower-income neighborhoods has been documented, with a 2023 study in *Nutrients* finding that "the greater concentration of establishments selling ultra-processed foods in these neighborhoods raises important reflections on the social, economic, political, and health inequalities historically present in Brazilian society." Similar patterns have been observed in South Africa, Mexico, India, and China.

## 5. Public Health Policy Approaches: Global Evidence and Emerging Strategies

### 5.1 Front-of-Pack Warning Labels

Latin America has led the world in implementing mandatory front-of-pack (FOP) warning labels. Chile's 2016 Law of Food Labeling and Advertising introduced black, octagonal "stop sign" warning labels on products high in sugar, salt, saturated fat, or calories. Evaluations have demonstrated substantial impacts:

- 24% reduction in calories purchased from labeled products
- 37% reduction in salt purchases from labeled products
- 27% reduction in saturated fat purchases
- 10% reduction in overall sugar purchases

Beyond Chile, similar warning label policies have been adopted in Peru, Uruguay, Mexico, Argentina, Colombia, and Venezuela, with 30 countries across Latin America introducing some form of FOP warning label system. A 2024 study in *The Lancet Regional Health – Americas* documented that these policies, when combined with marketing restrictions and school bans, are capable of regulating "at least 97–98%" of ultra-processed foods in these countries.

Mexico's 2020 warning label law, which required labels on products containing "excess" sugar, calories, sodium, or saturated fat, has been described as a "boulder going down the hill" in terms of policy diffusion. Researchers note that the food industry "put a lot of their energy and efforts into stopping Mexico because Mexico is seen as kind of the fulcrum of Latin America."

Evidence from multiple countries indicates that warning labels are most effective when:

- They are mandatory rather than voluntary
- They use simple, interpretable symbols (e.g., stop signs) rather than numeric or color-coded systems
- They are combined with restrictions on marketing, especially to children
- They are accompanied by public education campaigns

### 5.2 Taxation and Price Policies

Sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) taxes have been implemented in over 50 countries. Mexico's 2014 excise tax on SSBs led to a 12% reduction in purchases in the first year, with the largest reductions observed among low-income households. A 2023 meta-analysis in *The BMJ* found that SSB taxes of 10–20% were associated with average reductions in consumption of 15–20%.

Emerging evidence supports the extension of taxation to ultra-processed foods more broadly. The 2025 umbrella review by Liang et al. notes that "strategies such as front-of-pack labeling, taxation, and marketing restrictions could reduce UPF consumption by up to 30%." However, the authors caution that "these strategies should be carefully considered, particularly for regions with lower socioeconomic status, who may rely on UPFs owing to affordability and convenience. Implementing subsidies for healthier foods and improving access to fresh produce could help ensure equitable policy outcomes."

### 5.3 Marketing Restrictions

Chile's comprehensive approach included banning the advertising of products with warning labels on television and radio during hours when children are the primary audience (6:00 AM to 10:00 PM), as well as prohibiting the use of cartoons, characters, and toys in marketing to children. The UK has implemented a 9:00 PM watershed restriction on television advertising of high-fat, salt, and sugar (HFSS) products, though implementation has faced repeated delays due to industry lobbying.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis in *JAMA Pediatrics* by Boyland et al. found that food marketing exposure was significantly associated with increased food intake, preference for marketed products, and purchase requests among children. The review concluded that statutory (mandatory) regulations are more effective than voluntary industry self-regulation.

### 5.4 School-Based Policies

School food environment policies represent a key intervention point. Chile's law banned the sale of all products with warning labels in schools. Brazil's National School Feeding Program requires that at least 30% of ingredients be sourced from family farms and prohibits the purchase of ultra-processed products. A 2023 review in *The Lancet* found that comprehensive school food policies that combine nutrition standards, restrictions on marketing, and nutrition education are more effective than any single component alone.

### 5.5 Dietary Guidelines

The 2014 Brazilian Dietary Guidelines were the first to explicitly recommend avoiding ultra-processed foods, using the NOVA classification as their organizing framework. Since then, seven additional countries have adopted the term "ultra-processed" and similar recommendations in their national dietary guidelines, including Uruguay, Canada, Israel, and Ecuador. The 2021–2025 U.S. Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee considered evidence on UPFs but did not include specific recommendations, a gap that has been criticized by public health experts.

A 2024 policy brief from Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health recommends that the next U.S. Dietary Guidelines "set a policy around ultra-processed food. If they remain silent, it'll be another decade or two before our kids can benefit from needed policy changes." The brief suggests that the secretaries of health and agriculture could state: "We don't know why but ultra-processed food is making us sick."

### 5.6 Defining UPFs for Policy Purposes

A major barrier to regulatory action has been the lack of consensus on how to define UPFs for policy purposes. In 2024, Healthy Eating Research convened an expert panel to develop evidence-informed recommendations, producing a report that:

- Endorses an ingredient-based definition of UPFs suitable for policy use
- Provides guidance for exempting foods widely recommended as healthy
- Recommends specific policy options at the local, state, and federal levels

The panel's recommendations include: requiring warning labels, restricting marketing to children, prohibiting UPF sales in schools and public institutions, and implementing procurement policies that limit the purchase of UPFs with public funds.

### 5.7 Recognizing the Addictive Nature of UPFs

A December 2025 policy brief from the University of Michigan's Institute for Healthcare Policy and Innovation recommended that UPFs be recognized as addictive substances, arguing that "the unique combination of ingredients in ultra-processed food stimulates the brain in ways that can lead to addictive-like eating." The brief calls for:

- Inclusion of food addiction in diagnostic classifications (e.g., DSM-5, ICD-11)
- Regulation of UPF formulation and marketing consistent with other addictive products
- Treatment and support services for individuals with food addiction
- Public education campaigns about the addictive potential of UPFs

A 2023 review in *The BMJ* by Gearhardt et al. concluded that "the combination of refined carbohydrates and fats often found in UPFs seems to have a supra-additive effect on brain reward systems... which may increase the addictive potential of these foods." The authors note that 14% of adults and 12% of children meet criteria for food addiction, "similar to the levels of addiction seen for other legal substances in adults (e.g., 14% for alcohol)."

## 6. Conclusion and Recommendations

The evidence is now clear and consistent: ultra-processed foods are a major contributor to the global burden of non-communicable disease, and their effects are mediated through multiple biological, psychological, and structural pathways. The convergence of epidemiological, mechanistic, and behavioral evidence supports urgent policy action.

### 6.1 Research Priorities

The 2024 *BMJ* umbrella review and the 2025 *Systematic Reviews* meta-analysis both emphasize the need for:

1. **Mechanistic research:** Randomized controlled trials and experimental studies are needed to identify the causal pathways linking UPF consumption to specific health outcomes, including the roles of food matrix disruption, gut microbiome alterations, and neurobiological changes.
2. **Longitudinal studies:** Extended follow-up of cohorts is needed to assess the cumulative effects of UPF exposure across the life course, including intergenerational effects.
3. **Implementation science:** Research is needed on the effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and equity implications of different policy approaches, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.
4. **Industry accountability:** Independent research on corporate practices, including product reformulation, marketing strategies, and political influence, is essential for informed policymaking.

### 6.2 Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence reviewed, the following policy actions are warranted:

1. **Adopt a clear definition of UPFs** for regulatory purposes, building on the NOVA classification and the 2024 Healthy Eating Research expert panel recommendations.
2. **Implement mandatory front-of-pack warning labels** using simple, interpretable symbols, consistent with the evidence from Latin America.
3. **Restrict marketing of UPFs to children** across all media platforms, including digital and social media, with statutory rather than voluntary regulation.
4. **Tax UPFs and sugar-sweetened beverages** while simultaneously subsidizing fruits, vegetables, and other minimally processed foods to ensure equitable access.
5. **Eliminate UPFs from schools and public institutions** through procurement policies and nutrition standards.
6. **Update national dietary guidelines** to explicitly recommend limiting or avoiding UPFs.
7. **Fund public education campaigns** to increase awareness of the health risks of UPFs and the benefits of minimally processed diets.
8. **Regulate the formulation of UPFs** to reduce levels of added sugars, sodium, and saturated fats, and to eliminate additives with demonstrated health risks.
9. **Address structural determinants** including food deserts, economic inequality, and the commercial determinants of health through multisectoral policy action.
