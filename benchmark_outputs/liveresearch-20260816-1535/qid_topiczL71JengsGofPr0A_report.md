

# Remote Work and Early-Career Employment in the United States and Europe: A Comprehensive Research Report (2023–2026)

---

## Executive Summary

The landscape of remote work has undergone a profound transformation since the COVID-19 pandemic. By 2025–2026, remote and hybrid work arrangements have stabilized at levels far above 2019 baselines, but the distribution of flexibility is highly uneven. A growing body of evidence—including analyses of 50 million European job postings, Federal Reserve research, multi-country surveys by Stanford’s WFH Research project, and Eurofound’s EU-wide surveys—reveals that **entry-level and early-career professionals face the most constrained access to remote work, and the shift toward distributed work is reshaping hiring dynamics in ways that often disadvantage new graduates.** This report provides a comprehensive, evidence-based examination of how remote work is affecting early-career opportunities, skill requirements, earning potential, networking, and industry-specific commitments to flexibility, drawing on data from 2023 through mid-2026.

## 1. Remote Work’s Impact on Entry-Level Job Opportunities and Career Growth

### 1.1 Limited Access to Flexible Work for Early-Career Professionals

Across both the United States and Europe, flexibility in work location is strongly correlated with experience level. Data from multiple sources converge on a clear conclusion: **entry-level workers have the least access to remote or hybrid arrangements.**

**Robert Half’s 2026 analysis of U.S. job postings** (based on TalentNeuron data) breaks down availability by experience level:

- **Senior-level (5+ years):** 12% hybrid, 4% remote
- **Mid-level (3–5 years):** 10% hybrid, 3% remote
- **Entry-level (0–2 years):** 8% hybrid, 2% remote  

Source: [Robert Half Remote Work Statistics and Trends 2026](https://www.roberthalf.com/us/en/insights/research/remote-work-statistics-and-trends)

The **Interview Guys State of Remote Work 2025** report similarly finds that only **28% of entry-level jobs** offer any flexibility (18% hybrid, 10% remote), compared to significantly higher rates for senior roles. The report notes that organizations often view flexibility as “something you must earn rather than a baseline offering.”  

Source: [The Interview Guys – State of Remote Work 2025](https://blog.theinterviewguys.com/state-of-remote-work-2025)

The **National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE) Job Outlook 2025 Spring Update** reports that for entry-level positions, just **6% are fully remote**, 49% are hybrid, and 45% are fully in-person. Among internships for the 2024–25 cohort, only **0.9% are exclusively remote**, while 61.3% are hybrid and 34.7% are in-person.  

Source: [NACE – Internship, Entry-Level Job Modality Corresponds with Students’ Job Preferences](https://www.naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/trends-and-predictions/internship-entry-level-job-modality-corresponds-with-students-job-preferences)

### 1.2 The Shift in Hiring Patterns: Remote Work Reduces Demand for Entry-Level Talent

A growing body of rigorous academic research shows that the rise of remote work is directly contributing to declining opportunities for early-career professionals.

**HBR Study (August 2026):** Zhenyu Liao, Letian Zhang, and Shinan Wang analyzed **50 million European job postings** and conducted experiments with **1,200 hiring managers**. They found that **remote roles demand roughly 25% more skills, plus more experience and credentials than otherwise identical in-person jobs.** The authors attribute this to larger applicant pools, harder-to-verify fit, and the perceived cost and burden of onboarding new talent remotely. The implication is that entry-level candidates are systematically filtered out of remote positions in favor of more experienced workers.  

Source: [Harvard Business Review – Research: How Remote Work Is Narrowing Early-Career Opportunities (August 7, 2026)](https://hbr.org/2026/08/research-how-remote-work-is-narrowing-early-career-opportunities)

**Lambert & Schindler (2025):** Peter John Lambert (LSE) and Yannick Schindler (EIT Oxford) compared resume and job board data from 2017–2019 vs. 2023–2025. They found a **29% decline in entry-level hiring** and a **nearly 26% decline in junior-level hiring**, while hiring for senior-level roles increased by more than 5%. The researchers concluded that work-from-home arrangements are a “robust predictor of declining relative demand for early-career workers” and have “tilted hiring and recruitment towards more senior and experienced workers.”  

Source: [Built In – Remote Work May Be a Bigger Threat to Entry-Level Jobs Than AI](https://builtin.com/articles/remote-work-impact-on-entry-level-hiring)

**Federal Reserve Bank of New York (June 2026):** In a landmark analysis, NY Fed economists Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington, and Amanda Pallais found that the **unemployment rate for young college graduates rose from 3.6% in 2019 to 5.6% in 2026**. They estimate that **remote work accounts for 64% of this increase**. The researchers note: “Employers may not want to hire fresh graduates onto distributed teams because it is more difficult to teach them the requisite skills from afar.” By contrast, in non-remotable jobs, the youth unemployment gap returned to baseline after the pandemic.  

Sources:  
[CNBC – Remote work is worsening youth unemployment: New York Fed (June 2026)](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/01/remote-work-youth-unemployment-new-york-fed.html)  
[Federal Reserve Bank of New York Liberty Street Economics – Remote Work Leaves Younger Workers Sidelined (June 1, 2026)](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/06/remote-work-leaves-younger-workers-sidelined/)  
[Fortune – Mounting evidence suggests remote work is behind the Gen Z hiring nightmare (June 2, 2026)](https://fortune.com/2026/06/02/remote-work-causing-youth-unemployment-federal-reserve)  
[WSJ – Remote Work Drives 64% of Youth Unemployment Rise (2026)](https://www.wsj.com/pro/central-banking/remote-work-may-be-fueling-youth-unemployment-5a69a240)

### 1.3 Career Growth and Advancement Concerns

Concerns about career progression in remote settings are well-founded. **Zoom’s research** (cited in the Interview Guys report) finds that fully remote workers are **1.3 times more likely to feel job insecurity** than those always in the office. **85% of remote workers surveyed believe that office time helps their career advancement**, reflecting a widespread recognition that “face-time” with managers and executives influences promotion decisions, even in ostensibly meritocratic systems.

However, the same report notes that forward-thinking organizations are beginning to implement **outcome-based promotion criteria** and **skills-first hiring practices** that do not penalize remote workers.  

Source: [The Interview Guys – State of Remote Work 2025](https://blog.theinterviewguys.com/state-of-remote-work-2025)

**Nicholas Bloom’s hybrid work experiment** at Trip.com (published in *Nature*, 2024) found that hybrid workers (three days in-office, two days remote) were **just as likely to be promoted** as fully in-office workers. This suggests that **moderate hybrid arrangements** (2–3 days in-office) may preserve career advancement opportunities, while fully remote arrangements may be more problematic.  

Source: [Nature – Hybrid working from home improves retention without damaging performance (2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2)  
Summary: [Stanford SIEPR – Hybrid work is a “win-win-win” for companies, workers, study finds](https://siepr.stanford.edu/news/hybrid-work-win-win-win-companies-workers-study-finds)

### 1.4 The European Context

In Europe, remote work adoption has stabilized at structurally elevated levels. **Eurofound’s 2024 e-survey** found that the share of workers working entirely from the workplace rose from 36% in 2023 to 41% in 2024, signalling a partial return to the workplace but not a full reversal. **Eurostat data** indicates that approximately **22% of EU employed people worked from home in 2023**, with wide variation by country: the Netherlands (52% sometimes or most of the time), Finland (25%), Sweden (38%), Luxembourg (41%), versus much lower rates in Italy (6%), Hungary (7%), Bulgaria (8%), and Romania (4%).  

Sources:  
[Eurofound – Opportunities for remote work decline despite worker preferences (2024)](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/eurofound/pressreleases/opportunities-for-remote-work-decline-despite-workers-preferences-new-survey-finds-3364383)  
[Eurofound – Work from home preferences, 2020–2024, EU](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/surveys-and-data/data-catalogue/work-home-preferences-2020-2024-eu)  
[Eurofound – Living and working in Europe 2024](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/living-and-working-europe-2024)  
[Eurostat (via Statista) – EU remote work rates](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1376690/remote-work-in-europe-by-country/)

**Indeed Hiring Lab data** shows that in 2024, remote or hybrid language appeared in approximately **15–16% of postings in the UK and Germany**, **~10% in France**, and **~18% in Spain**—all well above pre-pandemic levels but plateaued since early 2023.  

Source: [Remotly Jobs – Europe Remote Work 2025: Data, Trends, Actions](https://blog.remotlyjobs.com/europe-remote-work-2025-data-trends)

The **HBR study of 50 million European job postings** (discussed above) confirms that the dynamic of reduced entry-level hiring in remote roles is equally pronounced in Europe, as the study was conducted using European data.

### 1.5 Gen Z’s Ambivalent Relationship with Remote Work

Contrary to stereotypes about digital natives preferring fully remote work, multiple surveys indicate that **Gen Z workers (born ~1997–2012) are the most likely to want some office time**. The Interview Guys report finds that **74% of Gen Z prefer face-to-face colleague interactions** (higher than Baby Boomers at 68% and Gen X at 66%), and Gen Z comes into the office an average of **three days per week**—more than any other age group. Only **26% of Gen Z want fully remote work**, lower than Millennials (31%), Gen X (33%), and Baby Boomers (37%).  

Source: [The Interview Guys – State of Remote Work 2025](https://blog.theinterviewguys.com/state-of-remote-work-2025)

A **Gallup poll (July 2025)** confirms that Gen Z workers favor hybrid work more than any other generation and are the least enthusiastic about exclusively remote work. This reflects a recognition that early-career professionals benefit from the **mentorship, relationship-building, and learning opportunities** that in-person work provides, as well as from the **implicit learning of workplace norms** that happens through observation and informal interaction.

## 2. Skills Required for Remote Positions vs. Traditional Office Jobs

### 2.1 The Premium on Self-Management and Communication

Remote work places a premium on skills that are either less critical or developed differently in office settings. The **Interview Guys report** identifies several core competencies:

- **Proactive communication and over-communication:** Regular updates, both work-related and social, are essential to prevent isolation and maintain visibility.
- **Self-management and accountability:** Setting one’s own schedule, managing workload, and meeting deadlines without external pressure.
- **Proactive relationship-building:** Scheduling virtual coffee chats, contributing to team channels, and making oneself visible without physical presence.
- **Work-life boundary management:** Creating psychological and physical separation between work and personal life, setting specific work hours, and actually disconnecting.
- **Digital collaboration proficiency:** Mastery of asynchronous communication tools, video conferencing etiquette, and project management platforms.

Source: [The Interview Guys – State of Remote Work 2025](https://blog.theinterviewguys.com/state-of-remote-work-2025)

### 2.3 Remote-Specific Skill Demands: The 25% Premium

The **HBR study** (2026) found that simply labeling a position as “fully remote” causes employers to **demand 25% more skills** compared to otherwise identical in-person roles. This includes both higher credential requirements and more years of experience. The study’s experiments with 1,200 hiring managers confirmed that this bias is driven by:

- **Larger applicant pools:** Remote positions attract more applicants, allowing employers to be more selective.
- **Harder-to-verify fit:** In the absence of in-person cues, employers rely more on formal credentials and experience as signals of quality.
- **Perceived onboarding costs:** Training new hires remotely is seen as more difficult and resource-intensive.

Source: [Harvard Business Review – Research: How Remote Work Is Narrowing Early-Career Opportunities (2026)](https://hbr.org/2026/08/research-how-remote-work-is-narrowing-early-career-opportunities)

### 2.4 Technical Skills in High Demand for Remote Roles

**Upskillist** (2025) reports that remote job postings for **data analysis roles jumped by 35% in 2024** (LinkedIn data), with professionals in these roles earning 20% more on average. **Project management tool expertise** saw a 40% increase in postings compared to pre-pandemic levels. **AI and machine learning specialist roles** have grown by **74% annually**, with remote opportunities in this field skyrocketing by **344% since 2019**.

**Cybersecurity** is another top remote-friendly field: surveys indicate that **70% of cybersecurity professionals work remotely**, and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 8% growth in digital marketing roles between 2023 and 2033.

## 3. Impact on Earning Potential and Professional Networking

### 3.1 Earning Potential: A Complex Picture

The relationship between remote work and compensation for early-career professionals is nuanced and varies by context.

**Willingness to trade salary for flexibility:** The **NBER working paper by Cullen, Pakzad-Hurson, and Perez-Truglia (January 2025)** found that U.S. tech workers are willing to accept a **25% pay cut** for partly or fully remote roles—three to five times higher than previous estimates. The **Owl Labs 2025 survey** reports that workers would sacrifice an average of **8–9% of their salary** for flexible working location or hours. Younger generations are more willing to make this trade-off.

Source: [NBER Working Paper No. 33383 – How Much Do Employees Value Remote Work? (January 2025)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33383/w33383.pdf)  
Summary: [Harvard Business School Working Knowledge – Remote Work or More Pay: What Tech Workers Value](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/remote-work-or-more-pay-what-tech-workers-value-in-one-chart)

**Salary levels by work arrangement:** According to **Bridged (2025)** data cited by Vena Solutions, median pay for office workers is approximately **$178,500**, for remote workers **$164,000**, and for hybrid roles **~$170,000**. However, this data reflects the overall workforce, which is heavily skewed toward senior workers in remote roles. For entry-level workers, the picture is different.

Source: [Vena Solutions – Remote Work Statistics and Trends for 2026](https://www.venasolutions.com/blog/remote-work-statistics)

**Entry-level salary dynamics:** A study in **Cogent Business & Management (2025)** on data science jobs found that “for entry-level or junior employees, remote work may not necessarily come with a significant pay increase, but for employees with more experience, compensation arrangements need to be considered more carefully.” This suggests that the remote wage premium is largely a **seniority-driven phenomenon**.

Source: [Taylor & Francis – Remote work impact on salary with experience level and company size (2025)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311975.2025.2577251)

**Cost savings:** The **Owl Labs report** notes that hybrid workers spend an average of **$55 per day** when going to the office (including $15 on commuting and $18 on lunch), while remote workers spend only **$18 per day at home**. For early-career professionals on tight budgets, these savings are significant, partially offsetting any potential salary differences.

Source: [Owl Labs – State of Hybrid Work 2025](https://owllabs.com/state-of-hybrid-work/2025)

### 3.2 Professional Networking and Mentorship

**The mentorship deficit:** Research consistently shows that remote work reduces access to informal learning, mentoring, and networking opportunities that are critical for career development. The **NACE “Redesigning Workplace Connectedness”** report notes that despite 80% of professionals acknowledging the importance of networking, only **40% have access to a mentor** at work.  

Source: [NACE – Redesigning Workplace Connectedness](https://www.naceweb.org/career-development/best-practices/redesigning-workplace-connectedness)

**The impact on Gen Z:** The **Interview Guys report** notes that 61% of Gen Z workers said it was challenging to make friends when working fully remote, and 39% found it difficult to connect with colleagues. The report emphasizes that Gen Z’s preference for at least some office time reflects their career stage: “As the newest entrants to professional environments, they’re still learning workplace norms, building professional networks, and establishing their reputations. These activities happen more naturally with at least some face-to-face interaction.”

**Owl Labs 2025** found that **25% of remote/hybrid workers believe they are missing out on impromptu or non-formal feedback and development opportunities** by not being in the office.

**The “Power of Proximity”:** The **New York Fed’s earlier research** found that young software engineers were more likely to come into the office than older engineers, recognizing the value of proximity for learning and advancement.

**Positive developments:** Despite these challenges, **remote mentorship is becoming more accessible and structured**. The **HPCC Systems guide to mentoring early-career professionals** notes that structured mentorship programs can help bridge the gap, with mentees reporting higher job satisfaction, faster promotions, and better problem-solving capabilities. **International virtual networking** via platforms like LinkedIn and Twitter/X has also expanded opportunities, as documented by Hayes et al. (2024) in *PMC*.

Sources:  
[HPCC Systems – Mentoring Early Career Professionals: A Beginner’s Guide](https://hpccsystems.com/resources/mentoring-early-career-professionals-a-beginners-guide)  
[PMC – Unlocking the power of virtual networking for early-career researchers (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10950328)  
[MentorcliQ – 40+ Definitive Mentorship Statistics and Research for 2025](https://www.mentorcliq.com/blog/mentoring-stats)

**The mentorship imperative:** **Adobe’s Future Workforce Study** found that **more than 80% of Gen Z want a mentor** as part of their development plan, but only **half have one**. **MentorcliQ research** shows that Millennial and Gen Z workers with a mentor are **21–23% more likely to report being satisfied with their current job**. The **Owl Labs report** notes that 51% of employers have introduced mentorship opportunities in response to hybrid/remote work, a positive trend.

### 4.4 The Return-to-Office Shift

The remote work landscape has been significantly reshaped by **return-to-office (RTO) mandates** that gained momentum in 2025. According to **Robert Half’s Q2 2026 analysis**, the number of fully in-office roles rose from **65% in Q4 2025 to 87% in Q2 2026**, with hybrid dropping to 10% and fully remote to just 3%. This represents a dramatic tightening of the market for flexible work.

**Pew Research Center** data shows that the number of workers required to be in the office regularly surged to **75% in late 2024**, up from 63% in early 2023. A **Cisco survey** supports this, with 72% of respondents saying their organizations have mandates for working in the office.

**Key examples of RTO mandates:**
- **President Trump’s executive order (January 20, 2025)** requiring federal agencies to end remote work (five days per week in office)
- Major tech companies (Google, Amazon, Meta, Apple, Disney, JPMorgan Chase) have enforced 3–5 day in-office requirements
- However, a **CNBC report (July 2025)** notes that despite widespread RTO mandates, more than one-third of employees still work from home, and **46% of workers say they would look for new work if their employer eliminated remote work** (Pew Research Center)

## 5. Conclusions and Implications

### 5.1 The Early-Career Disadvantage

The evidence is clear and converging: **remote work has created a structural disadvantage for early-career professionals**. Entry-level positions have the least access to flexible work arrangements, and when remote roles are available, employers systematically favor experienced candidates, demanding 25% more skills and credentials. The New York Fed’s finding that remote work accounts for 64% of the rise in youth unemployment among college graduates is a stark indicator of this trend.

### 5.2 The Hybrid Sweet Spot

The research suggests that **moderate hybrid arrangements (2–3 days in-office)** may offer the best balance for early-career professionals. Bloom’s Nature study shows that hybrid workers are just as likely to be promoted, while the Owl Labs data shows that hybrid workers report higher productivity. For Gen Z and new graduates, hybrid models provide the in-person mentorship and networking that are critical for career development while preserving flexibility.

### 5.3 Skills as the New Currency

The rapid evolution of skill demands—particularly the rise of AI literacy, adaptability, and communication—means that early-career professionals must invest in continuous learning. The 25% skill premium for remote roles creates a higher bar for entry, but also an opportunity for those who proactively develop in-demand competencies.

### 5.4 A Polarized Market

The remote work market is becoming increasingly polarized. Senior professionals retain significant leverage to negotiate flexibility (demand for experienced remote roles grew 19.8% year-over-year in Q4 2025, per Toptal), while entry-level workers face a tightening market. The RTO mandates of 2025–2026 have further reduced the availability of remote positions, but the underlying structural shift toward hybrid work appears durable.

### 5.5 Policy Implications

For employers, the evidence argues for **structured mentorship programs, skills-first hiring practices, and outcome-based promotion criteria** to ensure that remote work does not exacerbate inequality for early-career talent. For policymakers, the findings suggest that **flexible work regulations, investment in digital skills training, and support for remote-capable infrastructure** are essential to ensuring that the benefits of remote work are broadly shared.
