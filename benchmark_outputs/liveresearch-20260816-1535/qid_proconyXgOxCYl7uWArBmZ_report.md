

# Performance-Enhancing Drugs and Technologies in Elite Sports: A Comprehensive Global Analysis

## Introduction

The debate over whether performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs) and technologies should be permitted in elite sports is one of the most contentious issues in contemporary athletics. The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA), established in 1999, oversees a global prohibition regime, yet the prevalence of doping, the emergence of new technologies, and high-profile scandals continue to challenge the current system. This report presents six arguments in favor of allowing PEDs and technologies and six arguments against, each supported by credible evidence from academic research, official reports, media sources, and expert testimony.

## PRO ARGUMENTS

### Pro 1: Doping Is So Prevalent That Prohibition Only Disadvantage Clean Athletes and Fails to Achieve Its Goals

**Thesis:** The current anti-doping regime is fundamentally ineffective because doping is so widespread that bans primarily punish honest athletes while failing to stop those determined to cheat, making a regulated approach more pragmatic.

**Supporting Points:**

**1.1 Prevalence estimates far exceed detection rates.** Reliable studies consistently find that doping is far more common than the number of positive tests suggests. A 2024 study of U.S. elite athletes found that 6.5–9.2% reported using a banned substance or method in the past year, while only about 2% of samples return adverse analytical findings (Davoren et al., 2024). Among Dutch elite athletes, the 12-month prevalence was estimated at 12.5% (Van der Heijden et al., *Scientific Journal of Sport and Performance*). Other anonymous self-report studies estimate prevalence between 20% and 62% across different sports and nations (Hopker et al., 2024, *Drug Testing and Analysis*). WADA’s own Prevalence Working Group has acknowledged that the gap between actual doping and detection is “a long-standing issue” and that without a reliable baseline measure, it is impossible to assess whether anti-doping programs are effective (WADA, 2022).

**1.2 The cat-and-mouse game is unwinnable.** As doping science advances, detection methods perpetually lag behind. The Lance Armstrong case demonstrated that an athlete could evade detection for years while orchestrating “the most sophisticated, professionalized and successful doping program that sport has ever seen” (USADA, 2012). Armstrong never failed a drug test during his seven Tour de France victories. The 2016 McLaren Report revealed that Russia operated a state-sponsored doping program affecting over 1,000 athletes across 30 sports, with urine samples being swapped through a secret hole in the wall at the Sochi laboratory (McLaren, 2016). WADA’s annual budget of approximately $48.6 million (2023) is dwarfed by the resources available to sophisticated doping networks, and the agency has acknowledged that “compliance by all Signatories is still not ‘business-as-usual’” (WADA Annual Report, 2023).

**1.3 Bans create perverse incentives.** The prohibition model incentivizes athletes to use increasingly dangerous untested substances and methods to avoid detection. As the *Hastings Center* notes, “Athletes, caught in the sport arms race, would be pressed to take more and more drugs, in ever wilder combinations and at increasingly higher doses” — precisely because the ban forces doping underground. Bioethicist Julian Savulescu argues that legalization would allow “supervised use of steroids, growth hormone and blood doping, within physiological limits,” thereby improving safety (Savulescu, *Aeon*, 2016).

### Pro 2: PEDs Provide Legitimate Therapeutic Benefits for Injury Recovery and Athlete Health

**Thesis:** Many banned substances have proven medical benefits for injury recovery, pain management, and rehabilitation, and restricting their use deprives athletes of treatments that could extend careers and reduce long-term health damage.

**Supporting Points:**

**2.1 Anabolic steroids aid muscle repair and recovery.** Anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS) are used legitimately in medicine to treat muscle-wasting diseases, severe burns, and recovery from major surgery. Research has shown that AAS can improve the natural muscle repair process following injury and help attenuate muscle atrophy during immobilization (News-Medical, 2022). A review in *Physio-pedia* notes that “creatine supplementation has been proven not only to attenuate muscle atrophy during immobilization, but to stimulate muscle hypertrophy during rehabilitative strength training,” potentially decreasing recovery time for injured athletes. The University of Michigan Health Lab explains that some banned drugs — particularly those for recovery — “can accelerate the normal recovery process of athletes’ injured cells and help load up fuel stores quicker” (Michigan Medicine, 2021).

**2.2 Elite athletes face unique physiological demands.** The intense training and competition schedules of elite athletes place extraordinary stress on their bodies. PEDs can mitigate the damage caused by this stress. As the *Britannica* pro/con debate notes, “PEDs help athletes to recover from injuries and to endure the rigors of sports” (Pro 2). The argument extends beyond recovery: some beta-2 agonists, when used responsibly, can help athletes with exercise-induced asthma, and certain diuretics have legitimate medical uses. The Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) system exists precisely because some banned substances have valid medical indications, but critics argue the TUE process is overly burdensome, arbitrary, and inconsistently applied, creating a two-tier system where those with resources can more easily obtain exemptions.

**2.3 The distinction between therapy and enhancement is blurred.** Many of the same substances used for recovery also enhance performance, making the line between treatment and doping arbitrary. As Savulescu argues, “What matters is health and fitness to compete. Rather than testing for drugs, we should focus more on health and fitness to compete” (Savulescu, Foddy, & Clayton, *BMJ*, 2013). The current system forces athletes to choose between proper medical care and compliance with anti-doping rules, which is both unethical and medically irrational.

### Pro 3: Athletes Have a Right to Bodily Autonomy, and Paternalistic Bans Violate That Right

**Thesis:** Competent adult athletes should have the right to make their own decisions about what substances to put into their bodies, and the current prohibition regime is an unjustified paternalistic restriction on personal freedom.

**Supporting Points:**

**3.1 The liberal principle of autonomy supports athlete choice.** In liberal societies, competent adults are presumed to have the right to make decisions about their own bodies, even if those decisions involve risk. As philosopher J.S. Mill’s harm principle states, the only justification for restricting liberty is to prevent harm to others, not to prevent self-harm. Philosophers Brown (1984) and Tamburrini (2000) have argued that doping bans violate athletes’ autonomy and right to make independent decisions about matters pertaining to their bodies (PMC, 2022). The *Britannica* pro/con entry states: “Professional athletes are adults who deserve bodily autonomy, meaning the right to do with their own bodies as they choose. Banning PEDs, which are otherwise legal drugs, treats adult athletes like children” (Pro 3). The Enhanced Games, founded by Aron D'Souza, explicitly frames its model around bodily autonomy, arguing that athletes should be free to use FDA-approved substances under medical supervision (Verfassungsblog, 2025).

**3.2 The privacy intrusions of anti-doping are disproportionate.** The current anti-doping system requires athletes to report their whereabouts at all times, submit to random urine and blood tests, and maintain a biological passport. As the *Philosophical Disquisitions* blog notes, “a ban on doping would have to be enforced with a regime of year-round, randomised drug tests [that] would violate an athlete’s right to privacy.” The World Anti-Doping Code requires athletes to be available for testing 365 days a year, with no advance notice, and three missed tests in 12 months constitutes an anti-doping rule violation. Critics argue this represents an unprecedented level of surveillance that would be unacceptable in other contexts. Young athletes are particularly affected, as they “cannot fully comprehend the implications of taking a banned substance or providing a doping sample, particularly if coercion or parental pressure is involved” (PMC, 2022).

**3.3 The consent argument for anti-doping is flawed.** The claim that athletes voluntarily consent to anti-doping rules by choosing to participate in sport is undermined by the fact that there is no realistic alternative. As the World Players Association noted in its 2016 Statement of Concern, the governance of WADA is “conflicted regarding the enforcement of the WADA Code,” and athletes have little meaningful input into the rules that govern them. The choice is not between accepting or rejecting anti-doping rules, but between participating in elite sport or not — a choice that for most athletes is effectively no choice at all given the investment of their careers and lives.

### Pro 4: A Harm Reduction Model Would Improve Athlete Safety Compared to Prohibition

**Thesis:** Legalizing and medically supervising PED use would reduce the health risks to athletes by replacing the dangerous black market with regulated, monitored use — a proven approach from other areas of public health.

**Supporting Points:**

**4.1 Prohibition increases health risks.** When athletes are forced to obtain PEDs from underground sources, they face risks from contaminated or counterfeit substances, unknown dosages, and lack of medical oversight. The *Hastings Center* observes that “athletes often take drugs at multiples of the dosages that have been studied for their benefits and risks, and they take drugs in bizarre combinations” precisely because the ban prevents open medical supervision. The U.S. Anti-Doping Agency (USADA) warns that blood doping through transfusions increases the risk of infectious disease “such as HIV/AIDS or hepatitis” (USADA). Under a regulated model, as advocated by the Enhanced Games, “substances used by athletes during the Games include, for example, testosterone, erythropoietin, and anabolic steroids,” but they would be “legal under FDA rules” and “taken under medical supervision” (Verfassungsblog, 2025).

**4.2 Harm reduction has proven effective in other contexts.** The public health literature on supervised consumption sites for recreational drugs demonstrates that such approaches reduce overdose deaths, blood-borne infections, and public disorder without increasing drug use. A study on North America’s first medically supervised safe consumption site found that such facilities “reduce overall rates of drug use, and potentially promote an increase in addiction treatment” (Debeck et al., 2011). The *Lancet* study on Vancouver’s Insite facility found a 35% reduction in overdose deaths in the surrounding area (Marshall et al., 2011). While PEDs are not identical to recreational drugs, the same principle applies: prohibition drives use underground, while regulation allows for education, monitoring, and harm minimization. A 2025 article in *ScienceDirect* explicitly argues for “a medically supervised model in which harms are minimised by careful monitoring of biomarkers (e.g., blood counts) and physiological parameters” (*Harm Reduction in the Enhanced Games*).

**4.3 The current system fails to protect athlete health.** Despite WADA’s mandate to “protect the Athlete’s fundamental right to participate in doping-free sport and thus promote health, fairness and equality,” the evidence suggests that the system does not effectively protect health. Athletes who dope do so without medical guidance, often combining multiple substances with unknown interactions. Meanwhile, the intensive training and competition schedule that elite athletes endure — often involving 30+ hours per week of high-intensity exercise — is itself a significant health risk, yet this is not regulated. As Savulescu argues, the focus should be on “health and fitness to compete” rather than on drug testing per se. The current system creates an illusion of safety while actually making doping more dangerous.

(2011) *The Lancet*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21497898/; ScienceDirect on harm reduction: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211266925000246; Savulescu (2013) *BMJ*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149520/

### Pro 5: The Anti-Doping System Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent, While Many Allowed Technologies Provide Similar Advantages

**Thesis:** The distinction between prohibited drugs and permitted technologies is ethically incoherent, as many legal innovations — from high-tech swimsuits to carbon-fiber running shoes — provide performance advantages comparable to or greater than banned substances.

**Supporting Points:**

**5.1 Technology has revolutionized sports performance.** The use of full-body polyurethane swimsuits in 2008–2009 led to more than 130 world records being broken, with performance improvements of up to 5.5% (Foster et al., 2012). Nearly 94% of races won and 98% of records broken at the 2008 Beijing Olympics were by swimmers wearing the LZR Racer suit, which NASA helped design to reduce skin friction drag by 24% more than previous suits (NASA Spinoff, 2008). These suits were subsequently banned, but only after they had already transformed the sport. Similarly, Nike’s Vaporfly shoes with carbon-fiber plates and advanced foam have been shown to improve running economy by more than 4% (Muniz-Pardos et al., 2021, *PMC*). Since their introduction in 2016, virtually all endurance running world records from 5,000m to the marathon have been broken. Eliud Kipchoge’s sub-two-hour marathon in 2019 was achieved in prototype Nike shoes. These technologies are not prohibited, yet they provide advantages that dwarf many PEDs.

**5.2 Genetic advantages are already unequal.** The argument that PEDs create unfair inequality is undermined by the fact that natural genetic variation already produces enormous disparities between athletes. As the *Stanford Law School* study on regulating genetic advantage notes, “79% of respondents agreed or were indifferent that sports would be a fairer test if the biological playing field were more level,” suggesting that genetic modification is viewed by many as a tool to promote equality of opportunity rather than undermine it (Polcz, 2017). The ethical philosopher Claudio Tamburrini has asked “What’s wrong with genetic inequality?” arguing that the appeal to naturalness is incoherent given that sport already rewards genetic luck. The *Sport Journal* notes that “some researchers and members of sport ethics committees believe that new policies may actually encourage athletes to attempt gene doping” because the current system lacks clear ethical foundations.

**5.3 The line between natural and artificial is fuzzy.** Many so-called “natural” advantages — such as access to altitude training, specialized coaching, advanced nutrition, cryotherapy chambers, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy — are effectively unavailable to athletes from poorer nations or backgrounds. The claim that anti-doping creates a “level playing field” is a myth, as Paul Dimeo has noted: only a minority of athletes from affluent backgrounds have access to altitude training and specialist equipment. As the *Enhanced Games* legal analysis in *Sports Shorts* states, “fairness is a nebulous term in itself,” and the current system selectively prohibits certain enhancements while permitting others that are equally or more impactful.

### Pro 6: Legalization Would Eliminate the Black Market, Reduce Corruption, and Allow for Evidence-Based Regulation

**Thesis:** A regulated, legalized system would dismantle the illicit doping black market, remove the incentives for corruption that have plagued anti-doping enforcement, and enable evidence-based policies focused on safety rather than prohibition.

**Supporting Points:**

**6.1 The black market for PEDs is vast and dangerous.** The illegal market for performance-enhancing drugs is a multi-billion-dollar global industry operating outside any regulatory framework. Koch (2002) estimated that the legal performance-enhancing substance industry in the United States alone was worth $30 billion (cited in *Journal of Sport Behavior*). The black market exposes athletes to products of unknown quality, purity, and dosage. As the *New York State Department of Health* warns about anabolic steroids, “under both federal and New York State Law, anabolic steroids may only be prescribed by a physician” — yet athletes routinely obtain them from unregulated sources. Legalization would bring this market into the light, allowing for quality control, accurate dosing, and medical oversight.

**6.2 Corruption is endemic to the anti-doping system.** The Russian doping scandal revealed that the very institutions charged with enforcing anti-doping rules were complicit in the largest doping program in history. The McLaren Report documented that the Russian Ministry of Sport and the Federal Security Service (FSB) actively manipulated doping tests, swapping urine samples and covering up positives. The World Players Association has expressed concern that WADA’s governance is “conflicted regarding the enforcement of the WADA Code,” as 50% of its funding comes from the Olympic Movement and 50% from governments — both of which have vested interests in protecting reputations and avoiding scandals. In 2023, USADA issued a scathing critique of WADA’s failure to enforce its own rules, stating that “WADA’s failure to enforce these mandatory provisions to ensure transparency and harmonization of rule enforcement undermines the confidence and trust needed in a global regulator” (USADA, 2023).

**6.3 Resources could be redirected to education and harm reduction.** WADA’s budget of approximately $48.6 million (2023) and the billions spent globally on testing and enforcement could be redirected to evidence-based education, athlete health monitoring, and research into safer enhancement methods. The ASOIF 2023 Anti-Doping Survey found that International Federations spend significant portions of their budgets on testing and sample analysis, with relatively little on education. A 2025 meta-analysis found that anti-doping education programs are “effective in improving short-term doping intentions” and raising awareness (Reynoso-Sánchez et al., 2025, *PMC*). A regulated system would allow for a more rational allocation of resources, focusing on the most serious health risks rather than the current approach of banning hundreds of substances with varying degrees of evidence for harm or enhancement.

## CON ARGUMENTS

### Con 1: Performance-Enhancing Drugs Violate the Spirit and Integrity of Sport

**Thesis:** The use of PEDs fundamentally undermines the intrinsic values of sport — fair play, natural human excellence, and the celebration of dedication and effort — and legalization would destroy the very essence of athletic competition.

**Supporting Points:**

**1.1 The “spirit of sport” is a core ethical foundation.** WADA’s World Anti-Doping Code defines the spirit of sport as “the essence of Olympism, the pursuit of human excellence through the dedicated perfection of each person’s talents” and identifies it as “how we play true.” The Code lists core values including “Ethics, fair play and honesty; health; excellence in performance; character and education; fun and joy; teamwork; dedication and commitment; respect for rules and laws; respect for self and other participants; courage; community and solidarity” (WADA Code). Doping is antithetical to these values. As John William Devine, lecturer in sports ethics at Swansea University, argues, PEDs and smart technology “violate the spirit and integrity of sport” (Britannica Pro/Con). The UNESCO International Convention against Doping in Sport states that “doping jeopardizes the moral and ethical basis of sport and the health of those involved in it.”

**1.2 Sport is a meritocracy based on natural talent and effort.** The fundamental appeal of sport is that it tests the limits of human potential through natural ability, training, and dedication. When performance is chemically enhanced, the connection between effort and achievement is severed. As the Marquette University Law School Faculty Blog argues, “Sport is not about simply winning. The saying ‘It’s not about whether you win or lose, it’s how you played the game’… is absolutely correct.” The *Hastings Center* warns that if drugs were legalized, “sports that revere records and historical comparisons (think of baseball and home runs) would become unmoored by drug-aided athletes obliterating old standards.” The beauty of sport lies in the human drama of striving, struggling, and overcoming — elements that are diminished when the playing field is chemically altered.

**1.3 Public trust and the meaning of athletic achievement depend on clean sport.** Spectators, sponsors, and the broader public invest in sport with the understanding that what they are watching is a genuine test of human ability. The European Olympic Committee states that “doping undermines the fundamental values of sport, such as health, fairness, commitment, integrity, and equal opportunities.” A 2024 study on the impact of doping scandals found that “a doping incident reduces trust in athletes’ fairness” among spectators (ScienceDirect, 2025). While the same study found that doping did not necessarily reduce demand for watching events, the erosion of trust is a real and significant harm. The Olympic movement is built on the ideal of amateurism and fair play; legalizing doping would fundamentally alter the nature of the Olympic Games and what they represent to the world.

### Con 2: Performance-Enhancing Drugs Pose Serious and Often Irreversible Health Risks

**Thesis:** PEDs cause demonstrable and severe harm to athletes’ physical and mental health, including life-threatening cardiovascular events, organ damage, and psychological disorders, and legalization would lead to a public health crisis.

**Supporting Points:**

**2.1 Cardiovascular risks are well-documented and deadly.** The Mayo Clinic warns that performance-enhancing drugs “can have bad effects on health,” including increased risk of heart attack, stroke, and cardiac arrhythmia (Mayo Clinic, 2022). USADA states that blood doping “thickens the blood, making it difficult for the heart to pump,” resulting in “a higher risk of life-threatening diseases including stroke, heart disease, and cerebral or pulmonary embolisms.” The agency notes that “there have been multiple deaths attributed to blood doping” (USADA). In cycling, between 1987 and 1990, shortly after athletes began using erythropoietin (EPO), 20 young Belgian and Dutch cyclists died suddenly (*The Guardian*, 2015). Johannes Draaijer, a 27-year-old Dutch cyclist, died in his sleep of heart block in 1990, widely presumed to be caused by EPO use (Wikipedia). The *StatPearls* review on sudden death in athletes identifies “anabolic steroids and stimulants” as agents that “induce the risk of sudden cardiac death through multiple mechanisms” (NCBI Bookshelf).

**2.2 Long-term health consequences are severe.** Anabolic steroid use is associated with liver damage, infertility, testicular atrophy, masculinization in women, severe acne, and psychological effects including aggression, depression, and dependence. The Mayo Clinic notes that “anabolic steroids used by athletes are often forms of testosterone made in a lab” and that “doping for sports isn't one of the uses the drugs are approved for.” The New York State Department of Health warns that anabolic steroids can cause “potentially serious side effects” including “blood clotting, liver damage, and cardiovascular disease.” The *Hastings Center* argues that “the drug race in sport has the potential to create a slow-motion public health catastrophe” because athletes take drugs “at multiples of the dosages that have been studied for their benefits and risks” and in “bizarre combinations” that no research ethics committee would approve.

**2.3 The risks are amplified under a legalization scenario.** Critics of legalization argue that if doping were permitted, athletes would be compelled to use increasingly dangerous substances and dosages to remain competitive. As the *Britannica* con argument states, “despite any benefit in injury recovery, PEDs are dangerous and can still yield an unfair competitive advantage” (Con 2). The *Hastings Center* warns that “athletes, caught in the sport arms race, would be pressed to take more and more drugs, in ever wilder combinations and at increasingly higher doses.” The Enhanced Games model has been criticized precisely on these grounds: as noted in the *BBC Sport* documentary, experts warn that athletes in such events “could be exposing themselves to a greater chance of heart attacks and psychiatric issues” and that the long-term effects of the specific regimens used are “unknown” and potentially “irreversible.”

### Con 3: Legalizing Doping Would Create an Unfair Arms Race That Coerces All Athletes into Participating

**Thesis:** If doping were legalized, the competitive pressure would force all athletes — including those who wish to compete clean — to use PEDs, eliminating genuine choice and creating a coercive environment that harms everyone.

**Supporting Points:**

**3.1 The logic of the arms race is inescapable.** In a competitive environment, if some athletes use PEDs to gain an advantage, others must either follow suit or accept a competitive disadvantage. As the *Hastings Center* notes, “athletes, caught in the sport arms race, would be pressed to take more and more drugs.” The *Britannica* con argument states that “with doping legal, all competitive athletes would be compelled to use drugs, and the net effect would be a level playing field but with widespread health consequences.” This is not a hypothetical: the cycling world of the 1990s and 2000s demonstrated exactly this dynamic. As former cyclist Jan Ullrich, winner of the 1997 Tour de France, admitted in 2023, he had “entered a system” in which he believed it was “impossible to compete otherwise” without doping. Erik Zabel, a six-time Tour de France green jersey winner, admitted to using EPO, saying the substances were “EPO, cortisone, and later even blood doping. That’s really a lot.”

**3.2 Coercion undermines true autonomy.** The argument that legalization respects athlete autonomy is paradoxical: if athletes are forced to dope to remain competitive, their choice is not free. As the *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* article on safeguarding athletes argues, “paternalistic approaches often fail to promote genuine autonomy,” but neither does a system that coerces athletes into harmful practices. The research on doping and coercion shows that “a coercive environment can inhibit an athlete's autonomous choice to reject the use of performance-enhancing substances” (Miah, 2005). The concept of “clean anxiety” — the fear of inadvertent rule violations and reputational damage even among clean athletes — demonstrates that the current system creates its own pressures, but a legalized system would create a different and arguably worse form of coercion: the pressure to dope or lose.

**3.3 The Armstrong case illustrates the coercive power of doping networks.** The USADA investigation into Lance Armstrong revealed that “it was not enough that his teammates give maximum effort on the bike, he also required that they adhere to the doping program.” Armstrong pressured teammates into doping, and those who refused were marginalized or removed from the team. This was not a free choice; it was a coercive, systematic doping program enforced by team leadership. Under a legalized regime, such coercion could become institutionalized, with teams and sponsors demanding that athletes use specific enhancement protocols to maximize performance. The power imbalance between athletes and their entourages — coaches, team doctors, sponsors — would make truly voluntary consent difficult if not impossible.

### Con 4: Doping Undermines the Role Model Function of Sport and Harms Youth

**Thesis:** Elite athletes are role models for millions of young people, and legalizing doping would send the message that winning at all costs is acceptable, normalizing dangerous substance use among adolescents and amateur athletes.

**Supporting Points:**

**4.1 Youth exposure to PEDs is already concerning.** Studies show that adolescent athletes are using performance-enhancing drugs at alarming rates. Koch (2002) estimated that 8–15% of U.S. high school athletes use some form of performance-enhancing substance. The *Journal of Sport Behavior* notes that “evidence indicates that adolescents (athletes and non-athletes) use performance enhancing drugs,” and that multi-sport athletes and upperclassmen have higher probabilities of steroid use. The *Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System* data shows that steroid use is associated with motivations to change physical appearance and experimentation with other drugs. The expansion of PEDs into “local gyms and youth athletics” is a growing concern for healthcare providers, as “the specter of performance-enhancing drugs has found its way into local gyms and youth athletics” (University of Colorado Anschutz, 2023).

**4.2 Elite athletes influence youth behavior.** The behavior of elite athletes has a significant impact on young people who admire them. As Sport Integrity Canada states, “athletes are influential role models for young people. The behaviour of elite athletes can have significant impact on young people as they admire and aspire to emulate their sporting heroes.” Legalizing doping at the elite level would normalize the use of dangerous substances and send a powerful message that doping is an acceptable shortcut to success. The *Augusta Health* article on PEDs and teen athletes notes that “given that famous athletes have admitted to using performance-enhancing drugs, it's not surprising that teens might turn to steroids and other substances to boost their sports performance.”

**4.3 The public health implications are severe.** If doping were legalized in elite sport, the normalization would almost certainly lead to increased use among amateur and recreational athletes, with significant public health consequences. The risk of addiction, organ damage, and psychological harm would extend beyond the relatively small population of elite athletes to millions of young people. The *Hastings Center* warns that the drug race in sport “has the potential to create a slow-motion public health catastrophe.” The *International Journal of Drug Policy* review (2007) found that doping programs “are moving out of the professional sports market to our youth and other at-risk populations at alarming rates,” and that unethical pharmacists and medical professionals are “working secretly… to develop sophisticated doping programs where performance is optimized, often at the risk of the athletes' health.” Legalization would accelerate this trend.

### Con 5: Anti-Doping Programs, While Imperfect, Have Deterrent Value and Protect Clean Athletes

**Thesis:** Despite its flaws, the anti-doping system provides a crucial deterrent effect that reduces the prevalence of doping, protects the rights of clean athletes, and maintains a level of fairness that would be impossible without any regulation.

**Supporting Points:**

**5.1 Deterrence works, even if imperfectly.** The existence of testing and sanctions deters many athletes from doping. A 2024 study of U.S. elite athletes found that the “actual positivity rate from testing” was close to the self-reported prevalence rate for the most egregious substances (anabolic steroids, peptide hormones, and blood manipulation), which was only 2.2% (Davoren et al., 2024). This suggests that testing effectively detects and deters the most serious forms of doping. The same study found that the overall prevalence of any doping was 6.5–9.2%, which — while significant — is far lower than the 20–62% reported in some anonymous surveys, suggesting that the deterrent effect of the current system is meaningful. NORC at the University of Chicago, which partnered on the study, concluded that “this rate is close to the actual reported positivity rate from testing, suggesting testing effectively detects and deters doping” (NORC, 2024).

**5.2 Education programs are showing positive results.** A 2025 meta-analysis of anti-doping education programs found that they “effectively improve short-term doping intentions (ES = 0.29, p < 0.001) and anti-doping attitudes” (Reynoso-Sánchez et al., 2025, *PMC*). A 2025 systematic review found that “embedding anti-doping education within the university curriculum can reduce doping behaviors, improve athletes' health, and promote clean sports” (Nicholls et al., 2025, *ScienceDirect*). These programs demonstrate that education and prevention — which are part of the anti-doping framework — can be effective, and their value would be lost under a legalization regime that would necessarily reduce the stigma associated with doping.

**5.3 The system provides a framework for fairness and accountability.** While the anti-doping system is imperfect, it provides a rules-based framework within which athletes can compete with confidence that their competitors are not using certain banned substances. As the *Australian National Anti-Doping Policy* states, the purpose of anti-doping is “to protect the Athlete’s fundamental right to participate in doping-free sport and thus promote health, fairness and equality.” The WADA Prohibited List, updated annually following extensive consultation, provides a clear standard. The Athlete Biological Passport and longitudinal monitoring provide increasingly sophisticated tools for detecting manipulation. The *International Sports Law Journal* argues that “protecting the sanctity of procedural rights not only safeguards athletes but also upholds the legitimacy of the anti-doping framework.” Without any system, athletes would have no recourse against competitors who use dangerous or performance-enhancing substances, and the “wild west” scenario would be far worse than the current system.

### Con 6: Legalization Would Exacerbate Inequality Between Rich and Poor Athletes and Nations

**Thesis:** A legalized doping regime would disproportionately benefit wealthy athletes and nations with access to the best medical supervision, advanced enhancement technologies, and state-of-the-art protocols, widening the gap between rich and poor in global sport.

**Supporting Points:**

**6.1 Access to medical supervision is unequal.** Under a legalized regime, the quality of medical supervision, the sophistication of enhancement protocols, and the ability to monitor and manage side effects would vary dramatically based on resources. Wealthy athletes and teams from affluent nations could afford top-tier medical staff, advanced monitoring technology, and the most effective and safest enhancement protocols. Athletes from poorer nations would be left with inferior options, replicating the existing inequalities in sports medicine, training facilities, and coaching. As the *Sports Shorts* analysis of the Enhanced Games notes, “only a minority of athletes from affluent backgrounds and nations have access to resources such as altitude training and specialist equipment, thereby debunking the notion of a ‘level playing field’” (Dimeo, cited in *Sports Shorts*, 2025). Legalizing doping would add another dimension to this inequality.

**6.2 Genetic enhancement would create a new class divide.** Gene doping and genetic modification technologies, once developed, are likely to be expensive and accessible only to the wealthy. The *Stanford Law School* study on regulating genetic advantage notes that this raises concerns about “distributive justice” and whether a system that allows genetic modification would be fair across different socioeconomic groups. The *Sport Journal* warns that “the new class of humans that would result from germ line enhancement would interact with unenhanced humans in sport, thereby creating a potential disadvantage for the unenhanced.” This is not a distant future scenario: WADA has already banned gene doping preemptively because of the potential for precisely this kind of inequality.

**6.3 The Enhanced Games model illustrates the problem.** The Enhanced Games, proposed by Aron D'Souza and backed by venture capitalist Peter Thiel, explicitly markets itself as a high-tech, data-driven alternative to the Olympics. The event’s reliance on FDA-approved drugs under medical supervision inherently favors athletes from countries with access to sophisticated healthcare systems and legal pharmaceutical markets. The *NYT* article on the Enhanced Games notes that the event is designed to “usher in an era of ‘superhumans’” — but only for those who can afford it. The *Conversation* critique of the Enhanced Games argues that the event “neutralizes critics by appropriating the values espoused by his detractors” while actually serving “to conquer new markets.” The *Outside Online* article calls it “the dangerous promise of the pro-doping ‘Enhanced Games,’” highlighting that the event would deepen existing inequalities in sport.

**6.4 The current system, while imperfect, provides some leveling.** The WADA Code applies equally to all athletes, regardless of nationality or wealth. While enforcement is uneven, the principle of universal prohibition provides a baseline that prevents the most extreme forms of enhancement-based inequality. As the *Sports Medicine* review on legalization argues, “extremely complicated international regulations would have to be adopted” under a legalized system, and “audience mistrust, particularly toward athletes who achieved outstanding feats, would remain because it would still be possible that these athletes were reliant on illegal doping practices” (Kayser et al., 2007). The current system, for all its flaws, at least provides a common standard.

## Conclusion

The debate over whether performance-enhancing drugs and technologies should be allowed in elite sports is fundamentally a clash between competing values: individual autonomy and harm reduction versus the integrity of sport and the protection of athlete health. Both sides present compelling arguments supported by evidence. The pro-legalization perspective highlights the failures of the current prohibition regime, the prevalence of doping, the potential for harm reduction, and the value of respecting athlete autonomy. The anti-legalization perspective emphasizes the risks to athlete health, the coercive dynamics of an arms race, the importance of role models, and the need to preserve the fundamental values of sport.

What is clear is that the current system is not working as intended. Doping remains widespread, enforcement is inconsistent, and the ethical foundations of the prohibition are contested. Any reform — whether toward stricter enforcement or toward regulated legalization — must be grounded in evidence, respect for athlete welfare, and a clear-eyed assessment of the trade-offs involved. The emergence of the Enhanced Games and the growing willingness of scholars and commentators to question the anti-doping orthodoxy suggest that this debate will only intensify in the coming years.
