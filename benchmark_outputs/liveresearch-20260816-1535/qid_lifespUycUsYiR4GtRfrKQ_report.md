

# Chronic Ankle Instability in Recreational and Collegiate Basketball Players (Ages 16–30): A Comprehensive Evidence Review

## 1. Introduction

Lateral ankle sprains (LAS) are the most common injury in basketball, accounting for approximately 15–17% of all basketball injuries. The recurrence rate exceeds 40%, and a substantial proportion of athletes progress to chronic ankle instability (CAI). This review synthesizes the international literature from 2020–2026 on biomechanical and neuromuscular risk factors, rehabilitation versus surgical outcomes, return-to-play (RTP) criteria, and prevention protocols, with a focus on recreational and collegiate basketball players aged 16–30.

---

## 2. Biomechanical and Neuromuscular Factors Most Implicated in CAI

### 2.1 Peroneal Muscle Weakness and Evertor Deficits

Peroneal muscle weakness is one of the most consistently cited neuromuscular deficits in CAI. The peroneals are the primary evertors of the ankle and function as dynamic stabilizers against injurious inversion moments. Research demonstrates that both concentric and, more specifically, eccentric evertor weakness is present in individuals with CAI compared to healthy controls and copers. Isokinetic dynamometry studies confirm that deficits in peroneal strength reduce the ability to resist inversion and return the foot to neutral, thereby increasing reinjury risk (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC164382; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1268773117313309).

### 2.2 Proprioceptive Deficits

Proprioceptive impairment—defined as deficits in joint position sense (JPS) and kinesthesia—is a hallmark of CAI. Freeman et al. first proposed that mechanoreceptors in torn ligaments are disrupted, leading to impaired afferent signaling. A 2023 systematic review of 13 studies (347 patients) found that even after surgical restabilization, residual proprioceptive deficits persist compared to uninjured controls, suggesting that surgical retensioning alone may not fully restore degenerated mechanoreceptors (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10776890). Proprioceptive deficits are associated with inferior postural control during unplanned landing, further compounding injury risk (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2419217).

### 2.3 Delayed Peroneal Reaction Time and Neuromuscular Control

Patients with CAI exhibit delayed peroneal muscle reaction time in response to sudden inversion perturbations. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that exercise interventions—including balance training, neuromuscular training, and combined eccentric-concentric isokinetic exercise—significantly reduce peroneal reaction time, whereas external ankle supports alone do not (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590093521000266). This delay in reflexive activation increases the window of vulnerability to inversion injury.

### 2.4 Altered Landing Biomechanics

Landing mechanics are profoundly altered in athletes with CAI. Key findings from basketball-specific studies include:

- **Greater hip flexion and ankle eversion at initial landing**: CAI athletes adopt a "hip-dominant" strategy to avoid ankle inversion, suggesting compensatory neuromuscular reorganization (https://www.jssm.org/jssm-18-653.xml%3EFulltext).
- **Decreased peroneus longus activation**: During the pre-landing and descending phases of jump landing, CAI athletes show significantly lower peroneus longus EMG activity compared to healthy controls, which is associated with higher ankle inversion velocity and increased reinjury risk (https://www.jssm.org/jssm-18-653.xml%3EFulltext).
- **Altered three-step layup biomechanics**: A 2023 study of basketball players with CAI found that during the one-foot push-off phase, the maximum ankle inversion angle was significantly smaller (11.82° vs 16.52°), whereas during the one-foot landing phase, the maximum inversion angle was significantly larger (18.15° vs 9.98°) and the maximum dorsiflexion angle was also greater (11.32° vs 7.28°), with a prolonged peak time for ankle dorsiflexion moment (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618240). These findings indicate altered movement strategies that may increase the risk of recurrent sprains.

### 2.5 Cavovarus Alignment as an Anatomical Risk Factor

The subtle cavovarus foot deformity is increasingly recognized as a significant anatomical risk factor for recurrent LAS and progression to CAI. The cavovarus foot—characterized by a high arch, hindfoot varus, and plantarflexed first ray—places the lateral ankle ligaments under chronic tension and predisposes to inversion injury. The only agreed-upon protective intervention in the literature is the prescription of semi-rigid orthoses for basketball players with a history of ankle sprain, likely due to improved proprioception (https://www.clinmedjournals.org/articles/ijfa/international-journal-of-foot-and-ankle-ijfa-4-048.php). Surgical correction of cavovarus deformity (e.g., calcaneal osteotomy, first metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy) combined with modified Broström procedure (MBP) significantly improves clinical outcomes and prevents recurrent instability (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7329740). Failure to recognize and address cavovarus alignment is predictive of operative failure.

### 2.6 Talar Tilt and Anterior Drawer Laxity (Mechanical Instability)

Mechanical instability—defined as pathologic ligamentous laxity—occurs in approximately 30% of patients after initial LAS. Manual stress testing (anterior drawer and talar tilt) remains the cornerstone of clinical assessment. Stress radiography and arthrometry provide objective quantification. Importantly, the 2022 systematic review on proprioception after surgical restabilization found that despite significant improvements in mechanical stability, residual functional deficits often persist, highlighting that mechanical stability alone is insufficient for full recovery (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10776890).

### 2.7 Sensorimotor Control Deficits at Spinal and Supraspinal Levels

CAI involves deficits across the entire sensorimotor continuum, including altered spinal reflex excitability, impaired feedforward and feedback motor control, and central nervous system reorganization. Resting-state fMRI studies have identified altered cerebral inherent activity in sensorimotor networks among CAI patients, suggesting that the condition has a neurophysiological component beyond local joint pathology (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35197822).

## 3. Documentation Across the International Literature

### 3.1 Prevalence and Risk Factor Studies

A 2022 cross-sectional study in *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation* found that the prevalence of CAI among basketball athletes is substantial, with many athletes continuing to play despite persistent symptoms (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-022-00418-0). Koshino et al. (2020) reported prevalence and characteristics of CAI and copers in collegiate athletes using standardized criteria (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-022-00418-0). Kobayashi et al. (2020) linked CAI to foot arch characteristics in female collegiate athletes, reinforcing the role of structural foot type (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-022-00418-0).

### 3.2 Case Reports and Case Series

- **Case report of a 14-year-old African American basketball player**: A 2023 case report from Texas detailed a young athlete with recurrent LAS who developed CAI, highlighting the importance of early identification and comprehensive rehabilitation in adolescent populations (https://asu-ir.tdl.org/bitstreams/2ff2898f-c0de-4b4b-be83-1ffe0bcf7164/download).
- **Case series on cavovarus correction**: A 2020 case series of 15 patients undergoing MBP with concurrent cavovarus correction (calcaneal osteotomy, first metatarsal osteotomy) demonstrated significant improvements in VAS, AOFAS, and Karlsson-Peterson scores at mean follow-up of 58.5 months, with no recurrence of instability (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7329740).
- **Case series on Broström repair in elite athletes**: A Korean study of 18 elite athletes (including 2 basketball players) who underwent MBO reported 100% return to sport at 8 months, with 83.3% returning by 4 months (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6389534).

### 3.3 Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (2020–2025)

- **Proprioception after surgical restabilization** (2023): Systematic review of 13 studies, 347 patients—concluded that residual proprioceptive deficits persist after surgery despite improved mechanical stability (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10776890).
- **Exercise-based rehabilitation reduces reinjury** (2022): Meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (2182 participants) found that exercise-based rehabilitation reduced reinjury prevalence at 12 months (OR 0.60; 95% CI 0.36–0.99) compared to usual care (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8824326).
- **Balance training vs strength training** (2021): Systematic review of 15 RCTs (457 volunteers)—balance training was more effective for functionality outcomes, but both approaches improved instability and dynamic balance similarly (https://www.ovid.com/journals/clrb/pdf/10.1177/02692155211022009).
- **Acute ankle sprain management umbrella review** (2022): Strong evidence for NSAIDs and early mobilization for acute treatment; strong evidence for bracing and moderate evidence for neuromuscular training in preventing recurrence (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2022.868474/full).
- **Rehabilitation strategies do not reflect reinjury mechanisms** (2023): Systematic review found that current rehabilitation protocols for LAS often fail to address established mechanisms of reinjury, such as peroneal reaction time and landing biomechanics (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36716507).

## 4. Comparative Outcomes: Rehabilitation Programs vs. Surgical Stabilization

### 4.1 Conservative Rehabilitation Programs

#### Balance and Proprioceptive Training
Balance training is the most well-supported conservative intervention for CAI. A 2022 meta-analysis reported that proprioceptive training reduces ankle sprain risk by 35% (RR 0.65) in athletic populations, with an NNT of 17 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5737043). In basketball-specific studies, McGuine and Keene's landmark RCT (n=765) demonstrated a 38% reduction in ankle sprains among high school basketball players who completed a 4-week balance training program (https://pt-cpr.com/2022/05/balance-training-to-help-prevent-lateral-ankle-sprains). A 2024 RCT found that both strength training and balance training similarly improve dynamic balance, but strength training is more effective for improving joint position sense and vibration sense (https://www.mdpi.com/1930-8264/114/3/23008).

#### Neuromuscular and Perturbation Training
Neuromuscular training (NMT) that incorporates perturbation—sudden, unexpected disturbances to balance—has shown promise in improving sensorimotor control. A 2025 study found that CAI patients exhibit distinct patterns of neuromuscular adaptation during repeated perturbation trials, with significant reductions in ankle invertor torque over sessions, suggesting that perturbation-based training can improve postural control responses (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12984-025-01838-y). A 2024 RCT demonstrated that combining neuromuscular training with conventional training yielded greater benefits for tibialis anterior muscle morphology, posteromedial dynamic balance, and functional capacity than conventional training alone (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11569680). A 2022 meta-analysis of exercise types for CAI found that strength training is most effective for improving SEBT posterolateral direction, while proprioceptive training is most effective for the posteromedial direction (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-95896-w).

#### Peroneal Strengthening
Peroneal strengthening is a critical component of any CAI rehabilitation program. Exercises progressing from elastic band resistance to standing toe raises (which increase peroneal activation) are recommended. Isokinetic dynamometry should be used where available to assess and guide rehabilitation, as identified deficits in eversion-to-inversion strength ratios are strong predictors of reinjury risk (https://theprehabguys.com/ankle-sprain-prehab; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC164373).

#### Bracing and Taping
External ankle supports are effective for secondary prevention in athletes with a history of sprain. A 2024 narrative review found that bracing reduces ankle sprain risk by approximately 69% and taping by 71% in previously injured athletes, with no clear superiority of one over the other (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12942068). Zwiers et al. (2016) concluded that both taping and bracing have a preventive effect with minimal impact on sport-specific performance, and that athlete preference should guide selection (https://www.jisakos.com/article/S2059-7754(21)00176-0/fulltext). However, tape loses approximately 70% of its support within 25 minutes of activity, whereas lace-up braces provide more sustained protection (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12942068). A three-arm RCT (Janssen et al., 2014) found bracing superior to neuromuscular training for the prevention of self-reported recurrent ankle sprains (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6602401).

#### Conservative Rehabilitation Timeline and RTP
For acute LAS managed conservatively, the **PAASS framework** (Pain, Ankle impairments, Athlete perception, Sensorimotor control, Sport/functional performance)—developed through international multidisciplinary consensus and published in *BJSM* (2021)—provides guidance on RTP decision-making. The consensus identified 16 assessment items across five domains that should be evaluated before clearing an athlete for return to sport (https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/22/1270). However, specific cut-off thresholds have not been definitively established.

### 4.2 Surgical Stabilization

#### Modified Broström-Gould Procedure (MBP/MBO)
The modified Broström-Gould procedure (MBP) is the gold standard surgical treatment for CAI that has failed a minimum of 3–6 months of conservative management. Outcomes in elite athletes are excellent:
- **83.3% return to preinjury level by 4 months; 100% by 8 months** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6389534).
- Mean time to personal training: 1.9 months; team training: 2.9 months; first official game: 3.9 months.
- No significant differences in RTP based on age, sex, BMI, grade of instability, or presence of os subfibulare.
- Generalized joint laxity (Beighton score >4) may delay RTP.

A 2025 study of 62 patients with 3-year follow-up after MBP found that 82.3% returned to preinjury sports activity. Predictors of failure to return included residual pain, recurrence of mechanical instability, and insufficient recovery of dynamic postural control (https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/14/17/6046).

#### Arthroscopic vs. Open Broström Repair
A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies found:
- **Total complication rate**: 21.3% open vs 10.0% arthroscopic (not statistically significant, OR 0.73, p=0.34).
- **Wound infection rate**: 9.2% open vs 1.5% arthroscopic (statistically significant, OR 4.32, p=0.02).
- **Nerve complication rate**: 6.2% open vs 6.0% arthroscopic (not significant).
- Arthroscopic repairs offer comparable clinical outcomes with lower wound complication rates and faster return to activity (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8299886).

A 2023 study by Wang et al. confirmed that arthroscopic Broström-Gould repair has predictable mid-term outcomes and is a safe, effective alternative to open repair (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/surgery/articles/10.3389/fsurg.2023.1181493/full). However, a 2025 study by Rajnish et al. reported that open Broström-Gould repair may yield better functional outcomes and reduce knot-related discomfort (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41541026). The choice between open and arthroscopic approaches should be individualized based on surgeon expertise, patient anatomy, and concomitant pathology.

#### Internal Brace (Suture Tape) Augmentation
Internal Brace (IB) augmentation—where a collagen-coated FiberTape is anchored to reinforce the ligament repair—has emerged as a promising adjunct:

- **Biomechanical superiority**: IB constructs fail at 46.9° rotary displacement vs 24.1° for Broström alone, with torque at failure of 11.2 Nm vs 5.7 Nm (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11697995).
- **Accelerated RTP**: A multicenter RCT of 119 patients found that IB augmentation enabled return to preinjury activity at 13.3 weeks vs 17.5 weeks for traditional MBP (p=0.001). At 26 weeks, only 3.6% of IB patients had failed to return to activity vs 12.5% in the standard repair group (https://catalystorthoidaho.com/internalbrace-ankle-recovery-timeline; https://www.aaos.org/aaosnow/2026/mar/clinical/clinical06).
- **Clinical outcomes**: At minimum 2-year follow-up, no significant differences in FAAM, SF-12, or Tegner scores between IB augmentation and Broström alone, but IB allowed earlier full weight-bearing and accelerated rehabilitation (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9660380).
- **Rehabilitation protocol**: A 12-week accelerated protocol is now used, with Phase 1 (days 0–5) non-weight-bearing in splint, Phase 2 (days 5–21) transition to CAM boot with AROM, Phase 3 (weeks 3–4) ASO brace with resisted strengthening, Phase 4 (weeks 4–6) single-leg exercises, Phase 5 (weeks 6–8) plyometrics and jogging, Phase 6 (weeks 8–12) sport-specific training. Return to sport is possible as early as 8–10 weeks (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11697995).

#### Summary of Surgical Outcomes

| Procedure | RTP Timeline | Recurrence Rate | Key Advantages |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Modified Broström-Gould (open) | 4–8 months | Low (0–5%) | Gold standard, durable |
| Arthroscopic Broström | 3–6 months | Comparable to open | Lower wound complications, faster recovery |
| Broström + Internal Brace | 8–13 weeks | Very low (<4%) | Faster RTP, biomechanically stronger |

## 5. Return-to-Play Criteria and Prevention Protocols

### 5.1 Functional Testing Thresholds

There is currently no universally accepted set of objective RTP criteria for LAS or CAI, though significant progress has been made. The PAASS framework (2021) recommends assessment across five domains:

1. **Pain**: No pain during sport-specific activities.
2. **Ankle impairments**: Full range of motion (dorsiflexion >35–38° on lunge test), no effusion.
3. **Athlete perception**: Patient-reported outcome measures (FAAM, CAIT) and psychological readiness (ALR-RSI scale).
4. **Sensorimotor control**: Single-leg stance (eyes open/closed), Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT), Y-Balance Test.
5. **Sport/functional performance**: Single-leg hop test (>90% limb symmetry index [LSI]), figure-of-8 hop test, side-hop test, and sport-specific agility drills.

Specific functional testing thresholds proposed in the literature include:
- **FAAM-ADL >95% and FAAM-Sport >85–90%** (Hertel and Corbett, 2019) (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.902886/full).
- **Single-leg hop LSI ≥90%** (commonly used, though some research questions whether this threshold alone can differentiate safe vs unsafe RTP).
- **Knee-to-wall test within 2 cm of the uninjured side** (https://thebasketballdoctors.com/ankle-sprain-return-to-sport-tests).
- **Single-leg calf raise to failure ≥25–30 repetitions** as a threshold for sufficient plantarflexion strength (https://www.explorationpub.com/Journals/emd/Article/100736).
- **ALR-RSI score ≥83** as an optimal threshold for predicting return to preinjury sport level after ankle ligament reconstruction (https://hal.science/hal-04888936v1/document).

### 5.2 Psychological Readiness

The Ankle Ligament Reconstruction-Return to Sport after Injury (ALR-RSI) scale is a validated, reproducible tool for assessing psychological readiness. Patients who return to the same preinjury level score a mean of 72.9 ± 19.0, while those who return to a lower level score 48.5 ± 24.0 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38372173). A threshold of 83 has been proposed as optimal for predicting successful RTS (https://hal.science/hal-04888936v1/document). The short version of the ALR-RSI scale has also been validated (2025) (https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jeo2.70160).

### 5.3 Prevention Protocols

#### External Supports
- **Bracing**: Reduces ankle sprain incidence by 40–50% in basketball players with prior sprain history. Lace-up braces are preferred and recommended for a minimum of 6 months to 1 year following an ankle sprain. The Ultra Zoom hinged-cuff brace is specifically designed for basketball players with ≥4 sprains (https://www.ultraankle.com/sports/basketball-ankle-braces).
- **Taping**: Effective for approximately 45 minutes after application. Provides proprioceptive enhancement through cutaneous mechanoreceptor stimulation. Cost-effective for short-duration activity but must be reapplied.
- **Bracing vs taping**: Braces are equivalent or superior to taping at approximately one-third the cost over a season, with more consistent mechanical support (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12942068).

#### Exercise-Based Prevention Programs
- **Balance training programs**: A 4-week progressive balance program (5 days/week) reduced ankle sprains by 38% in high school basketball players (McGuine and Keene, 2006).
- **Neuromuscular training programs (NMTPs)**: The FIFA 11+ program, originally designed for soccer, has been shown to reduce ankle injuries in basketball. Effective NMTPs incorporate strength, plyometrics, balance, and sport-specific agility.
- **Maintenance throughout the season**: Prevention exercises should be maintained during the season and off-season, as they help maintain strength and improve proprioception, reducing recurrence risk (https://www.jscimedcentral.com/journal-article-info/JSM-Foot-and-Ankle/Treating-Lateral-Ankle-Sprain-in-Basketball-Players-A-physiotherapists-Prospective-8584).

#### Workload and Load Management
- **Training load monitoring**: Sharp spikes in court time, jump volume, sprint exposure, and tournament density increase injury risk. Gradual progression of activity over 4–6 weeks is recommended for returning athletes (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8077287).
- **Fatigue monitoring**: Game time and level of fatigue should be monitored, as fatigue impairs neuromuscular control and increases landing errors.
- **Rebounding**: The most common mechanism of ankle injury in basketball (34.4% of all ankle injuries), followed by general play, defending, and shooting. Training should emphasize landing mechanics during rebounding drills (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8077287).

#### Comprehensive Prevention Framework
The most effective prevention approach combines:
1. **Pre-season screening**: Identification of CAI, cavovarus foot type, and peroneal strength deficits.
2. **Structured warm-up**: Dynamic stretching, balance drills, and landing technique.
3. **In-season maintenance**: 2–3 sessions/week of balance and strengthening exercises.
4. **External support**: Bracing or taping for all athletes with a history of LAS.
5. **Load management**: Gradual return to full activity, monitoring of jump volume and fatigue.
6. **Footwear**: Well-fitting, supportive basketball shoes; avoid shoes with air cells in the heel, which are associated with 4× higher risk of ankle injury (https://www.ucsfbenioffchildrens.org/education/preventing-ankle-injuries-in-young-athletes).

## 6. Summary of Key Evidence-Based Recommendations for Basketball Players Aged 16–30 with Recurrent LAS and CAI

1. **Identify risk factors early**: Screen for cavovarus foot alignment (peek-a-boo heel sign), peroneal weakness, and proprioceptive deficits. Use the CAIT and FAAM for standardized assessment.

2. **Conservative rehabilitation as first-line treatment**: A minimum of 3–6 months of structured rehabilitation incorporating balance/proprioceptive training, peroneal strengthening, and neuromuscular/perturbation training is indicated before considering surgery.

3. **Use external supports for prevention**: Brace or tape all athletes with a history of LAS during practices and games. Lace-up braces are cost-effective and provide sustained protection.

4. **Consider surgical stabilization for refractory cases**: Modified Broström-Gould with or without Internal Brace augmentation is highly effective. Internal Brace augmentation allows accelerated RTP (8–13 weeks vs 16–24 weeks) and should be strongly considered in competitive basketball players.

5. **Address cavovarus deformity**: If concurrent cavovarus alignment is identified, consider surgical correction at the time of ligament repair to prevent recurrence.

6. **Use objective RTP criteria**: The PAASS framework provides a structured approach. Functional testing should include SEBT, single-leg hop for distance (LSI ≥90%), and ALR-RSI psychological readiness assessment (threshold ≥83).

7. **Implement comprehensive prevention programs**: Combine balance training, neuromuscular training, bracing, workload management, and proper footwear. Maintenance exercises should continue throughout the season and off-season.

8. **Monitor for residual deficits**: Even after successful surgical restabilization, proprioceptive and sensorimotor deficits may persist. Incorporate targeted sensorimotor training in the post-surgical rehabilitation protocol.
