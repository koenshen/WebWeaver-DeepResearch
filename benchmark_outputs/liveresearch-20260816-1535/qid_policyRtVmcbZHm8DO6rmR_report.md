
# Navigating the 2026 ESG Reporting Landscape: SEC, CSRD, and California SB-219

## Executive Summary

The global ESG reporting environment has reached an inflection point. As of mid-2026, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has proposed rescinding its 2024 climate disclosure rule entirely, the European Union’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) has been substantially narrowed by the Omnibus I package, and California’s SB-219 (consolidating SB 253 and SB 261) is moving forward with firm deadlines and active rulemaking. Multinational enterprises face a fragmented, three-tiered regulatory landscape that demands strategic coordination, not piecemeal compliance. This report provides a detailed comparison of each framework across reporting timelines, verification standards, materiality thresholds, and enforcement mechanisms, together with cost estimates from government and industry analyses.

## 1. Framework Descriptions

### SEC Climate Disclosure Rule (Proposed Rescission)

Originally adopted in March 2024, the SEC’s Enhancement and Standardization of Climate-Related Disclosures for Investors would have required publicly traded companies to disclose material climate-related risks, governance processes, and—for large accelerated and accelerated filers—material Scope 1 and 2 greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. The rule was immediately stayed and, after the SEC voted to end its legal defense in March 2025, the Commission formally proposed full rescission on May 29, 2026 (File No. S7-2026-19). The public comment period closed August 3, 2026. The rule has never been in effect.

### EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)

The CSRD, effective from January 2024, establishes mandatory sustainability reporting for EU and non-EU companies with significant EU operations. Reporting follows the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), which are built on the principle of **double materiality**—companies must disclose both how sustainability issues affect their financial performance (financial materiality) and how their operations impact people and the environment (impact materiality). The Omnibus I Directive (EU 2026/470), published February 26, 2026, and effective March 18, 2026, significantly narrowed the scope: companies with more than 1,000 employees and €450 million net turnover are now in scope, reducing the estimated number of covered entities by ~80%. Limited assurance is required, with a future move to reasonable assurance.

### California SB-219 (Consolidating SB 253 and SB 261)

California’s Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act (SB 253) and the Climate-Related Financial Risk Act (SB 261), as amended by SB 219, create a two-pronged mandatory disclosure regime for U.S. companies doing business in California. **SB 253** applies to companies with >$1 billion in annual revenue and requires annual disclosure of Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions (starting 2026, with Scope 3 in 2027), subject to third-party assurance. **SB 261** applies to companies with >$500 million in revenue and requires biennial climate-related financial risk reports aligned with the TCFD framework. The California Air Resources Board (CARB) approved initial implementing regulations on February 26, 2026. SB 261 enforcement is currently stayed by a Ninth Circuit injunction.

## 3. Enterprise Compliance Challenges and Cost Estimates

### SEC Climate Disclosure Rule

**Compliance challenges** (if the rule had been implemented):
- Determining materiality of climate risks and emissions without bright-line guidance.
- Collecting Scope 1 and 2 data across global operations for the first time.
- Implementing disclosure controls and procedures (DCPs) for climate data.
- Tagging disclosures in Inline XBRL.
- Attestation readiness: securing qualified assurance providers.

**Cost estimates** (from the SEC’s 2024 adopting release and 2026 rescission proposal):
- SEC estimated initial compliance costs for the full rule at **$6.37 billion** economy-wide in the first year, with recurring annual costs thereafter.
- Per-company estimates: $327,000 first year / $183,000 annual for governance and strategy disclosures; $151,000 first year / $67,000 annual for Scope 1&2 emissions reporting; $12,000 first year / $6,000 annual for scenario analysis.
- The SEC’s 2026 rescission proposal estimates **annualized cost savings of ~$4.9 billion per year** across all affected registrants over a 10-year horizon.
- Per-company savings from rescission range from $103,483 (targets and goals) to $526,850 (financial statement effects) in initial costs, and $56,916 (attestation) to $395,138 (financial statement effects) in annual costs.

### EU CSRD (Post-Omnibus I)

**Compliance challenges:**
- Conducting a robust double materiality assessment (DMA) with stakeholder engagement—the critical first step that determines which ESRS data points are required.
- Collecting data across the full value chain (Scope 3, supply chain social and environmental impacts).
- Transitioning from limited to reasonable assurance readiness.
- Managing the value-chain cap: limited ability to demand data from SMEs in the value chain.
- Mapping ~430 mandatory data points (under simplified ESRS) to existing systems.
- For non-EU companies: determining whether the €450M EU turnover threshold is met and which subsidiaries/branches trigger reporting.

**Cost estimates:**
- **EFRAG estimates**: Administrative costs for reporting under ESRS approximately **€740,000 average** for large listed companies plus **€430,000** in initial investments (adjusted to 2025 euros). The simplified ESRS will save **€4.7 billion** for First and Second Wave companies between FY2027 and FY2032 (44% reduction in reporting costs).
- **Per-company ranges**: €100,000–€250,000 per year for mid-size companies; **€500,000–€2 million or more** for large enterprises (10,000+ employees, multi-jurisdictional). A 2025 PwC survey found 60% of European multinationals exceeded their initial CSRD budget by 30% or more.
- **Assurance costs**: €50,000–€150,000 per year for limited assurance; rising to €100,000–€300,000 for reasonable assurance.
- **Total annual recurring costs**: Estimates range from **€0.82 billion to €11.54 billion** for all in-scope companies (academic and industry estimates). European Commission estimates of auditing costs alone reach €4.3 billion per year.
- **Hourly rates**: Big Four firms charge €300–€500/hour for CSRD advisory; specialist consultancies €200–€400/hour.

### California SB-219 (SB 253 + SB 261)

**Compliance challenges:**
- **Definitional uncertainty**: “Doing business in California” defined via broad gross-receipts test (including sales, property, and payroll thresholds). Revenue defined as “gross receipts” per California RTC §25120(f)(2)—broader than GAAP revenue.
- **SB 261 uncertainty**: No formal CARB regulation adopted yet; enforcement stayed by Ninth Circuit; companies must decide whether to prepare reports voluntarily.
- **Scope 3 data readiness**: Most companies lack systems to collect Scope 3 emissions across their value chain; safe harbor only through 2030.
- **Assurance capacity**: Limited assurance providers with climate expertise are scarce; costs are rising.
- **Parent-subsidiary consolidation**: CARB allows parent-level consolidated reporting, but structures must be mapped.

**Cost estimates:**
- **CARB administrative fees**: ~$2,000–$7,000 per entity per year (SB 253: ~$3,106; SB 261: ~$1,403 from CARB staff estimates).
- **Assurance costs**: $150,000–$500,000 per year for Scope 1 and 2 (limited assurance); rising for reasonable assurance by 2030.
- **Total compliance costs per company**: CARB staff estimates are modest for fees, but implementation costs (data systems, assurance, consulting) are estimated at **$600,000+ per report annually** per early California Chamber comments. A Nature study (2026) found that incremental costs for Scope 1 and 2 are relatively low because 97% of eligible firms already disclose, but Scope 3 costs will be substantially higher.
- **Penalties**: Up to $500,000/year for SB 253 non-compliance; $50,000/year for SB 261. CARB enforcement discretion applies for 2026 (good-faith efforts).
- **Total program cost**: CARB estimates ~$14 million annually to administer both programs, funded by entity fees.

## 4. Strategic Implications for Multinational Enterprises

### The Fragmentation Challenge

Multinational enterprises now face a **three-tiered compliance environment** with different materiality philosophies, verification requirements, and enforcement regimes. A company headquartered in the U.S. with EU operations and California revenue must potentially comply with:

1. **No federal SEC rule** (if rescission is finalized, though existing SEC materiality principles for climate risks remain).
2. **CSRD** if EU turnover exceeds €450M (or if it has an EU subsidiary/branch exceeding certain thresholds).
3. **SB-219** if it is a U.S. company doing business in California with >$1B revenue (SB 253) or >$500M revenue (SB 261).

### Key Strategic Considerations

#### 1. Materiality Divergence Drives Different Data Requirements
The SEC’s single financial materiality and the CSRD’s double materiality are fundamentally different concepts. A topic that is immaterial under the SEC’s investor-focused standard may be material under CSRD’s impact materiality (e.g., water pollution affecting a local community with no financial impact on the company). Companies designing a single reporting system should default to the **CSRD/ESRS framework** (the most comprehensive) and then filter for SEC and California requirements, rather than building separate systems.

#### 2. Assurance Requirements Are Becoming the Norm
Both CSRD and SB-253 mandate third-party assurance for GHG emissions, with a trajectory toward reasonable assurance. The SEC’s rule would have done the same. This creates a **structural supply constraint**: qualified assurance providers (especially for Scope 3) are limited. Multinationals should:
- Engage assurance providers 12–18 months before deadlines.
- Build audit-ready data systems from the start (not after-the-fact reconstruction).
- Budget for assurance costs as a recurring line item (€50K–€500K+ per year depending on scope).

#### 3. California and CSRD Share a Common Language (GHG Protocol)
Both SB-253 and CSRD require reporting under the **Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol**. Companies that invest in GHG Protocol-aligned Scope 1, 2, and 3 inventories for CSRD will be substantially prepared for California. This is a rare area of alignment. However, **California does not adopt double materiality**—all emissions must be reported regardless of financial materiality.

#### 4. The SEC’s Proposed Rescission Creates a Regulatory Vacuum, Not a Regulatory Freeze
Even if the SEC rule is rescinded, **existing SEC obligations** to disclose material climate risks under Regulation S-K remain. Moreover, the SEC’s rescission **does not affect California or EU law**. Companies that slow their climate data efforts because of the SEC’s retreat may face compliance gaps for the 2026 California deadline (November 10, 2026) and the 2028 CSRD Wave 2 deadline.

#### 5. SB 261’s Stay Creates a “Wait and See” Trap
With SB 261 enforcement stayed, companies may be tempted to delay preparation. However, the stay is temporary (pending appeal), and CARB has opened a public docket for voluntary submissions. The better strategy is to prepare TCFD-aligned reports now, as they also serve CSRD’s financial materiality pillar and ISSB (IFRS S2) requirements.

#### 6. The Omnibus I “Descoping” Does Not Mean No Obligation
The Omnibus I reduction means ~80% of companies initially in CSRD scope are now out. However, companies that were preparing for CSRD and are now descoped may still face **voluntary reporting expectations** from investors, banks, and supply chain partners. Moreover, Wave 1 companies (NFRD successors) still report in 2025/2026 and must comply with the full ESRS. Companies should not automatically dismantle their CSRD programs without assessing business and stakeholder needs.

#### 7. Cost Synergies Across Frameworks
While three frameworks create complexity, there are **significant cost synergies** for companies that build a unified sustainability data platform:
- **Single GHG inventory** serves CSRD, SB-253, voluntary ISSB, and investor requests.
- **Double materiality assessment** (CSRD) can be leveraged to identify climate risks for SB-261 and SEC materiality analysis.
- **Governance structures** (board oversight, risk management) required by all three can be documented once and adapted.
- **XBRL tagging** (SEC requirement if rule proceeds) is similar to the digital tagging required under ESRS.

---

## 5. Summary Recommendation

Multinational enterprises should **build for the highest common denominator** (the CSRD/ESRS framework) because:
- It is the most comprehensive (double materiality, full sustainability topics, value chain).
- It is already in effect and has survived legal challenges.
- Its simplified ESRS (post-Omnibus I) reduces the data point burden.
- Its assurance trajectory mirrors California’s and would have mirrored the SEC’s.

California’s SB-253 should be treated as a **priority compliance deadline** (November 10, 2026) that requires immediate action, while SB-261 should be prepared for but not yet filed. The SEC’s proposed rescission, while a significant policy shift, does not eliminate the need for climate data infrastructure—it merely shifts the enforcement locus from Washington to Sacramento and Brussels.
