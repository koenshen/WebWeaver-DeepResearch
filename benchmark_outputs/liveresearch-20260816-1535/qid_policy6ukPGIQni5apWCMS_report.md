## 1. Short Descriptions of Each Law

### Illinois — Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA)
**Citation:** 740 ILCS 14/1 *et seq.*  
**Enacted:** 2008 (effective 2008)  
**Key Amendment:** Public Act 103-0769 (SB 2979), effective August 2, 2024

BIPA is the oldest and most aggressively litigated biometric privacy law in the United States. It regulates any "private entity" that collects, captures, purchases, receives through trade, or otherwise obtains a person's biometric identifiers or biometric information. The law requires: (a) a publicly available written retention schedule; (b) informed written consent (now including electronic signatures) before collection; (c) prohibition on selling, leasing, or trading biometric data; (d) limitations on disclosure; and (e) destruction of data within three years of the individual's last interaction or when the initial purpose is satisfied, whichever comes first. The 2024 amendment capped the most common claims (Section 15(b) notice-and-consent and 15(d) disclosure violations) to a single recovery per person per method/recipient, overturning the "per-scan" accrual rule from *Cothron v. White Castle*. The Seventh Circuit confirmed retroactive application of this cap in *Clay v. Union Pacific Railroad Co.* (April 2026).

### Texas — Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI)
**Citation:** Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 503.001  
**Enacted:** 2009 (effective September 1, 2009)  
**Recent Amendments:** 2023 amendment (HB 4705) extended retention period for biometric identifiers used in documents required by law to be maintained longer; otherwise no major structural amendments.

CUBI prohibits any person from capturing a biometric identifier (retina/iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or record of hand or face geometry) for a commercial purpose unless the individual is informed before capture and provides consent. The law also prohibits selling, leasing, or disclosing biometric identifiers for commercial purposes (with narrow exceptions), requires reasonable care in storage and transmission, and mandates destruction within a reasonable time—not later than one year after the purpose for collection expires. Critically, CUBI provides **no private right of action**; enforcement lies exclusively with the Texas Attorney General, who may seek civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation plus injunctive relief. The Texas AG has also taken the position that unlawful capture and continued possession constitute separate violations.

### Washington — Biometric Identifier Law (RCW 19.375) and My Health My Data Act (MHMDA)
**Citation:** RCW 19.375.010–.900 (original biometric law, enacted 2017); RCW 19.373 (MHMDA, effective March 31, 2024)

Washington's original biometric law (RCW 19.375) is narrower than BIPA or CUBI. It applies only to "enrollment" of a biometric identifier—defined as capturing a biometric identifier, converting it into a reference template that cannot be reconstructed into the original output image, and storing it in a database that matches the identifier to a specific individual—for a "commercial purpose." The law requires notice and consent (or a mechanism to prevent subsequent commercial use) before enrollment, restricts disclosure and sale, and mandates retention only as long as reasonably necessary. Enforcement is solely by the Washington Attorney General, and **no private right of action exists** under RCW 19.375. The AG has never brought an enforcement action under this statute.

However, the Washington **My Health My Data Act (MHMDA)** , effective March 31, 2024, dramatically expanded the landscape. MHMDA broadly defines "consumer health data" to include biometric data and precise location data that could indicate health-related activities. It requires affirmative consent for collection and sharing, provides a **private right of action** through the Washington Consumer Protection Act (CPA), and allows treble damages up to $25,000. The first MHMDA class action was filed in February 2025 (*Maxwell v. Amazon.com, Inc.*). Importantly, MHMDA does **not** cover employee data, while RCW 19.375 does cover employee data.

## 3. Real-World Enforcement Cases and Class Action Examples

### A. Illinois BIPA — Enforcement Cases

#### Workplace/Employee Time-Tracking Cases

**1. *Speedway v. Howe* — $12.1+ Million Settlement (Final Approval 2025)**
- **Context:** Retail (gas station/convenience store chain). Employees were required to scan fingerprints on time clocks to clock in/out.
- **Violation:** Collection of fingerprint scans without informed written consent or proper disclosures.
- **Outcome:** $12,122,775 settlement fund. Class of ~7,700 employees. Estimated $970 per class member. Final approval granted October 2025.
- **Relevance:** Classic example of fingerprint timeclock liability. The case survived summary judgment and class certification was granted before settlement.
- **Source:** https://www.classaction.org/news/12m+-speedway-settlement-ends-class-action-lawsuit-over-alleged-biometric-privacy-violations

**2. *Pret A Manger* — $677,000 Settlement**
- **Context:** Restaurant chain (fast-casual dining). Fingerprint-based timeclock system for ~800 employees.
- **Violation:** Failure to meet BIPA's notice and consent requirements.
- **Outcome:** $677,000 settlement.
- **Relevance:** Demonstrates that even small-to-mid-size employers face significant exposure.
- **Source:** https://www.ebglaw.com/commercial-litigation-update/biometric-backlash-the-rising-wave-of-litigation-under-bipa-and-beyond

**3. *BNSF Railway* — $228 Million Judgment**
- **Context:** Logistics/transportation. Fingerprint scanning for truck drivers.
- **Violation:** Collection of fingerprint data without proper notice and consent.
- **Outcome:** $228 million class action judgment at trial (one of the largest BIPA trial verdicts).
- **Relevance:** Illustrates the catastrophic exposure before the 2024 amendment; case was decided under pre-amendment "per-scan" damages.
- **Source:** https://www.hbsslaw.com/cases/illinois-bipa-legal-investigation

**4. *Tri-City Foods (Burger King franchisee)* — $15.3 Million Settlement**
- **Context:** Fast-food franchise. Fingerprint timeclocks for employees.
- **Violation:** Collection of biometric data without written consent.
- **Outcome:** $15.3 million settlement (part of ~$17.5M total across three franchisee cases).
- **Relevance:** Demonstrates that franchise operators face the same exposure as corporate chains.
- **Source:** https://www.fishlawfirm.com/case-results

#### Healthcare Context

**5. *Mosby v. Ingalls Memorial Hospital* — Illinois Supreme Court (2023)**
- **Context:** Hospital. Nurses were required to scan fingerprints to access medication dispensing systems.
- **Violation:** Alleged collection of biometric data without consent.
- **Outcome:** Illinois Supreme Court unanimously held that BIPA's "health care exception" applies to **healthcare workers'** biometric data when used for treatment, payment, or operations — not just patient data. Case dismissed.
- **Relevance:** Critical win for healthcare providers. The exemption covers employee fingerprints used for medication dispensing, electronic health records, and other HIPAA-related functions.
- **Source:** https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2023/12/in-a-win-for-defendants-illinois-supreme-court-holds-that-health-care-exemption-under-bipa

**6. *Melzer v. Nuance Communications* (2025)**
- **Context:** AI-powered healthcare voice assistant. Plaintiff alleged voiceprints were collected without consent.
- **Ruling:** Court denied motion to dismiss but noted that plaintiffs were not "patients" receiving treatment from a medical professional — receiving advice from AI did not constitute medical treatment.
- **Relevance:** Suggests BIPA's healthcare exemption may not extend to AI-driven health tools.
- **Source:** https://www.privacyworld.blog/2025/12/2025-year-in-review-biometric-privacy-litigation

#### Consumer-Facing / Retail / Technology

**7. *Clearview AI* — $51.75 Million Settlement (Approved March 2025)**
- **Context:** Facial recognition company scraped 60+ billion photos from the internet without consent.
- **Violation:** BIPA (among other states' laws) for collecting face geometries without notice or consent.
- **Outcome:** Creative settlement — class receives 23% equity stake in Clearview AI (valued at ~$51.75M based on $225M valuation), plus 17% of future revenue. Nationwide injunction against selling faceprint database to most private entities.
- **Relevance:** Largest biometric privacy settlement. Shows novel resolution structures for asset-poor defendants. Permanent nationwide business practice changes.
- **Source:** https://www.loevy.com/big-wins/judge-oks-loevys-innovative-51-75-million-settlement-in-clearview-ai-class-action-lawsuit

**8. *Amazon Virtual Try-On (Svoboda v. Amazon)* — 7th Circuit (2025)**
- **Context:** Retail/e-commerce. Amazon's "Virtual Try-On" tool for eyewear used facial geometry.
- **Outcome:** 7th Circuit affirmed class certification of Illinois consumers who used the VTO feature. Amazon faces a class of hundreds of thousands.
- **Relevance:** Demonstrates that e-commerce virtual try-on tools are directly in BIPA's crosshairs.
- **Source:** https://blogs.duanemorris.com/classactiondefense/2025/12/30/seventh-circuit-affirms-certification-of-bipa-class-comprised-of-customers-who-used-amazons-virtual-try-on-tool

**9. *Charlotte Tilbury* — Settlement (2024)**
- **Context:** Beauty/cosmetics retail. Digital beauty tech tools collected facial geometries.
- **Violation:** Collection of Illinois consumers' facial geometries through virtual try-on tools without consent.
- **Outcome:** Settlement reached (amount not fully disclosed).
- **Relevance:** Illustrates risks for beauty and retail brands using augmented reality makeup try-on features.
- **Source:** https://www.classaction.org/news/category/illinois-biometric-information-privacy-act

#### 2025 BIPA Filing Statistics
- **150 new BIPA class actions** filed in 2025 (down from 427 in 2024 — a 185% decline post-amendment).
- **$136.6 million in total BIPA settlements** in 2025 (down 34% from $206M in 2024).
- Source: Duane Morris Class Action Review, reported in https://www.legalnewsline.com/cook-county-record/reforms-sliced-bipa-class-actions-in-2025-new-report-says/article_49ce0cf3-db9f-482a-b2eb-4c6084a65f96.html

### B. Texas CUBI — Enforcement Cases

**1. *Texas v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* — $1.4 Billion Settlement (July 2024)**
- **Context:** Social media/technology. Facebook's "tag suggestions" feature used facial recognition on uploaded photos.
- **Violation:** Capturing facial geometry of Texans without informed consent, in violation of CUBI. Also alleged violations of Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act.
- **Outcome:** $1.4 billion settlement — the largest privacy settlement ever obtained by a single state. Paid over five years.
- **AG's Position:** Both the unlawful capture and the continued retention of biometric identifiers constituted separate violations ($25,000 each).
- **Relevance:** Landmark enforcement demonstrates that CUBI, despite having no private right of action, carries enormous financial risk. The AG's Data Privacy and Security Initiative signals more enforcement to come.
- **Sources:** https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-secures-14-billion-settlement-meta-over-its-unauthorized-capture; https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/meta-settles-texas-biometric-data-lawsuit-for-record-1-4-billion

**2. *Texas v. Google LLC* — $1.375 Billion Settlement (Finalized October 2025)**
- **Context:** Technology. Google collected biometric identifiers (voiceprints from Google Assistant, facial geometry from Google Photos and Nest Hub Max) and geolocation data without consent.
- **Violation:** CUBI (for biometrics) and Texas consumer protection laws. Alleged use of "Face Grouping" in Google Photos and "Face Match" in Nest Hub Max without consent.
- **Outcome:** $1.375 billion settlement. Largest single-state settlement against Google. Concluded two lawsuits filed in 2022.
- **Relevance:** Second multibillion-dollar CUBI enforcement. Confirms that any company using facial recognition or voiceprint technology in Texas faces existential AG enforcement risk.
- **Sources:** https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-finalizes-historic-settlement-google-and-secures-1375-billion-big-tech; https://www.bracewell.com/resources/google-agrees-to-1-375-billion-settlement-as-texas-attorney-general-continues-data-privacy-push

**3. *Texas AG's Data Privacy and Security Initiative (2024–Present)***
- **Context:** The Texas AG created a dedicated unit focused on data privacy enforcement.
- **Significance:** The AG has signaled that CUBI enforcement will be a priority. The AG's office has stated that "unpermitted capture and retention of biometric identifiers result in two separate CUBI violations."
- **Relevance:** Any business collecting biometric data in Texas — including for employee time-tracking, security access, or consumer-facing AI tools — is at risk. The AG has indicated that the $25,000 per violation penalty is calculated per individual whose data is captured without consent.
- **Source:** https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2024/8/texas-attorney-generals-office-zeroes-in-on-data-privacy-and-entities-using-biometric-data

**4. *Workplace/Retail Implications Under CUBI***
- **Key Point:** To date, Texas AG enforcement has focused on large technology platforms (Meta, Google). However, the AG has not yet brought actions against employers using fingerprint timeclocks or retailers using facial recognition. The risk remains real: CUBI applies to any "person" capturing biometrics for a "commercial purpose," and the AG has broad discretion.
- **Best Practice Note:** Texas employers must provide notice and obtain consent before capturing biometric identifiers. The Texas AG's office has stated that biometric data collected for "security purposes" (e.g., access control) is presumed to expire upon termination of employment.
- **Source:** https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/employer-alert-texas-reaches-1-4b-settlement-over-allegations-of-violation-of-biometric-information-privacy-law

### C. Washington — Enforcement Cases

#### Under RCW 19.375 (Original Biometric Law)
- **No known enforcement actions** have been brought by the Washington Attorney General since the law's enactment in 2017. The statute has been described as "more bark than bite" due to its narrow scope (only "enrollment" for commercial purpose) and lack of private right of action.
- **Source:** https://hintzelaw.com/blog/wa-my-health-my-data-act-pt7-biometrics

#### Under Washington My Health My Data Act (MHMDA)

**1. *Maxwell v. Amazon.com, Inc.* — First MHMDA Class Action (Filed February 2025)**
- **Context:** Retail/e-commerce. Amazon's advertising SDK embedded in third-party mobile apps (e.g., Weather Channel, OfferUp) allegedly collected consumers' precise location and biometric data without consent.
- **Violation:** MHMDA — failure to obtain consent before collecting consumer health data (including biometric data and precise location data that could indicate health-related activities). Also alleged violations of federal wiretapping laws and Washington CPA.
- **Status:** Pending. First-ever MHMDA class action. Could set precedent for the law's scope and the viability of private right of action claims.
- **Relevance:** Demonstrates that MHMDA's private right of action is now being tested. The law's broad definition of "consumer health data" (including biometric data and location data that implies health-related activities) creates exposure for any company collecting location data through mobile SDKs, retail apps, or advertising technology.
- **Sources:** https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2025/02/first-washington-my-health-my-data-act-class-action-lawsuit-filed; https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/ph-privacy/biometrics-litigation-update-first-class-action-complaint-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act-is-your-company-ready

**2. *MHMDA Enforcement Structure***
- Private right of action: Violation of MHMDA is a per se violation of the Washington Consumer Protection Act (CPA).
- Damages: Up to $7,500 per violation, with treble damages available up to $25,000. Attorneys' fees recoverable.
- Class actions: Permitted — and the first class action was filed in 2025.
- AG enforcement: The Washington AG also has authority to enforce MHMDA.
- **Key Limitation:** MHMDA does **not** cover employee data. Only "consumer" data (Washington residents or data collected in Washington) is protected.
- **Source:** https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy

## 4. Strategic Implications for Enterprises with Multi-State Biometric Data Operations

### 4.1 The Enforcement Asymmetry Is the Defining Risk

The three states present fundamentally different enforcement models that require distinct compliance strategies:

| State | Enforcement Model | Risk Profile | Primary Exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Illinois | Private right of action (class actions) | **High-frequency, high-volume** | Employee fingerprint timeclocks, consumer facial recognition, voiceprints |
| Texas | AG enforcement only | **Low-frequency, catastrophic** | Any biometric capture at scale (AG targets large players) |
| Washington (RCW 19.375) | AG enforcement only | **Minimal** (never enforced) | Low risk for most employers |
| Washington (MHMDA) | Private right of action + AG | **Emerging** (first class action filed 2025) | Consumer-facing biometric/location data collection |

**Strategic Implication:** Illinois remains the highest-compliance-risk jurisdiction by volume, but the 2024 amendment has significantly reduced the "annihilative liability" risk. Texas, despite having no private right of action, has proven it can extract multibillion-dollar settlements. Washington's original law is a paper tiger, but MHMDA is a sleeping giant for consumer-facing businesses.

### 4.2 Consent Infrastructure Must Be Multi-Layered

Each state has different consent requirements:

- **Illinois:** Requires "informed written consent" — now explicitly including electronic signatures. The consent must disclose the specific purpose and duration of collection. For employees, this means a standalone consent form or electronic acknowledgment.
- **Texas:** Requires "informed consent" before capture. No specific format, but best practice is written or electronic consent. For employers using fingerprint timeclocks, a signed consent form or electronic acknowledgment is recommended.
- **Washington (RCW 19.375):** Requires notice and consent (or a mechanism to prevent subsequent commercial use) before "enrollment." Security purpose exemptions may apply.
- **Washington (MHMDA):** Requires "affirmative consent" with detailed disclosures for consumer health data. This is a higher bar than BIPA's "informed consent" and requires separate, unambiguous opt-in for each purpose.

**Strategic Implication:** A single consent form cannot serve all three states. Enterprises should design a modular consent system that (a) detects the individual's state of collection/employment, (b) applies the appropriate state-specific consent language, and (c) captures the consent in a verifiable, auditable format.

### 4.3 The 2024 BIPA Amendment Has Changed the Illinois Calculus — But Not Eliminated the Risk

The 2024 amendment (SB 2979) and the Seventh Circuit's *Clay* decision (April 2026) have:
- **Capped Section 15(b) and (d) violations** to one per person per method/recipient — meaning a $5,000 maximum per person for intentional violations, not $5,000 per scan.
- **Reduced BIPA class action filings** by 185% (from 427 in 2024 to 150 in 2025).
- **Reduced total settlement value** by 34% (from $206M to $136.6M).

**However**, BIPA is still the most active biometric privacy law in the country. A class of 10,000 employees still faces potential exposure of $10 million (negligent) to $50 million (intentional) per violation type. The amendment also did **not** affect Section 15(a) (retention policy) or Section 15(c) (prohibition on selling/trading) claims.

**Strategic Implication:** The amendment is a significant relief, but BIPA compliance remains mandatory. Enterprises should:
- Implement compliant written retention policies (publicly available).
- Obtain electronic or written consent for all biometric collection.
- Ensure data destruction within three years of last interaction.
- Audit existing biometric systems for compliance gaps.

### 4.4 Texas AG Enforcement Is Unpredictable but Catastrophic

The Texas AG's $1.4 billion Meta settlement and $1.375 billion Google settlement demonstrate that CUBI enforcement, while infrequent, can be existential. The AG has created a dedicated Data Privacy and Security Initiative and has stated that the $25,000 per violation penalty applies to **each individual** whose biometric data is captured without consent.

**Strategic Implication:** Enterprises with a significant Texas footprint should:
- Prioritize CUBI compliance even though no private right of action exists.
- Obtain informed consent for all biometric capture in Texas.
- Implement destruction protocols (within one year of purpose expiration, or immediately upon employee termination).
- Recognize that the AG may view both capture and retention as separate violations.
- Monitor AG enforcement activity — if the AG turns to workplace time-tracking or retail facial recognition, the exposure could be massive.

### 4.5 Washington's Two-Law Structure Creates a Compliance Gap

Washington's original biometric law (RCW 19.375) is narrow and rarely enforced. However, MHMDA (2024) creates significant new obligations for **consumer-facing** biometric data collection. The key distinction:

- **Employee biometric data** (fingerprint timeclocks, facial recognition for security access) is covered by RCW 19.375 only — which has never been enforced and provides no private right of action.
- **Consumer biometric data** (facial recognition in retail, voiceprints in call centers, location data from apps) is covered by MHMDA — which has a private right of action and is already the subject of class action litigation.

**Strategic Implication:** Enterprises should:
- Treat Washington employee biometric data as **low enforcement risk** but still implement best-practice consent and retention policies.
- Treat Washington consumer biometric data as **high litigation risk** — comply with MHMDA's affirmative consent, disclosure, and data minimization requirements.
- Audit all consumer-facing digital properties (mobile apps, websites, SDKs, advertising technology) for potential MHMDA exposure.

### 4.6 Technology-Specific Risk Areas

| Technology | BIPA Risk | CUBI Risk | WA Risk (RCW 19.375) | WA Risk (MHMDA) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fingerprint timeclocks (employees)** | **HIGH** — most common BIPA class action | **MODERATE** — AG enforcement risk, no private right | **LOW** — never enforced | **NONE** — excludes employee data |
| **Facial recognition for security access** | **HIGH** — requires consent | **MODERATE** — AG enforcement risk | **LOW** — security purpose exemption likely applies | **NONE** — excludes employee data |
| **Voiceprint IVR/call centers** | **HIGH** — Wilcosky v. Amazon Alexa case pending | **HIGH** — Google settlement included voiceprints | **MODERATE** — if voiceprints are "enrolled" | **HIGH** — if used for consumer identification |
| **Virtual try-on / AR beauty tools** | **HIGH** — Charlotte Tilbury, Amazon VTO cases | **MODERATE** — AG enforcement risk | **LOW** | **HIGH** — if biometric data collected from consumers |
| **Retail facial recognition (loss prevention)** | **HIGH** — requires consent (no security exemption for private entities) | **MODERATE** — AG enforcement risk | **LOW** — security purpose exemption | **HIGH** — if consumer biometric data collected |
| **AI-based health/video analytics** | **MODERATE** — healthcare exemption may apply | **MODERATE** — AG enforcement risk | **LOW** | **HIGH** — likely within MHMDA scope |

### 4.7 Recommended Multi-State Compliance Framework

**1. Centralized Biometric Data Inventory**
- Map all biometric collection points across the enterprise (employee timeclocks, security systems, consumer-facing tools, call centers, AI analytics).
- Tag each system by state(s) of operation and relevant legal framework.
- Identify which systems collect "biometric identifiers" (fingerprints, face geometry, voiceprints, iris/retina scans) vs. "biometric information" (derived data).

**2. State-Specific Consent Architecture**
- **Illinois:** Implement electronic or written consent with: (1) specific purpose; (2) retention period; (3) disclosure of third-party sharing; (4) confirmation of rights. Electronic signatures are valid.
- **Texas:** Implement informed consent with: (1) notice before capture; (2) clear consent mechanism; (3) retention/destruction notice.
- **Washington (RCW 19.375):** Provide notice and consent OR mechanism to prevent subsequent commercial use. Security purpose exemption available.
- **Washington (MHMDA):** Implement affirmative consent with detailed disclosures for consumer-facing biometric collection. Not applicable to employees.

**3. Retention and Destruction Policies**
- **Illinois:** Written policy (publicly available) with retention schedule. Destroy within 3 years of last interaction or when purpose satisfied.
- **Texas:** Destroy within reasonable time, **not later than 1 year** after purpose expires. For security purposes, destruction upon employment termination.
- **Washington (RCW 19.375):** Retain only as long as reasonably necessary.
- **Washington (MHMDA):** Follow data minimization principles; specific retention limits not specified.

**4. Vendor Management and Third-Party Risk**
- Audit all biometric technology vendors (timeclock systems, facial recognition software, voiceprint analytics).
- Ensure vendors provide contractual guarantees of compliance with all applicable state laws.
- Require vendors to provide data destruction certifications upon contract termination.
- Review SDK/data-sharing agreements for MHMDA compliance (as demonstrated in the *Maxwell v. Amazon* case).

**5. Insurance and Risk Transfer**
- BIPA claims are increasingly triggering insurance coverage disputes. Review CGL, cyber liability, and employment practices liability policies for biometric privacy exclusions.
- The 2024 amendment has reduced but not eliminated the risk — settlement values remain in the millions for large classes.
- In Texas, the risk is concentrated in AG enforcement, which may not be covered by standard liability policies.

**6. Monitoring and Preparedness**
- **Illinois:** Watch for continued litigation under Sections 15(a) and 15(c), which were not affected by the 2024 amendment. Monitor pending cases on the healthcare exemption's scope.
- **Texas:** Track the AG's enforcement priorities. The GA-created Data Privacy and Security Initiative is active and may target workplace or retail biometrics next.
- **Washington:** The *Maxwell v. Amazon* case will be the bellwether for MHMDA class action viability. If it survives dismissal, expect a wave of MHMDA class actions.
