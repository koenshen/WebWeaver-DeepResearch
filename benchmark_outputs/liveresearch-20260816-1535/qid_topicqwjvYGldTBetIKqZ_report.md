

# The Great American Retail Transformation: Malls, Main Streets, and the New Consumer Landscape

## Executive Summary

Over the past two decades, and especially since the COVID-19 pandemic, the United States has undergone a profound restructuring of its retail sector. The rise of e-commerce, shifting consumer preferences, and the shock of the pandemic have accelerated the decline of traditional shopping centers while reshaping how Americans shop, work, and gather. The impacts are stark: mall vacancy rates have more than doubled from pre-recession lows, over 7,300 stores closed in 2024 alone, and e-commerce now accounts for roughly 16–23% of all retail sales (depending on how it is measured). Yet the story is not simply one of decline. From the ashes of dead malls, communities are pioneering creative urban transformations—converting empty retail into housing, college campuses, medical centers, logistics hubs, urban farms, and even churches. This report examines the scale of these changes, their economic and social consequences, and the most promising models for repurposing abandoned retail spaces.

## 1. How the Retail Transformation Is Affecting Local Economies and Jobs

### 1.1 The Employment Shift

Retail trade employment in the United States has been on a long, slow decline as a share of total employment. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) projects that retail trade will make up 8.6% of total employment by 2030, down from 9.7% in 2020 and 10.2% earlier (BLS, "Retail trade employment: before, during, and after the pandemic," https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-11/retail-trade-employment-before-during-and-after-the-pandemic.htm). Monthly BLS data show retail trade employment hovering around 15.4–15.5 million in 2024, essentially flat to slightly declining over the prior 12 months as of mid-2026 (BLS, Employment Situation Summary, July 2026, https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm). In July 2026, retail trade lost 19,000 jobs, with declines concentrated in warehouse clubs, supercenters, and general merchandise retailers (−21,000) (ibid.).

Seasonal hiring patterns have also shifted. During the holiday buildup seasons of 2022–2024, retailers added an average of 475,000 seasonal employees—well below the 605,000 average of 2018–2021. Layoffs after the holidays have also been smaller, but the net result is that the retail sector is employing fewer people, especially in temporary roles (BLS, "Trends in retail trade holiday employment buildups and layoffs," https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/trends-in-retail-trade-holiday-employment-buildups-and-layoffs.htm).

A major BLS benchmark revision in 2025 revealed that the U.S. economy added 911,000 fewer jobs between April 2024 and March 2025 than originally reported, with retail among the sectors most heavily revised downward (Time, "Job Growth Has Been Much Weaker Than Reported," https://time.com/7315666/jobs-report-bls-revised-data). This suggests the retail labor market was significantly weaker than first reported.

### 1.2 The "Domino Effect" on Local Businesses and Tax Revenue

When a mall or major retailer closes, the damage ripples outward. The IMD business school describes a "domino effect": when large stores close, foot traffic to neighboring businesses collapses, endangering the viability of other local shops and restaurants. This reduction in foot traffic can trigger a cascading series of closures, particularly in shopping centers where smaller retailers depend on the draw of anchor tenants (IMD, https://www.imd.org/research-knowledge/strategy/articles/how-high-streets-and-shopping-malls-face-a-domino-effect-from-major-store-closures).

The fiscal impact on municipalities is severe. According to the International Council of Shopping Centers, malls and shopping centers across the country provide **$400 billion in local tax revenue annually** (Cornell Journal of Law and Public Policy, https://publications.lawschool.cornell.edu/jlpp/2021/10/12/the-great-american-shopping-mall-past-present-and-future). When retailers close, they reduce the taxable revenue base that municipalities depend on to fund local services. Bankrupt landlords also stop paying property taxes, compounding the shortfall. The Cornell article notes that shopping malls are often the largest taxpayer in any given municipality.

Real-world examples illustrate the pain:

- **Bangor, Maine**: The Bangor Mall's annual tax bill dropped below $1 million for the first time in two decades, though the city managed to absorb the blow because the mall accounted for only 1.31% of total taxable valuation (Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, "The Unmalling of America," https://www.lincolninst.edu/publications/articles/2019-12-unmalling-america-municipalities-navigating-changing-retail-landscape).

- **Spartanburg, South Carolina**: The city lost $1.3 million in annual tax revenue when Memorial Mall challenged its tax assessment before eventually closing in 2017 (ibid.).

- **Moreno Valley, California**: The closure of the Moreno Valley Mall in early 2026 due to safety code violations affected over 130 small businesses and 2,600–3,000 employees, with lost wages, business instability, and "reputational harm to the community" (Facebook/Lone Wolf News, Feb 2026; LinkedIn posts citing community impact).

Store closures also reduce property values in surrounding neighborhoods. The Camoin Associates report notes that successful malls stimulated real estate development and increased property values; their decline reverses those gains (Camoin Associates, "The Death and Rebirth of the American Shopping Mall: Part 1," https://camoinassociates.com/resources/the-american-shopping-mall-part-1).

### 1.3 The Productivity Paradox

The retail sector has experienced a productivity paradox. Although employment has been flat to declining, **total retail sales have continued to grow**. U.S. retail sales (excluding e-commerce) reached $5.25 trillion in 2024, up 2.6% from 2023, while e-commerce sales hit $1.19 trillion, up 7.5% (ExportUSA, "Online sales statistics in America," https://www.exportusa.eu/statistics-ecommerce-mcommerce-united-states.php). This means fewer retail workers are serving more total sales volume, a trend driven by automation, self-checkout, and the shift of volume to highly efficient e-commerce fulfillment centers.

## 2. What's Happening to the Empty Stores and Malls?

### 2.1 Vacancy Statistics: A Tale of Two Retail Real Estate Markets

The retail real estate market is deeply bifurcated. **Enclosed regional malls** are struggling mightily, while **neighborhood and strip centers** (especially grocery-anchored) have largely recovered to pre-pandemic vacancy levels.

**Mall vacancy rates:**
- Mall vacancy began rising steadily in 2017 and hit a pandemic-era peak of nearly 15% in 2021.
- It fell back to the 11% range as consumers returned to in-person shopping, but has edged up since Q4 2023 (MCB Real Estate, "The Shopping Center Opportunity," https://mcbrealestate.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Shopping-Center-Opportunity-White-Paper_MCB-Real-Estate.pdf).
- By early 2025, mall vacancy was approximately **8.9%**, according to MMCG Investment Research (https://www.mmcginvest.com/post/retail-investment-benchmarks-2025-sales-vacancy-rent-trends).
- Some projections suggest up to **87% of large shopping malls may close** over the next decade (Capital One Shopping Research, https://capitaloneshopping.com/research/mall-closure-statistics).

**Strip center and neighborhood center vacancy rates:**
- By contrast, neighborhood and community center vacancy rates have returned to pre-pandemic levels, falling in early 2024 to around **5.0–6.4%** (MCB Real Estate; MMCG Investment Research).
- Overall national retail vacancy (all types) was about **4.3%** in Q1 2025, only slightly above the all-time low (MMCG Investment Research).
- However, as of Q2 2025, the shopping center vacancy rate rose to **5.8%**, a 50-basis-point increase year-over-year, signaling some softening (Cushman & Wakefield, cited in CNBC, https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/28/chain-store-bankruptcy-mall-real-estate-small-business.html).

### 2.2 The Store Closure Wave

The "retail apocalypse" accelerated dramatically in 2024. According to Coresight Research, retailers announced more than **7,300 store closures** in 2024, a 57% increase over 2023 (Forbes, "Twice As Many Stores In The U.S. Are Expected To Close In 2025," https://www.forbes.com/sites/pamdanziger/2025/01/30/retail-apocalypse-well-over-twice-as-many-stores-will-close--as-open-in-2025). For 2025, Coresight Research predicted up to **15,000 store closures**, more than double 2024's total and potentially breaking the record set during the pandemic in 2020 (Forbes, ibid.).

### 2.3 The Physical Transformation of Empty Retail

Over 165 million square feet of older retail space—mainly dead malls and vacant anchor boxes—has been demolished since 2018 (MMCG Investment Research). The net expansion of retail inventory has been negligible: total retail stock grew less than 2% from 2019 to 2024, reflecting a deliberate market response to shifting conditions.

A significant amount of empty mall space is being absorbed by **non-traditional tenants**. As CNBC reported, "We are seeing a rise in medical offices and spas and other uses you wouldn't have typically seen" (James Bohnaker, Cushman & Wakefield, https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/28/chain-store-bankruptcy-mall-real-estate-small-business.html). The rise of discount retailers (off-price stores like TJ Maxx, Burlington, and Ross) and experiential tenants (escape rooms, pickleball, fitness centers) has been a major driver of absorption in the best-located centers.

## 3. How People's Shopping Habits Have Really Changed

### 3.1 The E-Commerce Surge—and Its Limits

The COVID-19 pandemic was a transformational event for online shopping. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, e-commerce accounted for **11.8% of total retail sales** in Q1 2020. By Q4 2020, it had surged to about **16.1%** (Cornell Journal of Law and Public Policy, https://publications.lawschool.cornell.edu/jlpp/2021/10/12/the-great-american-shopping-mall-past-present-and-future). As of Q1 2026, the Census Bureau's seasonally adjusted figure stood at **16.9%** (Census Bureau, Quarterly Retail E-Commerce Sales, Q1 2026, https://www.census.gov/retail/ecommerce.html).

However, different measurement methodologies yield different penetration rates. Digital Commerce 360, which excludes certain categories that the Census Bureau includes (such as gasoline, restaurants, and automotive), calculates that e-commerce accounted for **22.7% of total retail sales in 2024** (Digital Commerce 360, https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/article/us-ecommerce-sales). By this measure, e-commerce has more than doubled from about 11% in 2019.

Total U.S. e-commerce sales reached **$1.19 trillion in 2024**, up 8.2% from the previous year (SellersCommerce, https://www.sellerscommerce.com/blog/us-ecommerce-sales). E-commerce grew at a rate **more than double** that of total retail (7.5% vs. 2.6% in 2024, per ExportUSA).

### 3.2 The Great Hybrid: In-Store Isn't Dead, It's Transformed

Despite the e-commerce surge, physical stores remain the dominant channel. In-store shopping still drives **77% of FMCG (fast-moving consumer goods) sales** in the U.S., and the average household spends nearly three times more in-store than online (NielsenIQ, Nov 2025, https://www.facebook.com/NielsenIQ.global/posts/1218126693702034). A 2025 study found that approximately **45% of American consumers prefer to shop in-store**, while 28% prefer online and 27% use a hybrid approach (Instagram post citing study, 2025).

What has changed dramatically is **how** and **why** people visit stores:
- **Trip frequency has declined** but basket size has increased. Consumers are making fewer but larger shopping trips.
- **The "mission" of the store has shifted** from pure transaction to experience and discovery. As the TruRating blog noted, "We go online to buy the staples, we go to stores to discover" (TruRating, https://trurating.com/blog/are-consumer-behaviors-changing-in-light-of-covid-19).
- **Online research, in-store purchase (and vice versa)** is now the norm. "Webrooming" (research online, buy in-store) and "showrooming" (browse in-store, buy online) are both prevalent.
- **Grocery e-commerce** has seen a massive structural shift. The number of respondents who shop online at least once per week increased nearly five-fold between fall 2019 (11.6%) and spring 2020 (51.2%) (ScienceDirect, "The increase in online shopping during COVID-19," https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1757780223005012).

### 3.3 What the Pandemic Permanently Changed

The McKinsey consumer sentiment study from 2020 found that more people expected to make portions of their purchases online post-COVID. A follow-up survey by Nosto found that **67% of consumers said their online purchasing increased since the start of the pandemic**, with 27% saying it increased "a lot" (Nosto, "Consumer Behaviour Stats," https://www.nosto.com/blog/consumer-behavior-stats). The UNCTAD survey confirmed that following the pandemic, more than half of respondents now shop online more frequently (UNCTAD, https://unctad.org/news/covid-19-has-changed-online-shopping-forever-survey-shows).

The pandemic also permanently altered which shopping channels consumers use for different categories:
- **Essentials and commodity goods** have shifted heavily online (groceries, household supplies, pet food).
- **Big-ticket and high-consideration items** remain multi-channel, with consumers using digital for research and stores for final purchase.
- **Apparel and fashion** have seen a significant online shift, but returns remain high (30-40%).
- **Experiential retail** (dining, entertainment, fitness) has recovered strongly as people crave social interaction.

## 4. Creative Solutions: Bringing Abandoned Retail Spaces Back to Life

From coast to coast, communities are pioneering inventive reuses of empty retail spaces. These projects range from partial retrofits to complete demolitions and rebuilds. Below are the most notable models, with detailed case studies.

### 4.1 Housing Conversions: From Retail to Residential

The housing shortage (estimated at 4.5 million homes needed) and the glut of vacant retail space have created a powerful synergy. As of January 2022, at least **192 U.S. malls had plans to add housing** to their footprint, and 33 malls had added apartments since the start of the pandemic (Forbes, "From Retail To Rentals," https://www.forbes.com/sites/chriswestfall/2024/12/09/from-retail-to-rentals-how-empty-malls-became-housing-solutions). A report by Enterprise Community Partners found that converting just **10% of underperforming strip mall space into housing could create 700,000 new units** (Nice News, "Turning Abandoned Malls Into Affordable Housing," https://nicenews.com/humanity/turning-abandoned-malls-into-affordable-housing).

**Case Study: The Arcade, Providence, Rhode Island**
America's oldest indoor shopping mall (built 1828) was transformed into 48 micro-apartments ranging from 225 to 775 square feet. Rents started at $550/month (now $800–$1,800/month). The project also preserved 17 small retail spaces, which function as an incubator for artisans and start-ups. Demand is staggering: over 4,000 people are on the waiting list. The project has been a driver of economic recovery in Providence's Financial District (Business Insider, https://www.businessinsider.com/americas-first-shopping-mall-is-now-micro-apartments-2016-10; CNU, "Micro Lofts at the Arcade Providence," https://www.cnu.org/what-we-do/build-great-places/micro-lofts-arcade-providence).

**Case Study: Flatiron Crossing, Broomfield, Colorado**
The Macerich-developed "HiFi" project is a 345-unit luxury multifamily community built on a former parking lot at Flatiron Crossing mall. Residents can walk to 65,000 sq ft of retail, restaurants, a movie theater, and green spaces. The project demonstrates how underutilized parking lots can be converted into high-density housing that benefits from—and supports—the remaining retail (Forbes, "From Retail To Rentals"; CNBC, "How Shopping Malls Are Being Transformed Into Apartments," https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J1GIF6VNipE).

**Case Study: Skyview Park Apartments, Rochester, New York**
PathStone, a nonprofit development group, retrofitted an empty Sears department store at a former mall into 157 affordable rental units for adults 55+, with half of the units reserved for people at risk of homelessness. The project opened in 2022 (Nice News, "Turning Abandoned Malls Into Affordable Housing").

### 4.2 Educational and Civic Hubs

**Case Study: Austin Community College Highland Campus, Austin, Texas**
Highland Mall opened in 1971 as Austin's premier shopping destination. By 2010, it was a retail casualty: 1.2 million square feet sitting largely empty. Austin Community College purchased the former JCPenney building and eventually the entire mall, transforming it into an educational campus now serving 15,000 students annually. The "Jewel Box" study and social space incorporates black steel framing and glass reclaimed from the former mall. The project demonstrates how adaptive reuse can serve institutional needs while revitalizing failed commercial infrastructure (Perkins&Will, https://perkinswill.com/project/acc-highland-campus-phase-ii; CollegeTowns, "Reviving Dead Malls and Dying Colleges," https://www.collegetowns.org/p/reviving-dead-malls-and-dying-colleges).

**Case Study: Crosstown Concourse, Memphis, Tennessee**
An abandoned 1.5-million-square-foot Sears distribution center was converted into a "vertical urban village" containing theaters, offices, health providers, a YMCA, a college, a high school, restaurants, and 265 apartments. This project reused 1.5 million square feet of existing structure, saving 33% on embodied carbon compared to new construction (Forbes, "Turning Dead Malls Into Community Assets," https://www.forbes.com/sites/tomvanderark/2020/11/04/turning-dead-malls-into-community-assets; Gensler, "Stranded Assets," https://www.gensler.com/blog/realizing-the-potential-of-abandoned-malls-and-office-parks).

### 4.3 Medical and Healthcare Conversions

**Case Study: Jackson Medical Mall, Jackson, Mississippi**
In 1995, Dr. Aaron Shirley envisioned converting the failing Jackson Mall into a comprehensive healthcare facility for underserved communities. Today, the Jackson Medical Mall Thad Cochran Center is **98.9% occupied**, sees approximately **5,000 visitors per day**, houses the Jackson State University College of Health Sciences and University of Mississippi Medical Center services, and generates **200,000 healthcare visits annually**. The mall's tenants account for over 1,500 full-time jobs (Jackson Medical Mall, https://jacksonmedicalmall.org; StoryMaps, https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/e9be21e887694d63bc1a61e97a00305d).

**Case Study: Vanderbilt Health 100 Oaks, Nashville, Tennessee**
A former shopping mall was converted into a medical center, now housing dozens of clinics and specialty practices (UNC School of Government, "Reimagining Shopping Malls," https://ced.sog.unc.edu/2014/08/21/reimagining-shopping-malls).

### 4.4 Mixed-Use Town Centers: The "Unmalling" of America

**Case Study: Belmar, Lakewood, Colorado (The Gold Standard)**
The Villa Italia Mall, a 1.4-million-square-foot enclosed regional center built in the 1960s, had a **60% vacancy rate** by 2000. Rather than attempt a renovation, the city and developer Continuum Partners chose to demolish the mall and rebuild it as an urban neighborhood. Today, Belmar is essentially downtown Lakewood—a place the city never had. The project includes:
- 76 stores and 20 restaurants
- 1,300 residential units (2,000+ residents)
- 281,000 sq ft of office space
- 16-screen cineplex, Whole Foods, Target, Best Buy, Nordstrom
- 9 acres of parks and plazas
- 90,000 sq ft events center
- A Hyatt hotel
- **$200 million in annual retail sales**
- **60% price premium for housing** compared to the surrounding area
- An annual increase of over **$2 million in local tax revenues**

Lakewood Mayor Bob Murphy called it "urban renewal [that] took an outdated mall with an abysmal 60 percent vacancy rate and morphed it into a vibrant urban landscape" (CNU, "Belmar," https://www.cnu.org/what-we-do/build-great-places/belmar; Continuum Partners, https://continuumpartners.com/project/belmar-shopping-district; Econsult Solutions, "Town Center Redevelopment," https://econsultsolutions.com/town-center-redevelopment-as-a-local-growth-strategy).

### 4.5 Logistics and Fulfillment Centers

**Case Study: Ridgmar Mall, Fort Worth, Texas**
The long-struggling Ridgmar Mall (opened 1976) was acquired by Ramrock Real Estate in 2026 with plans to redevelop the site as "Ridgmar 30 Logistics Crossing," a six-building development of approximately **931,000 square feet** of logistics space. The mall had lost its JCPenney anchor and was down to a Dillard's outlet, a movie theater, and a few small businesses (Fort Worth Report, https://fortworthreport.org/2026/08/05/struggling-ridgmar-mall-finds-a-new-owner-set-to-become-logistics-center).

Amazon has reportedly been in talks with Simon Property Group to take over mall spaces left vacant by closed department stores (J.C. Penney, Sears) and convert them into online fulfillment centers. This would put fulfillment centers closer to consumers, enabling faster delivery and potentially allowing customers to pick up returns at the mall (Supply Chain Dive, https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/mall-department-store-fulfillment-center-warehouse-amazon/583652).

### 4.6 Churches and Community Centers

A number of dead malls have found new life as megachurches. The First Baptist Church purchased the 400,000-square-foot Lakeland Mall for $5.6 million in the late 1990s, spending $2 million on renovations to become the "Church at the Mall." The Lexington Mall in Kentucky was partially demolished and converted into a satellite worship center for Southland Christian Church. The Grand Village Mall in Grandville, Michigan was donated to Mars Hill congregation in 2000 (Business Insider, "Dying Malls Are Being Transformed Into Churches," https://www.businessinsider.com/dying-malls-are-being-transformed-into-churches-2017-6).

### 4.7 Indoor Vertical Farms

Empty malls are being repurposed into indoor vertical farms, using hydroponics and LED lighting to grow crops year-round. Examples include:
- **Southland Mall, Memphis, Tennessee**: Converted part of the mall into an indoor vertical farm growing leafy greens and herbs.
- **Rolling Acres Mall, Akron, Ohio**: Experimental vertical farming producing romaine lettuce, basil, and cherry tomatoes.
- **Valley View Center, Dallas, Texas**: Sections repurposed for community farming and education.

These projects address food insecurity and sustainability while revitalizing unused commercial spaces (LinkedIn, "Abandoned malls repurposed as urban farms," https://www.linkedin.com/posts/johnrfugazzie_urban-farms-sprout-from-sprawl-planet-natural-activity-7357795338868457472-6Vd7).

### 4.8 Other Notable Conversions

- **Data centers**: The former Pittsburgh Mall was redeveloped into a major carrier hotel and data center serving southwestern Pennsylvania (Wikipedia, "Dead mall," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_mall).
- **Springfield Town Center, Virginia**: The former Springfield Mall was converted into an open-air mixed-use development with a 12-screen movie theatre, shops, and restaurants with outdoor seating (ibid.).
- **Pembroke Lakes Mall, Florida**: A former Sears department store was converted into a Round One Entertainment Center (StoneCreek Partners, https://stonecreekllc.com/shopping-mall-redevelopment-and-re-use-projects).
- **Burlington, Vermont**: An old mall was converted into a high school (YouTube, "Converting abandoned shopping malls," https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t0sIUGEuEDM).

## 5. Challenges and Cautions

While the creative reuse of malls is inspiring, it is not without obstacles:

- **Conversion costs**: Moody's Analytics estimates conversion costs of **$200 to over $800 per square foot**, depending on the existing structure (Forbes, "From Retail To Rentals").
- **Zoning restrictions**: Many municipalities must rezone mall properties, a process that can take years. The Arcade Providence residents cannot have open flames (no stoves allowed) due to a city ordinance tied to the historic building's structure.
- **Design limitations**: Malls were not built for housing. They typically lack windows and natural light, making only exterior-facing spaces suitable for apartments. Plumbing and HVAC systems must be completely reconfigured.
- **Tax implications**: Retail properties are typically net tax generators (they generate more in tax revenue than they consume in services). Residential conversions can be net tax users, as housing requires more in public services (schools, police, fire) than it generates in property taxes. As retail expert Nick Egelanian notes, "Housing is the part of a community that actually costs the government money, and retail is the part that actually makes the government money" (Bluebeam, "Dead Mall Conversions," https://blog.bluebeam.com/mall-construction-conversions).
- **Environmental remediation**: Many mall sites have soil contamination from dry cleaners, automotive services, or asbestos. The Belmar project required significant environmental mitigation (Colorado Brownfields Partnership, https://cobrownfieldspartnership.org/success-stories/belmar).

## 6. Looking Ahead: The Future of Retail Real Estate

The U.S. retail real estate market is undergoing a fundamental restructuring that will likely continue for the next decade. Key trends to watch:

1. **The "flight to quality"** will continue, with Class A malls and grocery-anchored centers thriving while Class B and C malls struggle or close. Well-located properties with strong demographics will be redeveloped; poorly located ones will be demolished or left to decay.

2. **The rise of the "retail-tainment" model** will accelerate. Malls that survive will be those that transform into entertainment destinations with dining, fitness, gaming, and experiences rather than pure shopping.

3. **Housing conversions will grow** but will remain niche due to the high costs and design challenges. The most successful conversions will likely be on mall parking lots (new construction) rather than inside existing mall structures.

4. **Logistics and last-mile fulfillment** will absorb a significant share of former retail space, especially in densely populated urban areas where land for warehouses is scarce.

5. **Public-private partnerships** will be essential. As the Belmar and Jackson Medical Mall examples show, the most successful redevelopments involve collaboration between developers, local governments, and community stakeholders.

6. **The hybrid consumer is here to stay**. Retailers that succeed will be those that offer seamless omnichannel experiences—buy online, pick up in-store; endless aisle; convenient returns; and personalized service across all channels.
