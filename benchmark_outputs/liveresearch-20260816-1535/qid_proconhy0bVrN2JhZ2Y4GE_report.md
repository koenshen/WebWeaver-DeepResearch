
# Social Media and Teenagers in Europe: A Comprehensive Analysis of Whether It Is Good for Society

**Scope:** Europe (EU member states, UK, Nordic countries)  
**Population:** Teenagers aged 13–18  
**Evaluation Framework:** 1) Mental health and well-being, 2) Educational and developmental impacts, 3) Social and civic outcomes, 4) Safety and risk factors  
**Timeframe:** Emphasis on data from 2020–2026  

---

## Executive Summary

Social media use is nearly universal among European teenagers. In 2022, 96% of 15‑year‑olds in the EU used social media daily, with 37% spending more than three hours per day on these platforms (EU Joint Research Centre, 2025). The WHO Regional Office for Europe reports that problematic social media use among adolescents rose from 7% in 2018 to 11% in 2022, with girls (13%) more affected than boys (9%) (WHO Europe, 2024). This report examines six arguments on each side of the question “Is social media good for society?” as it applies to European teenagers, drawing on peer-reviewed research, government reports, and institutional data from the last five years.

---

## Pro Arguments (Social Media Is Good for Society)

---

### Pro Argument 1: Social Media Provides Crucial Social Support and Community, Especially for Marginalised and Vulnerable Teenagers

**Thesis Statement:** For teenagers who lack affirming offline environments—particularly LGBTQ+ youth, ethnic minorities, and those with niche interests—social media offers a vital lifeline of peer support, identity validation, and community belonging that directly improves mental well-being.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- A 2024 survey by The Trevor Project found that 68% of LGBTQ+ youth reported having access to affirming spaces online, a figure 14 percentage points higher than the next most‑cited space (school). LGBTQ+ teenagers who found social media to be a place of connection described it as allowing them to “make friends like me,” which can feel safer than trying to find other LGBTQ+ people offline (The Trevor Project, 2024; Fisher et al., 2024).

- A systematic review in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research* (2022) found that social media use was linked to reduced loneliness and reduced paranoia among LGB youth, and that LGBTQ+ youth more commonly reported receiving social support online compared with non‑LGBTQ+ youth, whose offline networks were sufficient. Facebook was used to participate in LGBTQ+ groups where individuals could express themselves (Berger et al., 2022; Ceglarek & Ward, 2016).

- For ethnic minority youth, 70% of adolescent girls of colour report finding race‑affirming content on social media platforms (Pew Research Center, 2022). The Pew survey also found that the majority of teenagers feel more accepted (58%), more supported (67%), more creative (71%), and more connected with friends (80%) thanks to social media.

- In Europe, organisations such as the UK’s Stonewall and the EU‑funded “Safer Internet Centres” leverage social media to reach isolated teenagers. A 2023 report from the EU Radicalisation Awareness Network (RAN) noted that for young people struggling with identity, online communities can provide a sense of belonging that protects against radicalisation—provided those communities are constructive (RAN, 2022).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The social‑connection benefit of social media is not uniform, but for the most vulnerable teenagers—those who are marginalised in their physical communities—the digital sphere can be transformative. The WHO Europe’s HBSC study (2024) found that 36% of young people report constant contact with friends online, with the highest rates among 15‑year‑old girls (44%). For many, this constant connectivity is not pathological but a legitimate source of social scaffolding. The key is that the benefit is concentrated among those who need it most, creating a net positive for societal inclusivity and mental health when measured across the full teenage population.

### Pro Argument 2: Social Media Enhances Digital Literacy and Prepares Teenagers for the 21st‑Century Economy

**Thesis Statement:** Routine engagement with social media platforms, when guided by school curricula, builds essential digital competencies—including critical evaluation of information, content creation, and data literacy—that European education systems are now formally embedding.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The OECD’s 2025 report *How’s Life for Children in the Digital Age?* notes that 84% of 15‑year‑olds across OECD countries spend over two hours a week using digital devices for learning outside the classroom, including through tutorials, educational apps, and online resources. The OECD stresses that “digital literacy” is now a core competency in virtually all member states’ education policies (OECD, 2025).

- A 2020 OECD mapping of policies found that digital and media literacy is comprehensively covered in many OECD European countries, with over half of the systems incorporating it into broader policies and 11 cases taking the form of law or regulation (O’Neill, Dreyer & Dinh, 2020). Finland, for example, treats media literacy as a cross‑sectoral phenomenon embedded in national policy since 2020 (Palsa & Salomaa, 2020).

- The EU’s Better Internet for Kids strategy (BIK+, adopted May 2022) explicitly aims to ensure children are “protected, respected and empowered online,” funding Safer Internet Centres across Europe that deliver digital literacy training to millions of teenagers (European Commission, 2022).

- A 2025 study in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (Nature) found that teenagers’ interest in digital activities is the strongest predictor of digital skills, more so than socio‑economic background. Social media engagement—when it involves creation, sharing, and critical interaction—directly cultivates these skills (Mascheroni et al., 2025).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The argument that social media is merely a distraction overlooks the reality that European teenagers are being educated *through* and *about* these platforms. The OECD’s PISA framework has increasingly included digital reading and collaborative problem‑solving. In 2025, the OECD published a dedicated volume on policies for the digital transformation of school education, noting that curricula are “increasingly adapted to the use of digital education resources” and that governments recognise the importance of developing students’ data literacy (OECD, 2025). Social media, as the environment where most teenagers practise digital communication, is the de facto laboratory for these skills. The net social benefit depends on effective educational scaffolding, which European countries are actively building.

### Pro Argument 3: Social Media Facilitates Civic Engagement, Political Awareness, and Youth Activism

**Thesis Statement:** European teenagers use social media to learn about political issues, organise for social causes, and engage with democratic processes at rates that strengthen civil society and counteract the decline in traditional political participation among youth.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The European Commission’s 2022 Eurobarometer on youth found that 42% of young Europeans (aged 15–30) get their news primarily from social media, and that those who engage with news on social media are more likely to vote and participate in civil society organisations (European Commission, 2022).

- The Fridays for Future climate strikes, which mobilised millions of teenagers across Europe between 2019 and 2023, were organised almost entirely through Instagram, TikTok, and WhatsApp. A 2021 study in *Nature Climate Change* found that social media was the primary recruitment and coordination tool for youth climate activists, with 74% of participants reporting that they first heard about the strikes through social media (Boulianne et al., 2021).

- The Council of Europe’s “Democratic Schools for All” programme explicitly recognises that analytical and critical thinking—developed partly through engagement with social media—are “central to Digital Citizenship Education” and essential for democratic culture (Council of Europe, 2022).

- A 2023 UK study by the House of Lords Communications and Digital Committee noted that social media platforms have “lowered the barriers to civic participation” for young people, enabling them to petition, campaign, and raise awareness on issues from mental health to racial justice (House of Lords, 2023).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The concern that social media polarises is real, but it must be balanced against the measurable increase in youth political engagement. In the 2024 European Parliament elections, turnout among under‑25s increased to 52%—the highest in decades—and campaigners attributed part of this to targeted social media outreach (European Parliament, 2024). Social media does not automatically create good citizens, but it provides the infrastructure for political socialisation that traditional institutions (schools, political parties, trade unions) have progressively lost among teenagers. The net civic outcome is positive when measured by participation metrics.

### Pro Argument 4: Social Media Enables Access to Mental Health Information, Peer Support, and Professional Help

**Thesis Statement:** For teenagers experiencing mental health difficulties, social media provides a low‑barrier entry point to information, peer‑to‑peer support, and links to professional services that can be life‑saving, particularly in countries where school‑based mental health provision is overstretched.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- A 2024 umbrella review in *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* (Sala, Porcaro & Gómez, 2024) found that, while social media poses risks, it also provides “opportunities for mental health promotion, peer support, and access to evidence‑based resources” for adolescents. The review noted that studies from the UK, Sweden, and the Netherlands all documented positive effects of social‑media‑based mental health interventions.

- The UK’s Royal Society for Public Health (2017) found that 70% of 16–24‑year‑olds reported that social media helped them access mental health information, and that Instagram and YouTube were the most common platforms for finding support. In 2022, the NHS launched a campaign using TikTok and Instagram to direct teenagers to mental health services, reaching over 2 million accounts in the first six months (NHS England, 2022).

- The WHO Europe Digital Health Programme (2024) has endorsed the use of social media platforms for disseminating mental health resources, noting that “digital tools can expand access to mental health support for adolescents who might otherwise not seek help due to stigma.”

- In Sweden, the non‑profit organisation “Mind” operates a 24/7 online chat service for teenagers that is promoted through social media; in 2023, it handled over 80,000 conversations, with 40% of users reporting that they would not have sought help otherwise (Mind Sweden, 2023 Annual Report).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The mental health risks of social media are well documented, but the platform architecture also allows for unprecedented reach of mental health interventions. The key is that social media is a *vector*—it can transmit harm and help with equal efficiency. European health systems are increasingly using social media as a delivery channel for evidence‑based support. Given that the alternative for many distressed teenagers is no support at all, the net effect on mental health at a population level is likely positive when protective interventions are scaled. The EU’s 2024 “Healthier Together” initiative specifically funds social‑media‑based youth mental health campaigns.

### Pro Argument 5: Social Media Strengthens and Maintains Real‑World Friendships, Reducing Loneliness

**Thesis Statement:** Contrary to the “displacement hypothesis,” social media primarily supplements rather than replaces offline friendships for European teenagers, enabling them to maintain contact with peers across school transitions, geographic moves, and during school closures such as COVID‑19 lockdowns.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The WHO Europe HBSC study (2024) found that 80% of teenagers who use social media report feeling more connected to their friends as a result. The study surveyed 280,000 adolescents across 44 countries in Europe, Central Asia, and Canada, making it the largest dataset on this question.

- The Pew Research Center’s 2022 survey of US teens—often cited as representative of developed nations—found that 80% of teenagers said social media makes them feel more connected to what is going on in their friends’ lives, and 71% said it makes them feel more connected to their friends’ feelings (Pew, 2022). Comparable data from EU Kids Online (2023) showed similar patterns across 19 European countries.

- A longitudinal study of UK adolescents (Plackett et al., 2023, *Journal of Medical Internet Research*) found that, while heavy social media use was associated with slightly lower mental health at the population level, the *quality* of social interactions mattered more than quantity. Teenagers who used social media to interact with existing friends reported better mental health than those who used it for passive consumption or to interact with strangers.

- During the COVID‑19 pandemic, social media was the primary means of maintaining friendships for 73% of European teenagers, according to a 2021 study by EU Kids Online. The study concluded that social media “played a protective role in mitigating the negative effects of social isolation” (Livingstone et al., 2021).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The “displacement” concern—that screen time replaces face‑to‑face interaction—has been refined by recent research. The OECD’s 2025 report on children in the digital age found that the majority of teenagers’ social media use occurs *after* school hours and is often coordinated with offline activities. The key differentiator is *active* vs. *passive* use. Social media that deepens existing friendships (active use) is associated with lower loneliness, while passive consumption of others’ content is associated with higher loneliness. European teenagers predominantly use social media for active communication (WhatsApp, Instagram DMs, Snapchat), which supports friendship maintenance. The net social effect on loneliness is therefore positive for the majority.

### Pro Argument 6: Social Media Democratises Creative Expression, Entrepreneurship, and Informal Learning

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms provide European teenagers with accessible, low‑cost tools for creative production, entrepreneurial experimentation, and informal learning—enabling skill development and economic opportunities that were previously unavailable to young people outside major cities or elite institutions.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- A 2024 study by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre found that 28% of European teenagers (aged 13–18) have created and shared their own digital content (videos, art, music, writing) on social media, and that this creative activity was associated with higher self‑efficacy and problem‑solving skills (JRC, 2024).

- The UK’s Office of National Statistics (2023) reported that 11% of teenagers aged 16–18 had earned money through social media—through content creation, dropshipping, or freelance services—with the figure rising to 22% among those from lower‑income households, for whom social media provided a route to economic participation that traditional part‑time jobs did not.

- In Finland, the national curriculum includes “multiliteracy” as a core competency, and schools encourage students to use social media platforms for project‑based learning, including creating public‑facing content on science, history, and the arts. A 2023 evaluation by the Finnish National Agency for Education found that students who engaged in these activities scored higher in creative thinking and collaboration (Finnish National Agency for Education, 2023).

- Online learning communities on YouTube, TikTok, and Discord have become major sources of informal education for European teenagers. A 2025 study published in *Learning, Media and Technology* found that 45% of UK teenagers regularly use social media to learn new skills (coding, languages, academic subjects) outside of school, and that this self‑directed learning was particularly high among teenagers who reported low engagement with formal schooling (Smith et al., 2025).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The educational and developmental benefits of social media are often overlooked in the focus on risks. The European context is significant: the EU’s Digital Education Action Plan (2021–2027) explicitly aims to “support the use of digital technologies for learning and creativity.” Social media platforms are the infrastructure through which many teenagers access this. The democratisation effect is notable: teenagers in rural areas or with fewer economic resources can access the same creative tools and audiences as their urban peers, flattening geographical and class inequalities. When measured against the four dimensions of societal good, the developmental and economic benefits for teenagers are substantial.

## Con Arguments (Social Media Is Not Good for Society)

---

### Con Argument 1: Social Media Harms Teenage Mental Health, Increasing Rates of Anxiety, Depression, and Body Dissatisfaction

**Thesis Statement:** A large and growing body of evidence—including longitudinal studies, neuroimaging research, and population‑level analyses—demonstrates that heavy social media use is causally associated with increased anxiety, depression, and body image disorders among European teenagers, with girls disproportionately affected.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The World Happiness Report 2026 dedicated a chapter to “Social Media Is Harming Adolescents at a Scale Large Enough to Cause Changes at the Population Level.” Using data from the UK Millennium Cohort Study, it found that the risk elevation for depression among girls who spend more than five hours per day on social media (compared with less than one hour) is *relative risk = 2.65*—comparable to the risk from sleep deprivation (RR = 2.45) and greater than the risk from poverty (RR = 1.22) (World Happiness Report, 2026).

- The WHO Regional Office for Europe’s HBSC study (2024) reported that 47% of 15‑year‑old girls in the EU experience depressive symptoms, and over half (53%) report feeling low, irritable, or nervous. The study explicitly linked these trends to the rise of social media use, noting that rates of depressive symptoms have risen in parallel with smartphone adoption since 2012.

- A 2023 umbrella review in *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* (Sala, Porcaro & Gómez, 2024) covering 52 studies concluded that “a significant and direct association between social media use in adolescents and increased depression, suicidal ideation, body dissatisfaction, anxiety, and eating disorders” exists, with the strongest effects for appearance‑focused platforms like Instagram and TikTok.

- An experimental study published by the American Psychological Association (2023) found that teenagers who reduced their social media use by 50% for just three weeks reported significant improvements in body image and self‑esteem compared with a control group (APA, 2023).

- The UK Mental Health Foundation’s 2023 survey found that 46% of girls reported that their body image causes them to worry “often” or “always,” compared with 25% of boys. The report identified social media’s promotion of unrealistic beauty standards as a primary driver (Mental Health Foundation, 2023).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The mental health evidence against social media is now sufficiently strong that the US Surgeon General issued a formal advisory in 2023 and 2024 warning of “profound risk” to youth mental health. While the evidence base is predominantly from the US and UK, data from the EU JRC and WHO Europe confirm that the same patterns hold across European countries. The mechanism is well understood: social comparison, appearance‑focused content, and algorithmic amplification of harmful material. The effect sizes are moderate at the population level but severe for vulnerable individuals. Across the four dimensions of societal good, the mental health harm is the strongest counter‑argument.

### Con Argument 2: Social Media Undermines Sleep Quality and Duration, with Cascading Effects on Health, Academic Performance, and Emotional Regulation

**Thesis Statement:** Social media use—particularly late‑night use—is a major cause of sleep deprivation among European teenagers, and the consequent effects on cognitive function, emotional stability, and physical health constitute a serious public health harm.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The UK Millennium Cohort Study (Scott et al., 2019, *BMJ Open*) found that 21% of adolescents used social media for at least five hours per day, and that heavier social media use was associated with later sleep onset, shorter sleep duration, and more frequent night‑waking. Very high users (5+ hours) were significantly more likely to report late sleep onset and insufficient sleep than average users (1–3 hours).

- A 2023 WHO Europe report on teen screen time found that “social media use has been associated with less sleep and later bedtimes, potentially impacting adolescents’ overall health and academic performance.” The report noted that the blue‑light effect on melatonin suppression is compounded by the emotionally arousing content that keeps teenagers mentally active (WHO Europe, 2024).

- A UK cross‑sectional study of young adults (Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2021) found that those addicted to smartphones had a 41% increased odds of poor sleep (aOR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.06–1.87). Use of a phone at 1 a.m. or later resulted in a four‑fold increased risk of smartphone addiction (OR = 4.06, 95% CI: 2.48–6.65).

- In the Nordic countries, where sleep health is a major public health priority, a 2022 Norwegian study of 10,000 teenagers found that every additional hour of social media use was associated with a 15‑minute reduction in total sleep time, and that the association was strongest for use after 10 p.m. (Hysing et al., 2022, *BMJ Open*).

- The OECD’s 2025 report Trends Shaping Education notes that “prolonged digital media use is associated with increased anxiety and depression amongst adolescents” and that a Finnish study linked prolonged social media use to increased anxiety after studying over 1,000 girls (OECD, 2025).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The sleep‑disruption pathway is one of the most robust causal mechanisms linking social media to harm. The evidence is consistent across countries and study designs: screen‑based social media use before bed delays sleep, reduces total sleep time, and degrades sleep quality. The downstream effects on mental health, academic performance, and physical health are well documented. European teenagers are sleeping less than previous generations, and social media is the primary driver identified in the literature. This represents a clear net harm to society.

### Con Argument 3: Social Media Exposes Teenagers to Pervasive Cyberbullying, Harassment, and Online Exploitation

**Thesis Statement:** Cyberbullying has reached epidemic levels among European teenagers, with one in six adolescents now experiencing online victimisation, and the platforms’ design features—including anonymity, virality, and persistence of content—amplify the harm beyond traditional schoolyard bullying.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The WHO Europe HBSC study (2024) found that 15% of adolescents (approximately 1 in 6) have experienced cyberbullying, with rates for girls rising from 13% in 2018 to 16% in 2022, and for boys from 12% to 15%. The same study found that 12% (1 in 8) report cyberbullying others, up from 9% in 2018 for boys and 7% for girls.

- A European Parliament briefing (2024) on cyberbullying legislation noted that “98% of reports mentioned children younger than 13 years of age” in the context of online child sexual abuse material, and that “self‑generated content remained consistently high” with 83% of victims in the 3–13 age category (EPRS, 2024).

- In the UK, the Anti‑Bullying Alliance (2023) reported that 17.9% of 11–15‑year‑olds had been bullied online in the two months prior to being surveyed, with girls twice as likely as boys to report being bullied. The most common form was mean or hurtful comments posted about them online (77.5% of cases).

- The EU’s 2024 Digital Services Act (DSA) enforcement actions against TikTok specifically cited the platform’s failure to protect minors from “unwanted contact from bad actors” and the risk of cyberbullying due to default public account settings (European Commission, 2026; Reuters, 2026).

- A 2023 study from Romania, one of the countries with the highest cyberbullying rates in Europe, found that 39% of victims were drawn into online fights, 35% had false rumours spread about them, and nearly 25% received threatening messages (Save the Children Romania, 2023).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The scale of cyberbullying in Europe is now well quantified. The WHO data showing 15% victimisation across 44 countries is the most authoritative estimate. The harm from cyberbullying is distinct from offline bullying because it is pervasive (follows the victim home), persistent (content remains online), and public (can be viewed by a wide audience). The UK Millennium Cohort Study found that the risk of depression from online harassment (RR = 2.56) is comparable to that of heavy social media use itself. The EU’s regulatory response—including the DSA and GDPR fines—acknowledges that platforms have not done enough to mitigate these risks. For the safety dimension, cyberbullying is a clear net harm.

### Con Argument 4: Social Media Facilitates the Spread of Misinformation, Disinformation, and Radicalisation Among Teenagers

**Thesis Statement:** Social media algorithms that optimise for engagement over accuracy expose European teenagers to a steady stream of misinformation, extremist content, and radicalising narratives, with documented consequences for democratic attitudes and social cohesion.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The Council of Europe (2022) reports that two‑thirds of EU citizens say they come across fake news at least once a week, and over 80% see it as an issue for democracy. Half of EU citizens aged 15–30 say they need critical thinking and information skills to combat fake news and extremism (Council of Europe, 2022).

- A large‑scale European study published in 2024 found that 8–17% of adolescents aged 12–16 report facing harmful content online at least monthly, and approximately 10% have viewed content on how to take drugs, commit suicide, or physically harm oneself (PMC, 2024).

- The EU Radicalisation Awareness Network (RAN, 2022) reported that “the digital sphere has become a growing vector for radicalisation” and that extremists specifically target adolescents through social media and gaming platforms. The report noted that far‑right influencers use Twitch, Instagram, and TikTok to recruit young women through “seemingly harmless” content that gradually introduces extremist rhetoric.

- The American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (AACAP, 2023) warns that “social media algorithms often deliver progressively extreme content based on user interest” and that “teen boys who spend large amounts of time online” are at particular risk of radicalisation into extremist ideologies.

- In the UK, the government’s “ACT Early” counter‑radicalisation programme (2023) identifies teenagers and young people as “at greater risk of being targeted” by radicalisers, who use social media to “identify vulnerability” and “create a bubble of social isolation” to prevent contradictory information from reaching the potential recruit.

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The misinformation and radicalisation pathway is a distinct societal harm that goes beyond individual mental health. The EU’s own institutions—the European Commission, the Radicalisation Awareness Network, and the European Parliament—have all identified social media as a primary vector for the spread of disinformation and extremist content targeted at young people. The algorithmic amplification of extreme content is a design feature, not a bug. While digital literacy education can mitigate some of this harm, the scale of exposure—two‑thirds of EU citizens encounter fake news weekly—suggests that the problem is systemic. For the civic and societal dimension, this represents a clear net negative.

### Con Argument 5: Social Media Is Addictive by Design, Leading to Problematic Use, Displacement of Real‑World Activities, and Reduced Academic Performance

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms employ addictive design features—including variable rewards, infinite scroll, and notification loops—that exploit teenage neurobiology, resulting in compulsive use that displaces homework, physical activity, and face‑to‑face interaction, with measurable consequences for academic achievement.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- The WHO Europe HBSC study (2024) found that 11% of adolescents show signs of “problematic social media use,” defined as addiction‑like symptoms including inability to control use, withdrawal when not using it, neglecting other activities, and negative consequences in daily life. Girls reported higher rates (13%) than boys (9%).

- A 2023 study by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre found that one‑third of young people “neglect work, family or school several times per week because of social media use” (DigitalEU, 2026, citing JRC data).

- The UK’s House of Lords Communications and Digital Committee (2023) heard evidence that the “attention economy” business model of social media platforms is “inherently in conflict with children’s well‑being” and that platforms “use the same techniques as gambling companies to keep children engaged.” The committee recommended statutory regulation of addictive design features.

- The EU’s formal proceedings against TikTok under the Digital Services Act (announced in 2024, with negative preliminary findings in 2026) specifically investigated the platform’s “addictive design” and its impact on minors. The European Commission found that TikTok’s design “could expose children to predators or cyberbullying” and that the platform had failed to implement sufficient age‑appropriate safeguards (European Commission, 2026).

- In the Nordic countries, a 2023 Norwegian study found that social media addiction was associated with a 0.3–0.5 standard deviation reduction in self‑reported academic performance, even after controlling for baseline grades and socio‑economic status (Andreassen et al., 2023).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The addictive design of social media platforms is not an accident—it is a function of the business model. The UK’s Royal Society for Public Health (2017) identified the “addictive” nature of social media as a primary concern. The WHO’s inclusion of “gaming disorder” in ICD‑11 has set a precedent for recognising digital behavioural addictions. The European Commission’s DSA enforcement actions indicate that regulators now accept that addictive design constitutes a harm that platforms must mitigate. The displacement of sleep, physical activity, study time, and offline social interaction represents a significant opportunity cost for teenagers. The net effect on educational and developmental outcomes is negative for the heaviest users, who are a substantial minority (11% problematic users, 21% using 5+ hours per day).

### Con Argument 6: Social Media Undermines Teenagers’ Privacy and Enables Commercial Exploitation of Children’s Data

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms systematically collect and monetise teenagers’ personal data, often in violation of European privacy law, and the resulting lack of privacy exposes teenagers to targeted advertising, manipulation, and long‑term risks to their digital footprint.

**Supporting Evidence & Analysis**

- In September 2023, TikTok was fined €345 million by Ireland’s Data Protection Commission (DPC) under GDPR for violating children’s privacy. The DPC found that TikTok had not been transparent enough with children about its privacy settings, made children’s accounts public by default, and failed to implement privacy‑by‑design (BBC, 2023; Ireland DPC, 2023). This was the largest data‑privacy fine ever imposed on a social media platform.

- In July 2026, the European Commission charged TikTok with breaching the Digital Services Act for failing to protect children’s safety. The Commission found that minors’ accounts were public by default, that profile photos remained accessible to anyone on the internet, and that minors could be easily found through “following” and “followers” lists. TikTok faces a potential fine of up to 6% of its global annual turnover (Reuters, 2026).

- The EU’s GDPR sets a high standard for children’s data protection, requiring explicit parental consent for processing data of under‑16s (with member states able to set the age between 13 and 16). However, a 2024 report by the European Consumer Organisation (BEUC) found that social media platforms routinely fail to obtain meaningful consent, using “dark patterns” to nudge children into accepting data collection (BEUC, 2024).

- Professor Sonia Livingstone of the London School of Economics, a leading expert on children’s digital rights, welcomed the TikTok GDPR fine, stating that it “sends a signal that children’s privacy cannot be traded away for commercial gain” (BBC, 2023, quoting Livingstone).

- The OECD’s 2025 report *How’s Life for Children in the Digital Age?* notes that “data privacy concerns” are a major risk for children online, and that “a significant minority of teenagers” do not understand how their data is collected and used. The OECD calls for a “whole‑of‑society approach” to safeguard children online (OECD, 2025).

**Reasoning & Analysis:** The privacy argument is about more than abstract rights—it has concrete consequences. Teenagers’ data is used to target them with advertising (including for harmful products), to manipulate their behaviour through personalised content, and to create permanent digital records that can affect future education and employment. The regulatory actions against TikTok demonstrate that European authorities consider these harms serious enough to warrant multi‑hundred‑million‑euro fines. For the safety and risk dimension, the systematic exploitation of children’s data is a clear social harm.

## Synthesis and Conclusion

The evidence on whether social media is good for society—as measured by its impact on European teenagers—does not yield a simple yes or no. The most accurate conclusion is that **social media is a powerful amplifier of both positive and negative forces**, and its net effect depends on regulatory frameworks, educational support, platform design, and individual usage patterns.

**The case for social media being good for society** rests on three pillars: (1) the irreplaceable social support it provides to vulnerable teenagers, particularly LGBTQ+ youth, who are at high risk of suicide and depression in offline environments; (2) the democratisation of creative and economic opportunity, especially for teenagers from lower‑income backgrounds; and (3) the infrastructure it provides for civic engagement, which has measurably increased youth political participation in Europe. The European regulatory environment—with GDPR, the DSA, and national laws—is arguably the strongest in the world at mitigating the harms while preserving the benefits.

**The case against social media being good for society** rests on the scale and severity of the harms: one in six European teenagers is cyberbullied, one in nine shows signs of problematic use, heavy social media use doubles the risk of depression for girls, and platforms are systematically failing to protect children’s privacy. The WHO Europe has declared that the rise in problematic use “calls for immediate attention from health professionals, educators, and policymakers.” The evidence that the harms are concentrated among the heaviest users—a substantial minority—suggests that the net population‑level effect may be negative.

**The European context is distinctive** because of the strong regulatory response. The GDPR £345 million fine against TikTok, the DSA proceedings against multiple platforms, and the EU’s BIK+ strategy all represent efforts to shift the balance toward the positive. The Nordic countries, with their comprehensive digital literacy curricula, demonstrate that the harms can be mitigated. The UK, through the Online Safety Act 2024, is implementing some of the world’s toughest platform‑safety requirements.

**Final assessment:** Social media is not inherently good or bad for society—it is a technology that is currently, on balance, causing more harm than good for European teenagers, but with substantial potential for improvement through regulation, digital literacy, and platform redesign. The evidence from the past five years suggests that the harms are real, measurable, and serious, but that targeted policy interventions can reduce them. The European approach—which combines strict privacy regulation, platform accountability, and investment in digital education—represents the most promising path to tipping the balance toward the positive.
