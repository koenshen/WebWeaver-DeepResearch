
# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: A Comprehensive Research Report

## 1. Introduction

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) — also known as lateral gene transfer (LGT) — is the movement of genetic material between organisms by means other than parent-to-offspring (vertical) inheritance. While HGT has long been recognized as a dominant force in prokaryotic evolution, its role in multicellular eukaryotes — especially plants and animals — has only come into sharp focus in the last two decades. Once considered vanishingly rare, HGT is now understood to be a small but significant evolutionary mechanism in many eukaryotic lineages, capable of providing adaptive genetic novelties that can be co-opted for new ecological niches, metabolic pathways, and even key evolutionary transitions.

This report synthesizes the current state of knowledge on HGT in non-microbial eukaryotes, covering the most compelling documented cases, the proposed mechanisms and barriers, and the broader evolutionary significance of these rare but consequential events.

## 2. Scope and Scale: How Much HGT Occurs in Eukaryotes?

The incidence of HGT varies dramatically across eukaryotic groups. In most complex multicellular organisms, the proportion of the genome derived from horizontal acquisition is modest — typically between 0.04% and 2% of the gene inventory [1]. However, there are striking outliers:

- **Bdelloid rotifers**: ~8–10% of genes are of foreign origin (bacteria, fungi, plants) [2,3].
- **Plant-parasitic nematodes**: Extensive HGT, particularly genes encoding plant cell wall–degrading enzymes, has been documented; in some species, more than 3% of the genome may be horizontally acquired [4,5].
- **Charophyte algae (e.g., *Klebsormidium nitens*)**: ~1.5% of the genome (95 gene families) appears to be foreign [6].
- **Mosses (*Physcomitrella patens*)**: 57 families of nuclear genes acquired from prokaryotes, fungi, or viruses [7].
- **Parasitic plants (e.g., *Cuscuta* spp.)**: Over 108 transcribed, likely functional HGT events [8].

A landmark 2020 study of 307 vertebrate genomes found evidence for more than 975 independent transfers of transposable elements among vertebrates, with the vast majority occurring in aquatic species [9].

## 3. Barriers and Mechanisms: Why HGT Is Rare in Complex Eukaryotes

Several factors conspire to make HGT rarer in plants and animals than in bacteria or unicellular eukaryotes:

- **Germline sequestration**: In most multicellular organisms, germ cells are physically isolated from somatic cells. A foreign gene taken up by a somatic cell will not be inherited unless it can reach a germ cell [10,11].
- **Complex gene regulation**: Eukaryotic genes require promoters, enhancers, introns, and appropriate chromatin environments; a foreign gene may not be expressed in its new host.
- **Protein compatibility**: The encoded protein must fold correctly, be targeted to the right cellular compartment, and function without disrupting host physiology.

### The Weak-Link Model

A compelling framework for understanding HGT in eukaryotes is the **weak-link model**, which proposes that foreign DNA is most likely to enter the germline during early developmental stages when cells are undifferentiated and unprotected [10]. Key predictions include:

- Unicellular eukaryotes, where every cell is a potential germ cell, show the highest HGT frequencies.
- Multicellular organisms with external fertilization (e.g., many fish, mosses, algae) have more HGT because gametes and zygotes are directly exposed to environmental DNA.
- Asexual multicellular organisms should show elevated HGT because any cell carrying foreign DNA can propagate it to offspring.
- Animals with close endosymbiotic associations (e.g., *Wolbachia* in insects) are especially prone to HGT because the symbiont resides in germline tissues [10,12].

### Known Vectors of HGT in Eukaryotes

Several mechanisms can mediate the physical transfer of DNA across species boundaries:

1. **Endosymbiotic bacteria**: *Wolbachia* and *Carsonella* are intracellular bacteria that reside in the germline of their hosts and can transfer DNA directly to the host genome [12,13].
2. **Virus-like transposons (Mavericks/Polintons)**: These ancient elements, which share features of both transposons and viruses, have been shown to carry cargo genes between nematode species. They encode a capsid, a DNA polymerase, and an integrase, and in nematodes they acquired a herpesvirus-like fusogen that enables them to form infectious virus-like particles [14,15].
3. **Parasitic plants (haustorial connections)**: The intimate physical connection between parasitic plants and their hosts provides a direct conduit for DNA exchange [8,16].
4. **Natural grafting**: In plants, wounding and grafting can lead to the exchange of chloroplasts, mitochondria, and even whole nuclei [17].
5. **Phagocytosis / trophic relationships**: The "you are what you eat" hypothesis suggests that DNA from consumed organisms can be incorporated into the consumer's genome. This is thought to be particularly important in unicellular eukaryotes [18].
6. **Viruses**: Endogenous retroviruses and other viral elements can carry host genes between species; for example, syncytin genes in placental mammals are derived from a retroviral envelope gene [19].

## 4. HGT in Plants

### 4.1 The Role of HGT in the Colonization of Land

One of the most profound examples of HGT in plant evolution occurred during the transition from aquatic to terrestrial environments. The colonization of land by plants — a pivotal event in Earth's history — was supported by the acquisition of genes from bacteria, fungi, and other microbial sources [7,20]. Key adaptations obtained via HGT include:

- **Phenylpropanoid metabolism**: The enzyme phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), central to the synthesis of lignin and flavonoids, was likely acquired from bacteria via a fungal intermediate [21].
- **Desiccation tolerance, UV protection, and pathogen defense**: Multiple bacterial genes were transferred to the common ancestor of land plants, contributing to the suite of traits needed for survival on land [20,22].
- **Cell wall modification enzymes**: Genes for expansin-like proteins and other cell wall–modifying enzymes were acquired from bacteria [7].

A systematic analysis of land plant genomes by Ma et al. (2022) found that HGT events are active in charophytes and all major groups of land plants, but their frequency declines rapidly in seed plants compared to seedless plants [6]. The moss *Physcomitrella patens* alone contains 57 families of foreign nuclear genes [7].

### 4.2 Parasitic Plants: Hotspots of HGT

Parasitic plants, which form direct vascular connections with their hosts via haustoria, are the most prolific HGT recipients in the plant kingdom.

**Dodder (*Cuscuta* spp.)**: A landmark 2019 study identified 108 transcribed and likely functional HGT events in *Cuscuta campestris*, plus 42 additional regions with host-derived transposon and non-coding sequences [8]. Many of these genes are highly expressed in the haustorial feeding structures and are involved in:

- **Haustorium development and function**
- **Amino acid metabolism**
- **Defense responses**
- **Production of small RNAs** that are sent back into the host plant to silence host defense genes [8,23]

Remarkably, 18 of the functional HGTs in *Cuscuta* were independently acquired by the same gene families in the parasitic Orobanchaceae family, suggesting convergent evolution and strong adaptive value [8]. Some of these acquisitions are ancient, predating the radiation of the genus, and may have contributed to the success of parasitism itself [8,23].

**Other parasitic plants**: HGT has been extensively documented in the mitochondrial genomes of *Rafflesia*, *Striga*, and *Orobanche* [16,24]. The transfer of the *Pytheas* sequence from *Cuscuta* to *Orobanche* via a shared host plant demonstrates that HGT can occur between two parasitic plants using the host as a "bridge" [25].

### 4.3 The *Fhb7* Gene: A Fungus-to-Plant Transfer with Agricultural Significance

One of the most striking and agriculturally important examples of HGT is the *Fhb7* gene in wheat. This gene, which confers resistance to Fusarium head blight (FHB) and crown rot, was found in the wheat relative *Thinopyrum elongatum* [26,27]. Key findings:

- *Fhb7* encodes a glutathione S-transferase (GST) that detoxifies trichothecene mycotoxins produced by *Fusarium* species.
- Homologs of *Fhb7* are absent in the plant kingdom but are ~97% identical to sequences in endophytic *Epichloë* fungi, strongly supporting a fungus-to-plant HGT event.
- When introgressed into wheat, *Fhb7* confers broad resistance to FHB without yield penalty, providing a powerful tool for resistance breeding [26,27].

### 4.4 Plant-to-Bacteria and Bidirectional HGT

The exchange of genes between plants and bacteria is a dynamic, bidirectional process. A 2024 study by Haimlich et al. detected 75 unique genes that were horizontally transferred between plants and bacteria, with significantly more genes moving from bacteria to plants than the reverse [28]. These transfers often provide functional advantages, such as the ability for bacteria to colonize plants by breaking down cell wall sugars [28].

### 4.5 HGT in Plant Mitochondria

Plant mitochondrial genomes are particularly prone to HGT, frequently harboring mitochondrial genes from distantly related plants. Mechanisms proposed include parasitism, transfer agents (insects, pathogens, viruses), and illegitimate pollination [10,29]. One of the earliest discoveries of plant-to-plant HGT came from the mitochondrial genes *rps2* and *rps11* in angiosperms, which showed phylogenetic distributions inconsistent with vertical inheritance [30].

## 5. HGT in Animals

### 5.1 Insects: The Dominant Animal Group for HGT

Insects, particularly those with intimate symbiotic relationships, are the best-studied animals for HGT. The majority of exogenous DNA inserts in insect genomes are derived from *Wolbachia* endosymbionts [12,31].

#### *Wolbachia*–Insect HGT

*Wolbachia* is an intracellular alphaproteobacterium that infects an estimated 20–70% of all insect species and is maternally inherited through the egg cytoplasm. This intimate association with the germline makes *Wolbachia* an ideal donor for heritable HGT [12,32].

- **Massive insertions**: In some insect species, nearly entire *Wolbachia* genomes (>1 Mb) have been found integrated into the host nuclear genome. In the beetle *Callosobruchus chinensis*, a large fragment of the *Wolbachia* genome was transferred to the X chromosome [33].
- **Functional transfers**: Some *Wolbachia*-derived genes are expressed in insects and may confer novel functions. For example, latrotoxin-related genes transferred from *Wolbachia* to *Halyomorpha halys* (brown marmorated stink bug) and *Aedes aegypti* (mosquito) may be involved in host cell manipulation [34].
- **Widespread occurrence**: A survey of 20 insect genomes found evidence of *Wolbachia*-to-host HGT in dipterans and hymenopterans, including *Drosophila ananassae* and *Nasonia* species [35].

#### Sap-Feeding Insects and Endosymbiont Gene Transfer

Sap-feeding insects in the order Hemiptera (aphids, whiteflies, psyllids, mealybugs) harbor ancient bacterial endosymbionts that provide essential nutrients missing from their phloem diet. These endosymbionts have undergone extreme genome reduction, and many of their genes have been horizontally transferred to the host insect genome [13,36].

- In the psyllid *Pachypsylla venusta*, genes have been acquired from both its current endosymbiont *Carsonella* and from historical endosymbionts, revealing a "fossil record" of past symbionts [37].
- These transferred genes often encode enzymes for the biosynthesis of essential amino acids and vitamins, allowing the host to maintain control over metabolic pathways that were once performed by the endosymbiont [13,36].

#### The Whitefly: A Plant-to-Insect HGT Case

The sweet potato whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*) is a globally devastating agricultural pest that feeds on more than 600 plant species. In a landmark 2021 study published in *Cell*, Xia et al. demonstrated that the whitefly has acquired at least **49 plant-derived genes** through at least 24 independent HGT events [38,39].

The most well-characterized of these is the *BtPMaT1* gene, which encodes a phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase. This enzyme detoxifies phenolic glycosides — the major class of plant chemical defenses — by malonylation. This is the first confirmed example of a functional gene transferred from a plant to an insect [38,39].

The implications are profound:
- The whitefly uses a plant's own genetic toolkit to neutralize plant defenses.
- When researchers silenced *BtPMaT1* using RNA interference, whitefly mortality increased dramatically, suggesting a potential new avenue for pest control.
- Additional whitefly HGTs include ribosome-inactivating proteins (RIPs) and other plant-pathogen interaction genes [39].

### 5.2 Nematodes: HGT as a Catalyst for Plant Parasitism

Plant-parasitic nematodes are among the most economically destructive agricultural pests. Their ability to infect plants is built on a foundation of horizontally acquired genes [4,5,40].

- **Cell wall–degrading enzymes (CWDEs)**: The first HGT cases in plant-parasitic nematodes were genes encoding cellulases, pectate lyases, polygalacturonases, and expansin-like proteins — all derived from bacteria and fungi. The genome of the root-knot nematode *Meloidogyne incognita* contains more than 60 genes from six different protein families for degrading plant cell walls [40].
- **Other adaptations**: HGT has also provided genes for modulating plant defense systems, establishing feeding sites, and synthesizing or processing nutrients. For example, the cyanase gene in *Trichinella* was acquired from a plant or fungus and enables survival inside mammalian cells by detoxifying cyanate [41].
- **Convergent evolution**: Multiple independent HGT events of the same functional genes have occurred in different nematode lineages, supporting the idea that these transfers are strongly adaptive [5].

### 5.3 Bdelloid Rotifers: The Champions of HGT

Bdelloid rotifers are microscopic aquatic animals that have been called "genetic pirates" because of their extraordinary capacity for HGT. Approximately **8–10% of their active genes** originate from bacteria, fungi, and plants [2,3,42].

Key features of HGT in bdelloid rotifers:

- **Massive scale**: The proportion of foreign genes in bdelloids is higher than in any other animal lineage.
- **Mechanism**: The rotifers' ability to survive extreme desiccation (anhydrobiosis) is central to their HGT capacity. When they dry out, their chromosomes break into fragments; upon rehydration, their highly efficient DNA repair machinery stitches the genome back together, sometimes incorporating foreign DNA present in the environment [43].
- **Functionality**: Many horizontally acquired genes are expressed and appear to be functional. For example, bacterial genes encoding trehalose-6-phosphate synthase contribute to desiccation tolerance, and horizontally acquired biosynthetic genes are deployed against fungal pathogens [44,45].
- **Evolutionary history**: HGT in bdelloids is ancient and ongoing, with new transfers occurring approximately once every 100,000 years. The process has been happening for tens of millions of years, leading to the accumulation of hundreds of foreign genes [3].

### 5.4 HGT in Vertebrates: Rarer but Real

Vertebrates have traditionally been considered the group least likely to experience HGT due to the early sequestration of their germline. However, genomic analyses have revealed more transfers than previously thought, particularly in aquatic lineages.

- **Transposable element transfers**: A 2020 study of 307 vertebrate genomes identified more than 975 independent transfers of transposable elements among vertebrates, with the vast majority occurring in fishes and other aquatic vertebrates [9]. The prevalence in aquatic species may be linked to external fertilization, which exposes gametes to environmental DNA, and to the aquatic medium facilitating DNA transport.
- **Syncytin genes**: Perhaps the most famous vertebrate HGT involves the capture of a retroviral envelope gene (syncytin) that is essential for placental development in mammals [19]. This gene, derived from an endogenous retrovirus, enables cell-cell fusion in the placenta and is a dramatic example of domestication of a horizontally acquired element.
- **Tardigrades**: Extensive HGT has been reported in tardigrades (water bears), with claims that up to 17.5% of their genes are foreign. However, some of these claims have been disputed as artifacts of contamination, and the topic remains controversial [46].

### 5.5 HGT in Other Animals

- **Mites**: The two-spotted spider mite (*Tetranychus urticae*) has acquired a bacterial gene for cyanide detoxification, which likely enabled colonization of cyanogenic host plants [47].
- **Lepidoptera**: Monarch butterflies and silkworms have been genetically modified by HGT from wasp bracoviruses, with viral genes integrated into the insect genome [48].
- **Adzuki bean beetle**: Has acquired genetic material from its (non-beneficial) endosymbiont *Wolbachia* [12].
- **Sea slug (*Elysia chlorotica*)**: An early high-profile claim of HGT from an alga to a sea slug, which was thought to explain the slug's ability to perform photosynthesis using stolen chloroplasts. However, subsequent genome sequencing found no trace of algal nuclear genes in the slug genome, and this case is now considered an artifact [49].

## 6. Evolutionary Significance of HGT in Eukaryotes

### 6.1 Adaptive Innovation and Niche Expansion

The most compelling theme emerging from the study of HGT in plants and animals is that horizontally acquired genes often provide **critical adaptive functions** that enable recipients to exploit new ecological niches:

- **Herbivory**: The acquisition of plant cell wall–degrading enzymes by nematodes and insects via HGT was a key innovation that allowed these animals to feed on plants [4,5,40].
- **Detoxification**: The whitefly's acquisition of a plant detoxification gene neutralizes the plant's chemical defenses, enabling extreme polyphagy [38,39].
- **Pathogen resistance**: The *Fhb7* gene in wheat provides resistance to a devastating fungal disease [26,27].
- **Desiccation tolerance**: Bdelloid rotifers use horizontally acquired genes to survive in ephemeral, drying habitats [44].
- **Parasitism**: Parasitic plants have acquired dozens of genes from their hosts that enhance their ability to extract nutrients and suppress host defenses [8,23].

### 6.2 HGT and Major Evolutionary Transitions

HGT has been implicated in several of the most important transitions in eukaryotic evolution:

- **Origin of photosynthesis (primary endosymbiosis)**: The acquisition of a cyanobacterium by a phagotrophic protist led to the first plastid, followed by massive endosymbiotic gene transfer (EGT) from the endosymbiont to the host nuclear genome. This process was essential for integrating plastid function with host cell biology [50].
- **Colonization of land by plants**: HGT from bacteria and fungi provided genes for desiccation tolerance, UV protection, lignin synthesis, and pathogen defense — all critical for survival on land [7,20,22].
- **Evolution of placental mammals**: The capture of a retroviral envelope gene (syncytin) was essential for the evolution of the placenta [19].
- **Evolution of parasitism in plants**: HGT has been a driving force in the evolution of parasitic plants, with genes acquired from hosts contributing to haustorium development and host manipulation [8,23].

### 6.3 HGT as an Evolutionary Ratchet

The "you are what you eat" hypothesis, formalized by W. Ford Doolittle, proposes that the constant exposure of eukaryotic genomes to foreign DNA through phagocytosis, endosymbiosis, and parasitism sets up an evolutionary ratchet that leads to the inevitable replacement of genes and addition of new functions [18,51]. While this model was originally developed for unicellular eukaryotes, the principle extends to multicellular organisms that engage in intimate trophic or symbiotic relationships with other species.

### 6.4 The Changing View of Eukaryotic HGT

The field has undergone a paradigm shift over the past 15 years. As noted by Van Etten and Bhattacharya (2020): "The time is ripe to move beyond the debate 'if HGT' to focusing on its adaptive and evolutionary implications" [1]. The key factors that have driven this change include:

- **The explosion of genome sequencing data**, which has made it possible to detect HGT with statistical rigor.
- **Long-read sequencing technologies**, which provide the genomic context needed to distinguish genuine HGT from contamination or assembly artifacts [9].
- **Improved phylogenetic methods**, including the ability to distinguish HGT from lineage sorting, convergent evolution, and gene loss.
- **Functional validation studies**, such as the demonstration that the whitefly *BtPMaT1* gene is actively transcribed, translated, and functional in detoxification [38].

Nevertheless, skepticism remains, particularly in the animal genome community, and rigorous standards are essential to avoid false positives from contamination, poor assemblies, or database errors [9,52].

## 7. Outstanding Questions and Future Directions

1. **How common is HGT in different animal and plant groups?** Systematic analyses across diverse lineages, especially those that are under-sampled, are needed to quantify the frequency and impact of HGT.
2. **What are the exact molecular mechanisms?** The discovery of Maverick/Polinton elements as vectors of HGT in nematodes is a major advance, but the mechanisms in other groups remain unclear.
3. **Do horizontally acquired genes have specific sequence signatures or functional biases?** Some studies suggest that foreign genes tend to be small, lack introns, or encode secreted proteins, but comprehensive analyses are lacking.
4. **What is the long-term fate of horizontally acquired genes?** Many may be lost or become pseudogenes; what determines whether they are retained and functionalized?
5. **Can HGT be harnessed for biotechnology?** The *Fhb7* story demonstrates the agricultural potential; could HGT-based approaches be used for pest control, stress tolerance, or other applications?
6. **How does HGT interact with sexual reproduction, population genetics, and speciation?** The transfer of genetic incompatibility factors via HGT may have implications for speciation [14].

## 8. Conclusion

Horizontal gene transfer in plants and animals — once dismissed as a rare curiosity — is now recognized as a small but significant force in eukaryotic evolution. The most compelling cases involve organisms that live in close physical or symbiotic association with other species: parasitic plants, sap-feeding insects, endosymbiont-harboring nematodes, and desiccation-tolerant rotifers. In each case, the horizontally acquired genes provide functions that are otherwise unavailable to the recipient, enabling the colonization of new niches, the evolution of parasitism, and the acquisition of novel metabolic capabilities. The discovery of mechanisms such as *Wolbachia*-mediated transfer, Maverick transposons, and haustorial DNA exchange is beginning to demystify how these rare events occur. As genome sequencing continues to expand across the tree of life, we can expect to uncover many more examples and to refine our understanding of the conditions under which genes can leap across species boundaries — and the profound evolutionary consequences when they do.
