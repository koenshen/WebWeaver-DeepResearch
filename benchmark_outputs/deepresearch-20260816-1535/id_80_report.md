

# Research Report: The Influence of Mass Media on Language – Shoujo Manga Consumption, Pronoun Use, and Sentence-Final Particles Among Queer Young Adults in Japan

## 1. Introduction

Japanese is a language in which gender is deeply encoded through grammatical and lexical choices. Two of the most salient gendered features are **first-person pronouns** (e.g., *watashi*, *boku*, *ore*, *atashi*) and **sentence-final particles (SFPs)** (e.g., *wa*, *zo*, *ze*, *na*, *no*). These features are not rigidly fixed but are actively negotiated by speakers, especially within minority communities such as the LGBTQ+ population. In parallel, **shōjo manga** (girls’ comics) has long been a site of gender play, featuring characters who deploy non-normative pronoun choices and a rich palette of SFPs. This report investigates the empirical question: *Does consumption of shōjo manga by queer Japanese young adults correlate with measurable differences in their pronoun use and sentence-final particle choices?*

## 2. Gendered Language in Japanese: Pronouns and Sentence-Final Particles

### 2.1 First-Person Pronouns

Japanese has a wide inventory of first-person pronouns, each carrying gendered, social, and register connotations. The most common, arranged from stereotypically feminine to masculine, are:

| Pronoun | Stereotypical Gender | Connotation |
|---------|----------------------|-------------|
| *atashi* (あたし) | Feminine | Cute, sometimes immature; used in *onee kotoba* |
| *watashi* (私) | Neutral/feminine | Standard polite form; used by all genders in formal settings |
| *jibun* (自分) | Neutral | Can be used as a gender-neutral statement |
| *boku* (僕) | Masculine | Used by boys, young men; also used by tomboyish girls |
| *ore* (俺) | Masculine | Rough, assertive; used in casual contexts by men |

Sources: Tofugu (2023), Tokyo Weekender (2023)

The choice of first-person pronoun is one of the most immediately noticeable markers of a speaker’s performed gender identity. Unlike English, where the speaker is referred to by others, in Japanese the speaker **chooses** their own pronoun, making it a powerful tool for identity construction.

### 2.2 Sentence-Final Particles

SFPs in Japanese carry information about the speaker’s gender, assertiveness, and emotional stance. The following table summarises the gendered associations:

| Particle | Gender Association | Function |
|----------|-------------------|----------|
| *wa* (わ) | Feminine (rising) / Neutral (falling) | Soft assertion, emotional emphasis |
| *no* (の) | Feminine/Neutral | Soft explanation, question marker |
| *zo* (ぞ) | Masculine | Strong assertion, warning |
| *ze* (ぜ) | Masculine | Emphatic, casual assertion |
| *na* (な) | Masculine | Self-reflection, prohibition |
| *sa* (さ) | Masculine/Neutral | Light emphasis, filler |
| *yo* (よ) | Neutral | Information, emphasis |
| *ne* (ね) | Neutral | Agreement, confirmation |

Source: Tofugu (2023), Migaku (2024)

## 3. Shōjo Manga as a Linguistic Space

### 3.1 Historical Context

Shōjo manga emerged in the early 20th century and was revolutionised in the 1970s by the **Year 24 Group** (Moto Hagio, Yumiko Ōshima, Keiko Takemiya, and others), who introduced philosophical, sci-fi, and queer themes. The genre has been a space for gender experimentation, featuring cross-dressing protagonists, androgynous *bishōnen*, and characters who use non-normative pronouns (e.g., *Utena* from *Revolutionary Girl Utena* using *boku*). 

Sources: Wikipedia – LGBTQ themes in anime and manga; NYPL (2019)

### 3.2 Linguistic Patterns in Shōjo Manga

Research by Unser-Schutz (2015) analysed a corpus of 10 popular manga series (including both shōjo and shōnen) and found:

- **Personal pronoun usage** in shōjo manga was **generally normative** (i.e., female characters used *watashi/atashi*, male characters used *boku/ore*), with only occasional deviations.
- **Sentence-final particles** showed **greater variability**: all genders used a mix of gendered SFPs, suggesting that SFPs are more flexible markers of character identity.
- Contrary to popular claims, the study concluded that **manga tend to reflect already-existing linguistic changes rather than driving them**. However, the perception of manga as "low" texts may contribute to their association with negative linguistic change.

Source: Unser-Schutz (2015), *Influential or influenced? The relationship between genre, gender and language in manga*, Gender & Language 9(2), 223–254.

### 3.3 Distinctive Features of Shōjo Manga Language

- **Use of *atashi***: Hyper-feminine pronoun often used by female leads and also by *onee kotoba* characters.
- **Use of *boku*** by female characters: Common in tomboy or gender-bending narratives (e.g., *The Rose of Versailles*, *Revolutionary Girl Utena*).
- **SFP *wa***: Appears with high frequency, often with rising intonation, marking a character as feminine.
- **SFP *no***: Used for soft explanation, perceived as feminine.
- **Minimal use of rough masculine SFPs (**zo, ze**) in shōjo compared to shōnen.

Source: Unser-Schutz (2015); Kloučková (dissertation, 2020)

## 4. Queer Japanese Language Practices

### 4.1 *Onee Kotoba* (オネエことば)

*Onee kotoba* (literally "older sister speech") is a stylised speech variety associated with queer men and transgender women in Japan. Its key features include:

- **Hyper-feminine pronouns** (especially *atashi*)
- **Feminine SFPs** (*wa* with rising intonation, *no*, *wa yo*)
- **Crude/vulgar vocabulary** (e.g., *kuu* "to eat" instead of *taberu*)
- **Direct, assertive**, and sometimes sexually explicit content

Example from linguistic research (cited in Tofugu):
*あたし、今カレー食ったら下痢だわ。*
*"If I ate curry right now, I'd have diarrhea."*  
This sentence uses the feminine pronoun *atashi*, the feminine SFP *wa*, but also the crude verb *kutta* (ate) and a taboo topic—a classic *onee kotoba* blend.

Sources: Tofugu (2023); Maree (2020), *queerqueen: Linguistic Excess in Japanese Media*

### 4.2 Queer Community Pronoun Strategies

Research by Abe (2010) and Bendall (2022) highlights several patterns:

- **Lesbian women** in Abe's 2010 study used more masculine pronouns in certain contexts, though Bendall's 2022 replication using the documentary *Queer Japan* (2019) found this pattern was **less pronounced a decade later**.
- **Gay men** may use *atashi* or *watashi* in casual contexts, but not all do.
- **Non-binary individuals** often choose *jibun* (自分) as a gender-neutral alternative, or use *watashi* in professional settings to avoid marking.
- **Situational pronoun switching**: In heated arguments, queer women who normally use *watashi* or *boku* may switch to *ore* to express anger, regardless of gender identity.

Sources: Abe (2010), *Queer Japanese: Gender and Sexual Identities through Linguistic Practices*; Bendall (2022), *QUEER JAPANESE. The modern-day language usage of Japanese LGBTQ+ people* (University of Gothenburg)

### 4.3 Sentence-Final Particles in Queer Communities

Dahlberg-Dodd (2022) conducted a quantitative phonetic analysis of SFPs in character speech, including *onee kotoba* speakers. Key findings:

- ***Onee* characters** use SFP *wa* with **significantly longer duration** and **rising intonation** compared to other character types.
- **Gyaru characters** also use lengthened SFPs, but with different intonation patterns.
- This suggests that *onee kotoba* speakers are not simply "copying" feminine speech but are actively **stylising and exaggerating** it, creating a distinct queer register.

Source: Dahlberg-Dodd (2022), *On the Interaction between Japanese Sentence-final Particle wa and Fundamental Frequency: A Characterological Perspective*, Ohio State University

## 5. The Intersection: Shōjo Manga Consumption and Queer Identity

### 5.1 Direct Evidence of Linguistic Influence

The academic literature does **not** yet contain a dedicated, peer-reviewed study that directly measures the effect of shōjo manga consumption on the pronoun/SFP choices of queer Japanese young adults. However, multiple lines of evidence converge to suggest a plausible pathway of influence:

1. **Self-reported influence**: Many queer readers (especially in online communities) report that shōjo manga provided them with a **linguistic template** for expressing gender non-conformity. The genre's history of gender-bending protagonists (e.g., *Utena*, *Oscar* from *The Rose of Versailles*, *Haruhi* from *Ouran High School Host Club*) offers models of non-normative pronoun use.

2. **Media as a resource for identity construction**: Sociolinguistic theory (e.g., Eckert's "communities of practice") views language as a resource that speakers draw upon to perform identity. For queer youth who may lack real-life models, **media becomes a key source of linguistic resources**.

3. **The *onee kotoba* / shōjo manga connection**: *Onee kotoba* itself draws heavily on the linguistic style of *ojōsama kotoba* (young lady speech) as depicted in manga and anime. Maree (2020) documents how *onee* characters in Japanese media use language that is simultaneously a parody and a homage to the hyper-feminine speech found in shōjo manga.

### 5.2 Indirect Evidence

- **Unser-Schutz (2015)** found that while manga do not necessarily *cause* linguistic change, they **reflect and amplify** existing trends. For queer speakers, shōjo manga may serve as a **validation** of non-normative language use rather than its origin.
- **Kloučková (2020)** compared the speech of real teenage girls with shōjo manga characters and found that shōjo manga characters used **more traditionally feminine forms** than real girls, suggesting that manga may be **conservative** in some respects rather than innovative.
- **Wang (2023)** studied unconventional use of "female" SFPs *wa* and *no* by male speakers in youth conversations, finding that young people are increasingly using these SFPs regardless of gender, a trend that may be **amplified by media exposure** but is not exclusively caused by it.

### 5.3 A Gap in the Literature

The specific question—*Does shōjo manga consumption affect pronoun/SFP use in queer Japanese young adults?*—remains **unresolved by direct empirical research**. The existing studies focus on either:

- **General youth populations** (not specifically queer)
- **Queer populations** without controlling for media consumption
- **Media content** without measuring reader uptake

A study that combines **survey/interview data on manga consumption** with **naturalistic speech data** from queer Japanese young adults (e.g., through sociolinguistic interviews or social media corpus analysis) would be needed to provide a definitive answer.

## 6. Synthesis and Hypotheses

Based on the available evidence, the following hypotheses can be proposed for future research:

| Hypothesis | Supporting Evidence | Evidence Level |
|------------|--------------------|----------------|
| H1: Queer young adults who consume shōjo manga are more likely to use *atashi* or *boku* as first-person pronouns. | Shōjo manga features these pronouns prominently; *onee kotoba* draws on shōjo manga styles. | Moderate (indirect) |
| H2: Queer young adults who consume shōjo manga use more diverse SFP inventories, including *wa* and *no*. | Shōjo manga characters use a wide range of SFPs; queer communities show similar patterns. | Moderate (indirect) |
| H3: The influence of shōjo manga is strongest for those who lack real-life queer peers. | Media serves as a substitute community of practice for isolated individuals. | Theoretical (not yet tested) |
| H4: Shōjo manga consumption correlates with use of *onee kotoba* features among queer men. | *Onee kotoba* lexicon overlaps significantly with shōjo manga language. | Strong (qualitative) |

---

## 7. Conclusion

The question of whether shōjo manga consumption affects pronoun and SFP use among queer Japanese young adults is a compelling one, situated at the intersection of **sociolinguistics, queer studies, and media studies**. The current evidence base is suggestive but not conclusive:

- **Yes**, shōjo manga offers a distinct linguistic repertoire that includes non-normative pronoun choices and a wide range of gendered SFPs.
- **Yes**, queer Japanese speakers (particularly those using *onee kotoba*) draw on hyper-feminine language that overlaps with shōjo manga styles.
- **However**, no study has yet directly measured the causal or correlational link between consumption of shōjo manga and individual linguistic behaviour in queer populations.

Future research should employ a mixed-methods approach: **quantitative corpus analysis** of queer speakers' natural language (e.g., from social media or interviews), combined with **self-report surveys** on manga consumption habits, to determine whether the linguistic patterns found in shōjo manga are adopted by queer readers. Until such a study is conducted, the claim that shōjo manga "affects" queer language use remains a plausible but unproven hypothesis.
