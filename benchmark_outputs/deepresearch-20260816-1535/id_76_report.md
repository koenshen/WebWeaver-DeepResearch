

# The Gut Microbiota in Intestinal Health and Colorectal Cancer: A Comprehensive Research Report

## 1. Introduction

The human gut microbiota comprises trillions of microorganisms that play a fundamental role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, modulating immune function, and influencing host metabolism. Disruption of this delicate microbial ecosystem—a state termed dysbiosis—has been increasingly linked to chronic intestinal inflammation and the pathogenesis of colorectal cancer (CRC). Conversely, the strategic use of probiotics and prebiotics has emerged as a promising avenue for mitigating inflammation and retarding CRC progression. This report synthesizes current evidence on the predominant types of gut probiotics, the mechanistic role of prebiotics, the pathogenic bacteria of concern and their toxic metabolites, and the practical dietary implications of these findings.

---

## 2. Predominant Types of Gut Probiotics

Probiotics are defined by the World Health Organization as "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host." The most common and extensively studied probiotic genera are **Lactobacillus**, **Bifidobacterium**, and **Saccharomyces** (a beneficial yeast). Among these, *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* species are the most widely used in both research and commercial products.

### 2.1. Lactobacillus

Lactobacilli are Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic or microaerophilic rods that naturally inhabit the human gastrointestinal tract, oral cavity, and vagina. Key species include:

- **Lactobacillus acidophilus** – perhaps the best-known probiotic; found naturally in the mouth, gut, and vagina; helps restore microbial balance across multiple body sites.
- **Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG)** – one of the most thoroughly researched strains, with documented efficacy in preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhea and reducing gastrointestinal discomfort.
- **Lactobacillus casei** – often used in fermented dairy products; supports immune function.
- **Lactobacillus plantarum** – versatile strain found in many fermented plant foods; produces antimicrobial compounds.
- **Lactobacillus reuteri** – known for producing the antimicrobial substance reuterin.

*Lactobacillus* species primarily colonize the small intestine and contribute to lowering luminal pH through lactic acid production, inhibiting pathogen growth, and enhancing mineral bioavailability.

### 2.2. Bifidobacterium

Bifidobacteria are Gram-positive, anaerobic bacteria that dominate the infant gut, especially in breastfed newborns, and remain important throughout life. Key species include:

- **Bifidobacterium longum** – prevalent in the adult colon; associated with immune modulation and pathogen exclusion.
- **Bifidobacterium breve** – common in infants; supports intestinal barrier integrity.
- **Bifidobacterium bifidum** – produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and antimicrobial peptides.
- **Bifidobacterium lactis** (also classified as *B. animalis* subsp. *lactis*) – widely used in commercial probiotics; demonstrates efficacy in relieving constipation and supporting digestive health.
- **Bifidobacterium infantis** – important for the developing infant gut microbiome.

Bifidobacteria primarily work in the large intestine, forming a microbial barrier that protects against undesirable substances and interfering with pathogen adhesion to the intestinal lining.

### 2.3. Other Notable Probiotic Organisms

- **Saccharomyces boulardii** – a non-pathogenic yeast that is resistant to antibiotics; has demonstrated benefit in preventing and treating antibiotic-associated diarrhea and *Clostridioides difficile* infection.
- **Bacillus coagulans** and **Bacillus subtilis** – spore-forming bacteria with high stability; used in many probiotic supplements.
- **Streptococcus thermophilus** – commonly used in yogurt fermentation; produces lactase, aiding lactose digestion.
- **Enterococcus faecium** – used in some probiotic formulations, though its safety profile requires careful evaluation in immunocompromised individuals.

### 2.4. Food Sources of Probiotics

Probiotic-containing foods include:

- **Yogurt** (with live and active cultures)
- **Kefir** – a fermented milk drink containing multiple bacterial and yeast strains
- **Sauerkraut** (unpasteurized)
- **Kimchi** – a Korean fermented vegetable dish
- **Miso** – fermented soybean paste
- **Tempeh** – fermented soybean cake
- **Kombucha** – fermented tea
- **Pickled vegetables** (naturally fermented, not vinegar-pickled)

It is important to note that processing methods such as heating, baking, or pasteurization can kill live microorganisms. Products labeled as "contains live and active cultures" are more likely to provide viable probiotics.

## 3. Prebiotics: Definition and Mechanistic Role

### 3.1. Definition

Prebiotics are defined as "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit." This definition, updated by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), expands the original concept beyond carbohydrates to include other substances, and beyond the gut to other body sites. Most prebiotics, however, are non-digestible carbohydrates—primarily dietary fibers and oligosaccharides—that resist digestion in the small intestine and reach the colon intact, where they serve as fermentable substrates for beneficial bacteria.

### 3.2. Mechanisms of Action

The health effects of prebiotics are multifaceted and involve several interrelated mechanisms:

**1. Selective Stimulation of Beneficial Microbes:** Prebiotics are preferentially fermented by health-promoting bacteria such as *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus*, leading to increased abundance of these genera. This "bifidogenic effect" is the most consistently documented action of prebiotics.

**2. Production of Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs):** Bacterial fermentation of prebiotics yields SCFAs, primarily **acetate, propionate, and butyrate**. Butyrate is of particular importance for colorectal health:
- It serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes (colon epithelial cells).
- It strengthens the intestinal barrier by promoting tight junction integrity.
- It exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by modulating nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production.
- It induces apoptosis (programmed cell death) in cancerous cells and inhibits cell proliferation.
- It promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation, supporting immune homeostasis.

**3. Reduction of Luminal pH:** The production of organic acids (lactic acid, acetic acid) during fermentation lowers the colonic pH, which inhibits the growth of pH-sensitive pathogenic bacteria such as *Clostridium perfringens* and *Escherichia coli* strains.

**4. Enhanced Mineral Absorption:** SCFAs and the acidification of the colonic lumen increase the solubility of minerals such as calcium, magnesium, and iron, thereby improving their absorption.

**5. Immune Modulation:** Prebiotics and their metabolites influence immune function through multiple pathways, including modulation of toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, enhancement of secretory IgA production, and regulation of cytokine profiles.

**6. Pathogen Exclusion:** Prebiotics can interfere with the adhesion of pathogenic bacteria to the intestinal epithelium by occupying binding sites or by stimulating mucin production, which strengthens the mucus barrier.

### 3.3. Major Types of Prebiotics

| Prebiotic Type | Examples | Food Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Inulin-type fructans | Inulin, fructooligosaccharides (FOS) | Chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, garlic, onion, leeks, asparagus, bananas |
| Galactooligosaccharides (GOS) | Trans-galactooligosaccharides | Legumes, human milk (human milk oligosaccharides), some dairy products |
| Resistant starch | Starch that escapes small-intestinal digestion | Cooked and cooled potatoes, green bananas, legumes, whole grains |
| Xylooligosaccharides (XOS) | Xylan-derived oligomers | Fruits, vegetables, bamboo shoots |
| Pectin | Soluble fiber | Apples, citrus fruits, carrots, berries |
| Beta-glucans | Mixed-linkage polysaccharides | Oats, barley, mushrooms |
| Polyphenols | Secondary plant metabolites | Berries, tea, coffee, red wine, dark chocolate, cocoa |

## 4. Pathogenic Gut Bacteria and Their Toxic Metabolites

A growing body of evidence has identified specific pathogenic bacteria that are enriched in the gut microbiota of individuals with CRC. These bacteria promote carcinogenesis through the production of genotoxins, pro-inflammatory metabolites, and other virulence factors.

### 4.1. Fusobacterium nucleatum

*Fusobacterium nucleatum* is a Gram-negative, anaerobic bacterium that has been strongly associated with CRC. Its pathogenic mechanisms include:

- **FadA adhesin:** Binds to E-cadherin on epithelial cells, activating β-catenin signaling and promoting cell proliferation.
- **Fap2 protein:** Mediates immune evasion by inhibiting natural killer (NK) cell and T-cell activity.
- **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) production:** *F. nucleatum* generates H₂S, a genotoxic gas that induces DNA damage, disrupts the autophagy process, and alters gut microbiota composition, promoting CRC progression.
- **ADP-heptose secretion:** A pro-inflammatory metabolite that upregulates PD-L1 expression, contributing to immune checkpoint activation and potential immunotherapy resistance.
- **Succinate and formate:** Metabolic byproducts that modulate local immune responses, altering immune cell function and polarization.

### 4.2. Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF)

*Bacteroides fragilis* is a common commensal, but enterotoxigenic strains (ETBF) that harbor the *bft* gene produce **Bacteroides fragilis toxin (BFT)**, a 20 kDa zinc-dependent metalloprotease. Recent research (2026) has revealed that BFT must first bind to the host receptor claudin-4 before it can cleave E-cadherin, disrupt epithelial barrier integrity, and activate pro-inflammatory pathways (NF-κB, STAT3, Wnt/β-catenin). This cascade leads to:
- Chronic inflammation
- Enhanced epithelial proliferation
- DNA damage
- Promotion of colon tumor formation

ETBF can be detected in up to 20% of healthy individuals, underscoring the importance of host-microbe interactions in determining cancer risk.

### 4.3. pks+ Escherichia coli

Certain strains of *E. coli* (particularly those belonging to the B2 phylogroup) carry a genomic island known as the *pks* island, which encodes the enzymatic machinery to produce **colibactin**. Colibactin is a genotoxic polyketide-nonribosomal peptide that:
- Covalently alkylates DNA, forming DNA adducts and interstrand cross-links.
- Induces double-stranded DNA breaks and ATR-dependent replication stress.
- Leaves a distinct mutational signature found in approximately one in eight colon tumors.
- Promotes cancer stemness, therapy resistance, and immune suppression.

Recent evidence suggests that colibactin-induced DNA changes are more common in younger adults with early-onset bowel cancer, pointing to a possible contributor to the rising incidence of CRC in younger populations.

### 4.4. Clostridioides difficile

*Clostridioides difficile*, well known for causing antibiotic-associated diarrhea, has been identified as a potential driver of colorectal cancer. Its toxin **TcdB** (toxin B) is the primary culprit:
- TcdB induces inflammatory and carcinogenic pathways in colon cells.
- It modulates mucosal immunity and disrupts epithelial barrier function.
- Mouse models show that toxigenic *C. difficile* alone is sufficient to promote colon tumor formation.
- Epidemiological studies have found that individuals with recurrent *C. difficile* positivity have a significantly increased risk of incident colorectal cancer.

### 4.5. Sulfate-Reducing Bacteria (SRB) and Hydrogen Sulfide

Sulfate-reducing bacteria, including **Desulfovibrio**, **Bilophila wadsworthia**, and **Desulfomicrobium** species, metabolize dietary sulfur compounds (inorganic sulfate and organic sulfur from taurine, cysteine, and methionine) to produce **hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)**. H₂S is a genotoxic and pro-inflammatory gas that:
- Induces DNA damage and genomic instability.
- Triggers hyper-proliferation and pro-inflammatory signaling pathways.
- Disrupts colonocyte energy metabolism.
- Is associated with increased risk of CRC, particularly distal CRC.

A "sulfur microbial diet"—high in red and processed meat, low-calorie beverages, and animal fat—promotes the expansion of sulfidogenic bacteria and is associated with an elevated CRC risk (hazard ratio 1.27).

### 4.6. Other Pathogenic Bacteria and Their Metabolites

| Pathogen | Key Metabolite/Toxin | Mechanism of Pathogenicity |
|---|---|---|
| *Enterococcus faecalis* | Superoxide, hydrogen peroxide | Induces oxidative DNA damage in colonocytes |
| *Peptostreptococcus anaerobius* | Indole derivatives | Promotes inflammation and epithelial proliferation |
| *Clostridium* species (clusters XIVa, XI) | Secondary bile acids, especially deoxycholic acid (DCA) | DCA is a tumor promoter; induces DNA damage, apoptosis resistance, and inflammation |
| *Campylobacter jejuni* | Cytolethal distending toxin (CDT) | Causes DNA double-strand breaks |

### 4.7. Summary of Detrimental Metabolites

| Metabolite Class | Examples | Source Bacteria | Carcinogenic Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genotoxins | Colibactin, BFT, CDT | *pks+ E. coli*, ETBF, *Campylobacter* | DNA alkylation, cross-links, double-strand breaks |
| Sulfur compounds | Hydrogen sulfide | SRB (*Desulfovibrio*, *Bilophila*), *F. nucleatum* | DNA damage, inflammation, hyper-proliferation |
| Secondary bile acids | Deoxycholic acid (DCA) | *Clostridium*, *Bacteroides* | DNA damage, ROS generation, apoptosis resistance |
| N-nitroso compounds | N-nitrosamines, N-nitrosamides | Colonic bacteria (fermentation of red/processed meat) | DNA alkylation, potent carcinogens |
| Heterocyclic amines | HCAs | Gut microbiota (from meat fermentation) | DNA-reactive metabolites; *Bacteroides* strains convert HCAs into carcinogens |
| Polyamines | Putrescine, spermidine, spermine | *F. nucleatum*, ETBF, *Bacteroides* | Cell proliferation, oxidative stress |
| Trimethylamine N-oxide | TMAO | Gut microbiota (from dietary choline, carnitine) | Promotes inflammation, enhances CRC risk |

## 5. Dietary Optimization: Translating Research into Daily Choices

The interplay between diet, gut microbiota, and CRC risk offers a powerful opportunity for prevention. The following dietary strategies are supported by robust evidence.

### 5.1. Increase Dietary Fiber and Prebiotic Intake

A high-fiber diet is one of the most effective dietary interventions for reducing CRC risk. The mechanisms include:
- **SCFA production, especially butyrate:** SCFAs provide fuel for colonocytes, strengthen the intestinal barrier, and induce apoptosis in cancer cells.
- **Reduced transit time:** Fiber increases stool bulk and accelerates bowel transit, reducing the duration of exposure of the colonic epithelium to carcinogens.
- **Inhibition of secondary bile acid synthesis:** High-fiber, low-fat diets shift the microbiota toward saccharolytic fermentation, reducing the production of tumor-promoting secondary bile acids.
- **Reduction of TMAO:** Dietary fiber can inhibit intestinal microbial TMA lyase activity, reducing TMAO production.

**Practical recommendations:**
- Aim for **25–35 grams of dietary fiber per day** (most adults consume less than 15 g/day).
- Include a variety of fiber sources: whole grains (oats, barley, quinoa, brown rice), legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas), fruits (berries, apples, bananas, pears), vegetables (broccoli, carrots, leafy greens), nuts, and seeds.
- Incorporate prebiotic-rich foods daily: **garlic, onions, leeks, asparagus, Jerusalem artichokes, chicory root, bananas, oats, apples, and flaxseeds**.

### 5.2. Include Probiotic and Fermented Foods

Fermented foods can introduce live beneficial microbes to the gut, supporting microbial diversity and competing with pathogenic species.

**Practical recommendations:**
- Consume **yogurt with live and active cultures** (at least 10⁶ CFU/g).
- Include **kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut (unpasteurized), miso, tempeh, and kombucha** in the diet.
- Consider **probiotic supplements** containing *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* species, especially after antibiotic use, but consult a healthcare provider first, as not all products are of equal quality.

### 5.3. Limit Red and Processed Meat

Red and processed meat consumption is a well-established risk factor for CRC. The mechanisms are linked to:
- Heme iron, which catalyzes the formation of N-nitroso compounds (NOCs) in the gut.
- Sulfur-containing amino acids (taurine, cysteine) that fuel H₂S production by SRB.
- High fat content, which stimulates bile acid secretion and secondary bile acid formation.

**Practical recommendations:**
- Limit red meat consumption to **no more than 500 g (cooked weight) per week**.
- Avoid processed meats (bacon, sausages, ham, salami, hot dogs) as much as possible.
- When consuming meat, pair it with high-fiber vegetables to mitigate harmful effects.

### 5.4. Adopt a Mediterranean-Style Diet

The Mediterranean diet, characterized by high intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, and fish, with moderate consumption of poultry and dairy, and limited red meat, is consistently associated with:
- Increased microbial diversity and richness.
- Higher abundance of beneficial bacteria (*Bifidobacterium*, *Lactobacillus*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*).
- Increased SCFA production.
- Reduced levels of pro-inflammatory bacteria and metabolites.

### 5.5. Avoid the "Sulfur Microbial Diet"

The "sulfur microbial diet" is a pattern characterized by high intake of red meat, processed meat, low-calorie beverages (with artificial sweeteners), and alcohol, which promotes the expansion of sulfidogenic bacteria. Long-term adherence to this dietary pattern is associated with a 27% increased risk of CRC.

**Practical recommendations:**
- Reduce consumption of red and processed meat.
- Limit alcohol intake.
- Choose water, herbal tea, or unsweetened beverages over artificially sweetened drinks.
- Increase intake of plant-based, sulfur-poor foods.

### 5.6. Consider Synbiotics

The combination of probiotics and prebiotics—termed **synbiotics**—may offer synergistic benefits. Prebiotics can improve the survival and colonization of co-administered probiotics, while the probiotics provide additional metabolic and protective functions. Some studies have shown that synbiotic supplementation can reduce the expression of genes involved in carcinogenic pathways and decrease levels of detrimental metabolites.

### 5.7. General Dietary Guidelines for Gut Health

| Food Category | Recommended | Limit/Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Fruits & Vegetables | ≥5 servings/day; variety of colors | — |
| Whole Grains | 3–5 servings/day (oats, quinoa, brown rice, whole wheat) | Refined grains (white bread, white rice, pasta) |
| Legumes | 2–3 servings/week (lentils, beans, chickpeas) | — |
| Fermented Foods | Yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, tempeh | Heated/pasteurized fermented products (no live cultures) |
| Protein | Fish, poultry, plant-based proteins (tofu, legumes) | Red meat (>500 g/week), processed meats (avoid) |
| Fats | Olive oil, avocados, nuts, seeds, fatty fish | Saturated fats, trans fats, fried foods |
| Beverages | Water, green tea, coffee (in moderation) | Sugary drinks, artificially sweetened beverages, excessive alcohol |

---

## 6. Conclusion

The gut microbiota occupies a central position at the intersection of diet, intestinal health, and colorectal cancer risk. Beneficial bacteria such as *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* species, supported by prebiotic fibers, produce SCFAs that nourish the colon, strengthen the intestinal barrier, and suppress inflammation. Conversely, pathogenic bacteria including *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis*, *pks+ Escherichia coli*, *Clostridioides difficile*, and sulfate-reducing bacteria produce genotoxic and pro-inflammatory metabolites that drive DNA damage, chronic inflammation, and carcinogenesis.

The evidence strongly supports a dietary pattern centered on high fiber intake, abundant prebiotic-rich plant foods, regular consumption of fermented foods, and limited red and processed meat—a pattern that promotes a resilient, anti-inflammatory gut microbiota. While the field continues to evolve, and the promise of personalized microbiome-based therapies looms on the horizon, the fundamental dietary principles for cultivating a healthy gut ecosystem are already well established.
