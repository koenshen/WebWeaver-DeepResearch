

# The Restructuring Impact of Artificial Intelligence on the Labor Market: A Literature Review

## 1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is widely regarded as a defining technology of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, a phase characterized by the fusion of digital, physical, and biological systems that is reshaping production, consumption, and employment at an unprecedented pace (Schwab, 2016, as cited in Doubková & Magdin, 2026). Unlike previous waves of automation that primarily targeted routine manual tasks, the new generation of AI—rooted in deep learning, natural language processing, and large language models—extends its reach into cognitive, non-routine domains that were once considered exclusively human (Wu et al., 2024; Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). This shift has generated intense scholarly debate about whether AI will lead to mass technological unemployment, wage polarization, or a fundamental restructuring of how labor is organized within firms and across industries.

This literature review synthesizes the growing body of high-quality, English-language research on the labor market impacts of AI, with a focus on the mechanisms of restructuring, displacement, complementarity, and skill transformation. It draws on theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence from leading economics, management, and policy journals to provide a comprehensive assessment of how AI is reshaping labor demand, wages, and the very structure of work.

## 2. Theoretical Frameworks: From Skill-Biased Technical Change to Task-Based Models

The canonical model of skill-biased technical change (SBTC) long dominated the literature on technology and labor markets. In this framework, technological progress is assumed to increase the relative demand for highly educated workers, leading to rising wage inequality between skill groups. However, as Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) demonstrated in their seminal *Quarterly Journal of Economics* article, the polarization of employment in the United States during the 1980s and 1990s could not be fully explained by a simple SBTC narrative. Instead, they proposed a task-based framework in which computer capital substitutes for routine cognitive and manual tasks—those that can be codified as procedural rules—while complementing non-routine problem-solving and interactive tasks. This substitution-complementarity dynamic generates a "hollowing out" of middle-skill occupations, a pattern now known as job polarization.

Acemoglu and Autor (2011), in their comprehensive *Handbook of Labor Economics* chapter, extended this framework by formalizing the assignment of heterogeneous skills to tasks, arguing that the impact of technology depends on the specific tasks it automates or augments. Building on this foundation, Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018) in the *American Economic Review* developed a task-based model in which automation is conceptualized as an expansion of the set of tasks that capital can perform. This "displacement effect" reduces labor demand for the tasks that are automated, but it is counterbalanced by a "reinstatement effect" through the creation of new tasks in which labor has a comparative advantage. The net effect on employment and wages depends on the race between these two forces.

In a subsequent *Journal of Economic Perspectives* article, Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) formalized this race, demonstrating that the key question is not whether automation destroys jobs, but whether the economy generates sufficient new tasks—and the associated productivity gains—to absorb displaced workers. This framework has become the dominant theoretical lens for analyzing AI's labor market impacts, with Agrawal, Gans, and Goldfarb (2019) in the same journal applying it specifically to AI by characterizing the technology as a dramatic reduction in the cost of prediction. They argue that cheaper prediction changes the value of judgment, decision-making, and complementary human skills, generating ambiguous net employment effects that depend on the elasticity of substitution between AI and human labor across different tasks.

## 3. Measuring AI Exposure: Methodological Innovations

A critical challenge in the empirical literature has been measuring the extent to which different occupations, industries, and geographic regions are exposed to AI. Early work by Frey and Osborne (2017) used a probabilistic approach, asking experts to assess the likelihood of computerization for 702 occupations. However, this approach was criticized for its reliance on expert judgment and its binary treatment of automation.

A landmark methodological contribution came from Felten, Raj, and Seamans (2021) in the *Strategic Management Journal*. They developed the AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE) measure, which links specific AI capabilities (e.g., image recognition, language modeling, abstract reasoning) to the abilities required by occupations as detailed in the O*NET database. Rather than treating AI as a monolithic technology, the AIOE approach captures the differential exposure of occupations to particular AI applications. The authors validated their measure by showing that occupations with higher AIOE scores experienced greater growth in AI-related job postings. Their data, publicly available, has become a standard tool in the literature.

An alternative approach was developed by Webb (2020) in a widely cited working paper. Webb used a text-matching method that compares the language of AI-related patents with the task descriptions in O*NET, creating a measure of the "task overlap" between patented AI technologies and current job tasks. A key finding from Webb's analysis is that AI, unlike earlier automation technologies such as software and industrial robots, is disproportionately directed at high-skilled cognitive tasks. Webb estimates that, under the assumption that historical patterns of substitution continue, AI will reduce 90:10 wage inequality by compressing the upper tail of the wage distribution.

Acemoglu, Autor, Hazell, and Restrepo (2022) in the *Journal of Labor Economics* constructed yet another measure by analyzing the text of online job vacancy postings to identify the AI-related skills demanded by employers. They found that establishments with higher AI vacancy exposure experienced slower hiring growth, particularly in the post-2010 period, though the effects were modest in magnitude. Their work highlights the importance of using multiple measurement approaches, as each captures a different dimension of AI exposure.

## 4. Employment Effects: Displacement, Augmentation, and Net Impact

The empirical evidence on AI's aggregate employment effects is characterized by a striking tension between theoretical predictions of large-scale disruption and the relatively modest impacts observed to date. A comprehensive review by the Yale Budget Lab (Gimbel et al., 2025) found that, as of 33 months after the launch of ChatGPT, the broader U.S. labor market has not experienced a discernible disruption in occupational mix or economy-wide employment. The pace of change in the occupational structure since 2022 is only marginally faster than historical trends observed during the internet adoption period of the late 1990s and early 2000s.

However, this aggregate stability masks significant heterogeneity. Acemoglu et al. (2022) found that AI-exposed establishments in the U.S. experienced a decline in hiring for non-AI positions, consistent with a substitution effect concentrated at the firm level. Hartley et al. (2026) report that 35.9% of U.S. workers used generative AI by December 2025, with small positive wage effects but no statistically significant declines in job openings or aggregate employment. Chandar (2025), using Current Population Survey data, similarly finds no aggregate employment decline but documents heterogeneity across education levels.

The distinction between displacement and complementarity is crucial. A study by the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta (2026) found that only 5% of firms report any employment impact from AI, with equal numbers reporting gains and losses. Eighty percent of executives surveyed reported that AI investments had not altered their headcount. A large-scale Danish study linking data on AI adoption by workers to firms found that AI adoption results in the restructuring of worker tasks and time allocation rather than outright job losses.

At the sectoral level, the BCG study (Emerson et al., 2026) projects that AI will reshape more jobs than it replaces, with the net effect varying significantly by industry. In technology and software, AI adoption is already well above average, while industries such as financial services and legal services are in earlier stages of adoption. The authors emphasize a multiyear lag between automation potential and realized labor market impact, as organizational and skill complementarities take time to develop.

## 5. Wage Dynamics and Inequality

The impact of AI on wages and wage inequality is a central concern in the literature. Alekseeva et al. (2021) found that AI skills command a significant wage premium: 16% when controlling for occupation and industry, rising to almost 20% with firm fixed effects, and remaining at 11% after additional controls. This premium is more than twice as high as that for other software skills. Stephany and Teutloff (2024) found an even larger premium of 21% for AI competencies, reflecting the growing demand for these skills across industries.

Wu et al. (2024), in a study of Chinese listed firms from 2014 to 2022 published in *Economic Modelling*, found that AI applications raise overall wages through three mechanisms: productivity improvement, displacement of low-wage routine jobs, and creation of high-wage creative and social jobs. Importantly, they found that AI narrows the wage gap between executives and regular employees, as executive pay does not increase with AI adoption while regular employee pay rises through job restructuring. This finding challenges the conventional narrative that AI inevitably exacerbates inequality.

However, the evidence on inequality is mixed. Doubková and Magdin (2026), in their comprehensive review published in *Management & Marketing*, synthesize multiple studies showing widening wage gaps between AI-skilled and non-AI-skilled workers, particularly in sectors with advanced AI adoption. The authors note that middle-skilled workers, whose tasks are often easy to automate, are most at risk of wage losses. Cheng et al. (2024, as cited in Doubková & Magdin, 2026) find that while inequalities may stabilize in the long term due to productivity gains for lower-skilled workers, current trends show that without supportive measures, AI can further increase structural inequalities.

The theoretical literature is equally divided. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2021) develop a model in which automation and the creation of new tasks jointly determine wage inequality, and they find that automation has been a major contributor to the rise in U.S. wage inequality since 1980. In contrast, Webb (2020) predicts that AI, because it targets high-skilled tasks, will reduce 90:10 wage inequality. The resolution of this debate awaits further empirical evidence as AI adoption continues to scale.

## 6. Firm-Level Restructuring and Industry Heterogeneity

A growing body of research examines how AI adoption reshapes firms' internal labor structures. A study of Taiwanese electronics firms by Yang (2022) in *Research Policy* found that AI technology is positively associated with both productivity and employment at the firm level. However, AI adoption crucially alters firms' workforce compositions, reducing the share of labor with educational qualifications of college level and below. This suggests that within-firm restructuring shifts demand toward higher-skilled workers even as total employment may increase.

A Brookings Institution analysis (2024) synthesizes firm-level evidence and finds that the effects of AI on firms are heterogeneous. Larger firms are more likely to reduce employment due to AI, while smaller firms report modest employment gains. The authors document that AI adoption is associated with increases in firm-level sales and employment at the firm level, but these gains are concentrated in publicly traded firms and may mask industry-level declines as market share concentrates among AI-adopting firms.

A study by the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta (2026) using CFO surveys finds that while AI is expected to displace some workers, the aggregate effects remain very small. The employment consequences of AI are concentrated among larger firms and AI adopters, and vary somewhat across sectors. Importantly, the study finds that AI shifts the composition of workers away from routine clerical work and toward skilled technical roles.

The restructuring of labor costs is a key mechanism. A 2026 study in *Research in International Business and Finance* using data from Chinese A-share listed firms (2010–2023) finds that AI significantly reduces corporate labor costs, with the effect strongest in labor-intensive enterprises. The study also finds that supply chain finance amplifies this cost-reduction effect, suggesting that financial liquidity enables firms to maximize the labor-cost benefits of AI adoption.

The Copenhagen Business School and IZA Institute study (2025) using Danish data finds that AI adoption results in the restructuring of worker tasks and time allocation, with workers reallocating effort toward tasks that remain difficult to automate. This within-firm reallocation is consistent with the presence of strong AI-task substitution that is offset by productivity spillovers across tasks.

## 7. Skill Transformation and the AI Premium

The literature consistently finds that AI is fundamentally changing the nature of skill demand. Doubková and Magdin (2026) identify four key findings from their review: (1) AI adoption is strongly associated with rising demand for technical and interdisciplinary skills, including programming, data analytics, and soft skills; (2) routine skills become less valued; (3) high-skilled workers benefit most from AI adoption; and (4) firms that invest in training and reskilling are more successful in adapting to AI-driven change.

A study by Serrano (2025, as cited in Doubková & Magdin, 2026) found that the impact of digitalization on employees varies according to the type of technology. While exposure to robotics and software technologies often leads to worse working conditions, employees working with AI generally tend to have better job prospects. This finding supports the idea that AI, unlike previous waves of technological advancement, is not primarily focused on replacing manual labor but rather on supporting skilled tasks.

The AI skill premium has been documented across multiple contexts. Felten, Raj, and Seamans (2021) found that occupations with higher AIOE scores experienced greater growth in AI-related job postings and wages. Babina et al. (2024) found that AI investments at the industry level are associated with growth in both sales and employment, at least for publicly traded firms. The premium is highest in management occupations, highlighting the value of strategic AI understanding beyond technical implementation.

## 8. Institutional and Policy Dimensions

The literature increasingly recognizes that the labor market impacts of AI are not technologically determined but are shaped by institutional, regulatory, and organizational factors. Doubková and Magdin (2026) emphasize that outcomes vary significantly depending on the institutional environment. Strong unions, participatory governance, and supportive legal frameworks help to mitigate job insecurity and inequality. The authors argue that the social shaping of technology perspective challenges the deterministic narrative of inevitable job replacement and instead highlights the role of policy choices in determining AI's distributional impacts.

The OECD (2019) has developed AI principles emphasizing that "AI should benefit people and the planet by driving inclusive growth, sustainable development, and well-being." These principles recognize that the impact of AI on labor markets depends on complementary investments in education, social protection, and active labor market policies.

The Council of Economic Advisers (2026) argues that AI could generate an international growth divergence, with the United States positioned to benefit from advantages in investment, compute capacity, and innovation ecosystems. The report reflects an official policy position favoring rapid deployment while acknowledging the need for distributional safeguards.

The Brookings Institution (2024) identifies several policy implications: the benefits of AI have accrued disproportionately to larger, better-resourced firms and more highly educated workers, contributing to increased industry concentration and a more skill-biased labor market. Policymakers should prepare for these distributional consequences by investing in education, training, and social safety nets.

## 9. Conclusion and Future Research Directions

The literature on AI's restructuring impact on the labor market has advanced rapidly, but significant gaps remain. The empirical evidence to date suggests that AI's aggregate employment effects are modest, but this may reflect the early stage of adoption rather than the technology's ultimate potential. The Yale Budget Lab (2025) emphasizes that while generative AI looks likely to join the ranks of transformative, general-purpose technologies, it is too soon to tell how disruptive the technology will be to jobs.

Several key findings emerge from the literature:

1. **Task restructuring, not mass displacement**: AI is primarily restructuring tasks within occupations rather than eliminating entire job categories. The net effect on employment depends on the balance between substitution and complementarity, which varies across occupations, industries, and firms.

2. **Skill-biased effects**: AI increases demand for high-skilled workers in technical and analytical roles while reducing demand for routine cognitive and manual tasks. The AI skill premium is substantial (11–21%), and the gap between AI-skilled and non-AI-skilled workers is widening.

3. **Firm-level heterogeneity**: Large firms are more likely to reduce employment with AI, while smaller firms report gains. Within firms, AI shifts workforce composition away from routine tasks and toward skilled technical roles.

4. **Institutional moderation**: The impact of AI on labor markets is mediated by institutional factors, including labor market regulations, union strength, social protection systems, and education and training policies.

5. **Uncertainty about inequality**: The literature is divided on whether AI will increase or decrease wage inequality. Some studies find that AI compresses the upper tail of the wage distribution by targeting high-skilled tasks, while others find that it widens the gap between AI-exposed and non-exposed workers.

Future research should focus on several priorities: (1) longer-term panel data as AI adoption scales; (2) causal identification of displacement versus complementarity effects at the worker level; (3) cross-country comparative studies to understand the role of institutional variation; (4) the impact of generative AI specifically, which may differ from earlier AI technologies; and (5) the dynamics of task creation and the emergence of new occupations in response to AI.
