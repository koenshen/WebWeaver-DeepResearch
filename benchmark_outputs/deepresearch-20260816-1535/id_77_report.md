
# The Role of Need for Closure in Misinformation Acceptance: A Comprehensive Research Review

## 1. Introduction

The spread of misinformation—ranging from conspiracy theories and fake news to bullshit and false eyewitness memories—has become a defining challenge of the digital age. One psychological construct that has received increasing attention in explaining why people accept misinformation is the **need for cognitive closure** (NCC, also abbreviated NFCC). This report synthesizes the current state of knowledge on the relationship between need for closure and misinformation acceptance, drawing on theoretical frameworks, experimental studies, correlational surveys, and meta-analytic evidence.

## 2. Theoretical Background: The Lay Epistemic Theory

The need for cognitive closure is defined as a desire for a definite answer on a given topic—any answer, as opposed to confusion and ambiguity (Kruglanski, 1989, 2004). It is a central construct in Kruglanski’s lay epistemic theory. The NCC can be conceptualized both as a stable dispositional trait (individual differences) and as a situationally evoked state (e.g., under time pressure, noise, or fatigue).

Kruglanski and Webster (1996) identified two key cognitive tendencies associated with high NCC:

- **Seizing (urgency tendency):** Individuals high in NCC quickly "seize" on information that provides closure, especially when it is readily available and easy to process. They form judgments rapidly and are less likely to search for alternative explanations.
- **Freezing (permanence tendency):** Once a judgment is formed, these individuals "freeze" on it—they become resistant to new information that might challenge or complicate their conclusion. This results in closed-mindedness, reduced information seeking, and a reluctance to update beliefs.

These tendencies have direct implications for misinformation acceptance. High-NCC individuals should be more likely to accept simple, certain-sounding misinformation (e.g., conspiracy theories, pseudo-profound bullshit) because such content provides epistemic closure. They should also be less likely to engage in the effortful analytical thinking required to detect falsehoods and less likely to revise their beliefs in the face of corrective information.

## 3. Need for Closure and Conspiracy Theories

### 3.1. Core Theoretical Link

Conspiracy theories offer simple, structured explanations for complex, uncertain societal events. They propose clear causality (often malevolent agency) and resolve ambiguity. This makes them attractive to individuals with a high need for closure, who are intolerant of uncertainty and prefer definitive answers (Kossowska & Bukowski, 2015; Marchlewska et al., 2018).

### 3.2. Experimental Evidence

Marchlewska, Cichocka, and Kossowska (2018) conducted two experiments directly testing the NCC–conspiracy link. In **Experiment 1**, high NCC positively predicted endorsement of a conspiracy theory about the refugee crisis, but only when conspiratorial explanations were made salient. In **Experiment 2**, when a plane crash had no clear official explanation, high NCC predicted belief in a conspiracy account; however, when an official, non-conspiratorial explanation was available, the relationship reversed—high-NCC individuals actually endorsed the conspiracy explanation *less* (Marchlewska et al., 2018). This suggests that NCC does not simply make people believe any conspiracy theory; rather, it drives them to "seize" on whatever explanation is most accessible and provides closure.

### 3.3. Large-Scale Survey Evidence

Jedinger and Masch (2025), in a large representative German sample (N = 2,883), found a small but statistically significant positive association between NCC and COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs. The standardized effect size was modest (β = 0.08, p < 0.001) compared to much stronger negative effects of political trust (β = −0.33 for pre-pandemic trust; β = −0.51 for crisis-specific trust). Trust did not moderate the NCC–conspiracy relationship. The authors concluded that NCC plays a "rather marginal" role compared to political trust (Jedinger & Masch, 2025, *Frontiers in Social Psychology*).

### 3.4. Meta-Analytic Findings

Bowes, Costello, and Tasimi (2023) conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of motivational and personological correlates of conspiratorial ideation, covering 170 studies and over 158,000 participants. They found that the meta-analytic effect size for need for closure/structure on conspiracy beliefs was **small but significant** (rs = .15 to .21). However, NCC was not among the strongest correlates; the strongest predictors were related to perceiving danger and threat, reliance on intuitive thinking, and social identity motives. Notably, the relationship between NCC and conspiracy beliefs was significantly larger when using measures of general conspiracy theories than for specific, real-world conspiracies (Bowes et al., 2023, *Psychological Bulletin*).

A subsequent systematic review and meta-analysis by Biddlestone et al. (2025) similarly found that the relationship for need for structure and closure became significant only when outliers were removed and when publication bias was accounted for, echoing the conclusion that the effect is "weak" (Biddlestone et al., 2025, *Utrecht University Repository*).

## 4. Need for Closure and Fake News

### 4.1. Correlational Evidence from Hungary

Kreko et al. (2020, published in *PMC*) examined social psychological predictors of belief in fake news in the run-up to the 2019 Hungarian elections. In a large sample, need for closure was measured with a validated 15-item scale (α = 0.84). At the bivariate level, NCC was positively associated with belief in non-political fake news (r = 0.26, p < 0.001), pro-government fake news (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), and conspiracy mentality (r = 0.24, p < 0.001). However, in regression models controlling for demographics, political orientation, and other psychological traits (RWA, SDO, system justification), NCC remained a significant predictor only for **non-political fake news** (β = 0.13, p < 0.05). It was not a significant predictor for pro-government or anti-government fake news. The authors concluded that NCC did not consistently explain fake news belief across all types; instead, conspiracy mentality was the most consistent predictor (Kreko et al., 2020, *PMC*).

### 4.2. The Illusory Truth Effect

A key question is whether high-NCC individuals are more susceptible to the *illusory truth effect*—the phenomenon whereby repeated exposure to a statement increases its perceived truth. De keersmaecker et al. (2020) conducted seven studies to test whether individual differences in cognitive ability, need for cognitive closure, and cognitive style moderate the illusory truth effect. Across all studies, the illusory truth effect was robust and significant, but **there was no evidence of moderation by NCC** (or by cognitive ability or cognitive style). The effect was equally strong for individuals high and low in NCC (De keersmaecker et al., 2020, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*). This suggests that the basic cognitive mechanism of repetition-induced fluency is universal and not amplified by NCC.

### 4.3. Bullshit Receptivity

Pennycook et al. (2015) introduced the concept of "bullshit receptivity"—the tendency to find meaning in pseudo-profound, randomly generated sentences. Pennycook and Rand (2020) found that bullshit receptivity was a strong predictor of belief in fake news. However, the relationship between NCC and bullshit receptivity appears to be **indirect** rather than direct. The MIT working paper by Pennycook and Rand (2018) explicitly tested whether the relationship between analytic thinking and fake news susceptibility was robust to controlling for NCC. They found that the effect of analytic thinking on fake news discernment remained significant after controlling for NCC, indicating that NCC does not account for the variance explained by cognitive style (Pennycook & Rand, 2018, *SSRN Working Paper*).

A dissertation by Huff (2021) tested whether NCC predicted bullshit receptivity. The study found that NCC was positively correlated with bullshit receptivity at the bivariate level, but the relationship was not significant in full regression models that included critical thinking and other covariates (Huff, 2021, *Walden University*). This suggests that the NCC–bullshit link may be confounded with other variables like low analytical thinking or high religiosity.

## 5. Need for Closure and Eyewitness Misinformation

### 5.1. Retrieval-Induced Forgetting and Misinformation Effects

Pica, Pierro, Bélanger, and Kruglanski (2014) examined the role of NCC in retrieval-induced forgetting (RIF) and misinformation effects in eyewitness memory. In three experiments, they found that NCC **augmented retrieval-induced forgetting**, which in turn magnified the misinformation effect. High-NCC individuals were more likely to forget previously learned information after retrieving related information, and this forgetting made them more susceptible to misleading post-event information. The authors argued that the "seizing" and "freezing" tendencies lead high-NCC individuals to preferentially process and rehearse information that is consistent with their current focus, at the expense of competing information (Pica et al., 2014, *Social Cognition*).

### 5.2. False Confessions

Hejniak et al. (2019) conducted two experiments using the false confession paradigm (Kassin & Kiechel, 1996). In Experiment 1, they replicated the standard misinformation effect and found a **positive correlation between NCC and susceptibility to misinformation**. In Experiment 2, they demonstrated that high NCC was associated with a greater tendency to make false confessions. The authors suggested that high-NCC individuals are more likely to accept misleading information during interrogation and to "seize" on the interrogator's suggested account to resolve the ambiguity of the situation (Hejniak et al., 2019, *Problems of Forensic Sciences*).

## 6. Need for Closure as a Moderator of the Education–Misinformation Relationship

A recent and important contribution is the work of Cosgrove (2026, *Personality and Individual Differences*), which examined whether NCC offsets the protective effect of education against misinformation. Across two studies, the author found that education was generally associated with lower conspiracy beliefs and better misinformation discernment. However, **at higher levels of NCC, this protective effect was significantly attenuated**. At +1 SD above the mean on NCC, the education advantage in misinformation discernment "shrunk to near-zero" (Cosgrove, 2026). Similarly, narcissism and uniqueness striving also moderated the education effect. The author proposed a framework of "epistemic-social motivated reasoning," whereby education-related cognitive abilities can be redirected toward satisfying psychological needs for certainty, distinctiveness, and superiority rather than accuracy (Cosgrove, 2026, as cited in *PsycNet* and *ZME Science*).

## 7. Need for Closure and Political Trust: An Important Interaction

The relationship between NCC and misinformation is not uniform across all contexts. One important moderator is **trust in institutions**. Jedinger and Masch (2025) hypothesized that high-NCC individuals with high political trust would reject conspiracy theories (because official accounts provide closure), while high-NCC individuals with low trust would embrace them. However, the interaction was **not significant**—the effect of NCC was constant across trust levels. Instead, trust had a strong direct negative effect on conspiracy beliefs, whereas NCC had a small independent positive effect (Jedinger & Masch, 2025, *Frontiers in Social Psychology*).

Other studies have found that **interpersonal trust** does not correlate with NCC but does predict lower conspiracy beliefs (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013, *PMC*). The relationship between NCC and conspiracy beliefs may be more pronounced when official explanations are unavailable or when conspiratorial narratives are salient (Marchlewska et al., 2018).

## 8. Meta-Analytic Summary of Effect Sizes

The following table summarizes the key meta-analytic and large-scale findings on the NCC–misinformation relationship:

| Outcome | Effect Size | Source |
|---------|------------|--------|

Overall, the evidence points to a **small but consistent positive association** between NCC and certain forms of misinformation (especially conspiracy theories and non-political fake news), but the effect is **domain-specific, context-dependent, and often weaker than other factors** such as political trust, conspiracy mentality, or analytical thinking.

## 9. Mechanistic Pathways

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how NCC leads to misinformation acceptance:

1. **Reduced information seeking:** High-NCC individuals search for fewer alternatives and are less likely to engage with counter-attitudinal information (Klein & Webster, 2000; Kruglanski et al., 1991).
2. **Heuristic processing:** NCC promotes reliance on simple heuristics (e.g., familiarity, fluency, source credibility) rather than systematic processing (De Dreu et al., 1999).
3. **Stereotyping and prejudice:** High-NCC individuals rely more on stereotypes, which can serve as ready-made explanations for social events and may overlap with conspiracy narratives (Kruglanski & Webster, 1996).
4. **Resistance to correction:** The "freezing" tendency makes high-NCC individuals resistant to debunking or corrective information, as they are motivated to preserve their existing closure (Kruglanski et al., 1993).
5. **Group-centrism:** NCC fosters identification with in-groups and rejection of out-groups, which can amplify belief in conspiracy theories that portray out-groups as malevolent (Kruglanski et al., 2006).

## 10. Limitations and Future Directions

### 10.1. Limitations

- **Measurement issues:** The NCC scale has been criticized for multidimensionality; sub-facets (e.g., closed-mindedness, need for predictability) may relate differently to misinformation.
- **Cultural and contextual variation:** Most studies have been conducted in Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) populations. The role of NCC may differ in non-Western contexts.
- **Publication bias:** Meta-analyses suggest that the NCC–conspiracy effect may be inflated by publication bias (Biddlestone et al., 2025).
- **Confounding with other variables:** NCC is correlated with conservatism, religious fundamentalism, and authoritarianism, which also predict misinformation acceptance. Establishing unique causal effects is challenging.

### 10.2. Future Directions

- **Experimental manipulations of NCC:** More studies should experimentally induce high or low NCC (e.g., via time pressure, noise, or accountability) to establish causal effects on misinformation acceptance.
- **Longitudinal designs:** To examine whether NCC prospectively predicts misinformation belief, or whether belief in misinformation itself increases NCC.
- **Sub-facet analysis:** Examining which specific sub-facets of NCC (e.g., closed-mindedness vs. decisiveness) are most relevant to misinformation.
- **Interventions:** Testing whether interventions that reduce NCC (e.g., promoting intellectual humility, encouraging perspective-taking, or fostering a "need for cognition") can reduce misinformation susceptibility.
- **Cross-cultural replication:** Conducting large-scale cross-cultural studies to test the generalizability of the NCC–misinformation link.

## 11. Conclusion

The need for cognitive closure plays a **consistent but modest role** in misinformation acceptance. High-NCC individuals are more likely to endorse conspiracy theories, believe non-political fake news, and succumb to eyewitness misinformation, particularly when simple, certain-sounding explanations are readily available and official alternatives are lacking. However, the effect size is small relative to other psychological factors such as political trust, analytical thinking, and conspiracy mentality. The NCC–misinformation relationship is also highly context-dependent: it is moderated by the salience of conspiratorial explanations, the availability of official accounts, and the individual's level of education. Importantly, NCC does not appear to amplify the universal illusory truth effect, suggesting that the role of this motivation is more pronounced in the *content* of what people believe than in the basic cognitive mechanisms of repetition-induced fluency.

The most recent evidence (Cosgrove, 2026) suggests that NCC can even *offset* the protective effects of education, raising important questions about the limits of cognitive ability in combating misinformation. This underscores the need for interventions that go beyond mere fact-checking and address the underlying epistemic motivations that drive people to seek—and accept—misleading information.
