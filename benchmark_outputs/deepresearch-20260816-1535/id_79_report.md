

# Transgender Themes in Middle Eastern and North African Cinema: A Critical Overview

## Abstract

This paper provides a comprehensive survey of Middle Eastern and North African (MENA) films that engage with transgender themes, situating them within the intersecting frameworks of trans studies and film theory. It argues that MENA trans cinema occupies a unique position: it must negotiate not only the visual and narrative conventions of national cinemas but also the specific legal, religious, and social conditions of the region, where gender variance is often met with state sanction yet also, in some cases, with surprising legal pathways. The paper examines in depth three key works—*Facing Mirrors* (Negar Azarbayjani, 2011, Iran), *Cinema Fouad* (Mohamed Soueid, 1993, Lebanon), and *Warsha* (Dania Bdeir, 2022, Lebanon/France)—and contextualises them within a broader landscape of MENA queer and trans filmmaking. It draws on the work of foundational trans studies scholars (Susan Stryker, Jack Halberstam, Eliza Steinbock, Cáel Keegan) and film theorists (Laura Mulvey, Vivian Sobchack, Sara Ahmed) to develop a framework for analysing how these films articulate trans identity, embodiment, and resistance.

---

## 1. Introduction: Situating MENA Trans Cinema

The field of transgender cinema studies has coalesced over the past two decades as a distinct subfield, drawing on and departing from queer theory and feminist film theory. Foundational texts such as Jack Halberstam's *In a Queer Time and Place* (2005) and Susan Stryker's *Transgender History* (2008) established the theoretical grounds for understanding trans representation as more than a mere identity category—it is a mode of embodiment, a way of seeing, and a political intervention. More recently, scholars such as Eliza Steinbock (*Shimmering Images: Trans Cinema, Embodiment, and the Aesthetics of Change*, 2019) and Cáel Keegan ("Revisitation: A Trans Phenomenology of the Media Image," 2016) have developed sophisticated frameworks for analysing trans cinematic aesthetics, including the concepts of "shimmering" and the "trans gaze."

Middle Eastern and North African films with transgender themes have received far less critical attention than their Western counterparts, yet they constitute a vital and growing body of work. These films emerge from contexts where transgender identity is legally and socially precarious—ranging from Iran's state-sanctioned but socially stigmatised gender reassignment programme to the criminalisation of same-sex conduct in many MENA countries—yet they also demonstrate remarkable formal and narrative innovation.

A key feature of MENA trans cinema is its negotiation of multiple gazes: the Western gaze (which may exoticise or rescue), the state gaze (which may police or pathologise), and the communal gaze (which may enforce gender norms). As noted in the call for chapters for *The Handbook of Trans Cinema* (2025), "The Transgender Gaze" and "Queer Phenomenology and Trans Cinema" are among the core concepts that need to be explored in relation to this regional cinema (http://wikicfp.com/cfp/servlet/event.showcfp?eventid=187726).

## 2. Theoretical Frameworks: Trans Studies and Film Theory

### 2.1 The Transgender Gaze

Laura Mulvey's foundational essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975) theorised the "male gaze" as a structure of looking that positions women as passive objects of heterosexual male desire. Building on this, Jack Halberstam's 2001 essay "The Transgender Gaze in *Boys Don't Cry*" and his subsequent book *In a Queer Time and Place* (2005) proposed a transgender gaze that disrupts the binary logic of the male/female gaze. Halberstam argues that the transgender gaze is not simply a reverse of the male gaze but a fundamentally different mode of seeing that "moves through space and time" differently, opening up alternative modes of identification for queer spectators (https://www.thegadflymagazine.org/home-1/awareness-in-the-transgender-gaze-and-the-birth-of-trans-kinship). This framework is crucial for understanding MENA trans films, where the act of looking—and being looked at—carries specific cultural and political weight.

### 2.2 Trans Phenomenology and Embodiment

Cáel Keegan's "Revisitation: A Trans Phenomenology of the Media Image" (2016) draws on Vivian Sobchack's phenomenological film theory to argue that trans subjects can fashion "their own archives of becoming through encounters with media that unintentionally" represent them. This approach emphasises the embodied, affective relationship between the spectator and the screen image, a perspective that is particularly productive for analysing MENA films that use visual metaphors of height, interiority, and transformation. Sara Ahmed's *Queer Phenomenology: Orientations, Objects, Others* (2006) provides additional tools for understanding how trans bodies are "oriented" in space and how film can reorient them (https://necsus-ejms.org/questions-of-embodied-difference-film-and-queer-phenomenology).

### 2.3 Gender Performativity

Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity, articulated in *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993), posits that gender is constituted through the repeated stylisation of the body, a set of acts that produce the illusion of a stable identity. This framework is especially relevant to MENA cinema, where gender performance is often hypervisible—whether in the context of cross-dressing comedies in Egyptian cinema (a long-standing tradition noted by Brian Whitaker: https://brian-whit.medium.com/transgender-issues-in-the-middle-east-1-crossing-lines-2cbfbbd29e93) or in the more explicit trans narratives of contemporary art cinema.

### 2.4 Shimmering and Trans Aesthetics

Eliza Steinbock's concept of "shimmering" describes the aesthetic quality of trans cinematic images that "oscillate between visibility and invisibility, legibility and illegibility" (*Shimmering Images*, 2019). This concept is particularly useful for analysing MENA films in which trans identity is often expressed through coded, indirect, or metaphorical means due to censorship and social taboos. The short film *Warsha*, for instance, uses the shimmering lights of Beirut at night and the metallic gleam of a crane to create a trans aesthetic of becoming.

### 2.5 Susan Stryker and Trans Studies as a Field

Susan Stryker, founding executive editor of *TSQ: Transgender Studies Quarterly*, has been instrumental in establishing trans studies as a distinct academic field. Her essay "My Words to Victor Frankenstein Above the Village of Chamounix" (1994) reclaims monstrosity as a trans position, arguing that trans people "rise up from the operating tables of our rebirth" as subjects who challenge the naturalised order of gender. This framework of trans rage and reclamation is relevant to the political urgency of many MENA trans films, which often depict violence, exile, and survival (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Stryker).

## 3. The Landscape of MENA Trans and Queer Cinema

### 3.1 Historical Context

Cross-dressing and gender ambiguity have long been present in Arab cinema, particularly in Egyptian popular cinema, where comic cross-dressing was a staple. As Brian Whitaker notes, "Egyptian cinema, which developed in the 1920s and is popular throughout the Arab region, has shown little inhibition about portrayals of cross-dressing in films" (https://brian-whit.medium.com/transgender-issues-in-the-middle-east-1-crossing-lines-2cbfbbd29e93). However, explicit transgender representation—where characters identify as trans rather than merely cross-dressing for comic effect—is a more recent phenomenon, emerging in the 1990s and accelerating after the Arab uprisings.

The Arab Film and Media Institute (AFMI) maintains a growing list of queer Arab films, including several with trans themes. Notable entries include *Cinema Fouad* (1993), *Facing Mirrors* (2011), and *Warsha* (2022), as well as documentaries such as *The Window* (2022) and *Dress Up* (2022) (https://arabfilminstitute.org/queer-arab-films-to-watch-during-pride-month).

### 3.2 Legal and Social Context

The legal status of transgender people varies widely across the MENA region. Iran is a unique case: following a fatwa by Ayatollah Khomeini in the 1980s, sex reassignment surgery was deemed permissible under Islamic law, and the state partially funds such procedures. However, as the Yale Review of International Studies notes, "in spite of the government's apparent approval of transgender issues, transgender people continue to face maltreatment and discrimination from a more personal scale" (https://yris.yira.org/campus/facing-mirrors-a-film-about-transgender-rights-in-iran). Homosexuality, by contrast, remains punishable by death in Iran, creating a situation where trans identity is medically legible but socially precarious.

In Lebanon, transgender people face legal discrimination and police violence, though Beirut has emerged as a hub for queer and trans cultural production. The Human Rights Watch report "Don't Punish Me for Who I Am: Discrimination Against Transgender Women in Lebanon" (2019) documents the specific vulnerabilities faced by trans women (https://worldwithoutgenocide.org/genocides-and-conflicts/background/treatment-of-transgender-individuals-in-the-middle-east-and-north-africa).

## 4. In-Depth Film Analysis

### 4.1 *Facing Mirrors* (Negar Azarbayjani, 2011, Iran)

*Facing Mirrors* (Persian: *Aynehaye Rooberoo*) is the first Iranian narrative film to feature a transgender protagonist. Directed by Negar Azarbayjani, the film tells the story of Adineh/Eddie (played by Shayesteh Irani), a young transgender man seeking to escape Iran for gender-affirming surgery in Germany, and Rana (Ghazal Shakeri), a traditional wife and mother forced to drive a taxi to pay off her husband's debts. The film's central conceit—an unlikely friendship between two women (one cis, one trans) who are both trapped by patriarchal structures—enables an intersectional analysis of gender, class, and agency.

**Trans Theory Lens:** The film explicitly engages with the Iranian state's paradoxical position on trans identity. Eddie's desire to go to Germany rather than access Iran's state-sanctioned surgery programme highlights the gap between legal recognition and social acceptance. As the Yale review notes, "Transgender people in Iran encounter day-to-day harassment, and find it very difficult to obtain work" (https://yris.yira.org/campus/facing-mirrors-a-film-about-transgender-rights-in-iran). The film thus stages what Susan Stryker would call a "transgender rage" against a system that pathologises even as it permits.

**Film Theory Lens:** The film's title—*Facing Mirrors*—suggests a Lacanian mirror stage, but one that is doubled and recursive. The relationship between Eddie and Rana functions as a mirror in which both characters see their own gender performance reflected and challenged. The film uses the confined space of the taxi as a heterotopia—a "safe space" where Eddie can be himself, away from the gaze of family and state. The driving scenes, in which Rana navigates the chaotic streets of Tehran, become a metaphor for navigating gender norms.

**Key Scene:** The moment when Rana discovers Eddie's trans identity is depicted through a car accident—a literal collision of worlds. As the Frameline programme note observes, "When Rana discovers that Eddie is 'trans,' she panics and drives into the path of a bus" (https://www.frameline.org/films/frameline36/facing-mirrors). This scene dramatises the visceral shock of encountering gender variance within a society that polices gender boundaries. The film's resolution—Eddie's departure for Germany—is bittersweet, underscoring the theme of exile that runs through much MENA trans cinema.

### 4.2 *Cinema Fouad* (Mohamed Soueid, 1993, Lebanon)

*Cinema Fouad* is a 41-minute documentary portrait of Khaled El Kurdi, a Syrian trans woman living in Beirut, where she works as a domestic worker and belly dancer. Directed by Lebanese filmmaker Mohamed Soueid, the film follows El Kurdi through her domestic world—eating, applying makeup, dancing in her bedroom—while she reflects on her life, her desire for gender-affirming surgery, and her love for a Palestinian freedom fighter who was killed.

**Trans Theory Lens:** *Cinema Fouad* is a landmark of trans documentary filmmaking, predating the "trans tipping point" of the 2010s by two decades. The film's intimate, observational style refuses the sensationalism that often characterised early trans media representation. Instead, it allows El Kurdi to speak on her own terms, articulating a complex subjectivity that encompasses gender, nation, and desire. Her story of fighting alongside Palestinian resistance groups while being open about her gender identity challenges the erasure of trans people from histories of anticolonial struggle.

The film can be read through Stryker's framework of "transgender rage" and reclamation. El Kurdi's defiant refusal to be shamed—her continued practice of belly dancing, a form of performance that foregrounds bodily expressiveness—enacts a kind of trans agency within a context of precarity. As the Sinema Transtopia programme notes: "Gently and compassionately, *Cinema Fouad* weaves a complex and multi-layered story of sexuality, identity and desire" (https://sinematranstopia.com/en/yearnings-and-unfulfilled-dreams-from-the-personal-to-the-political/cinema-fouad-soha).

**Film Theory Lens:** The film's title—*Cinema Fouad*—references both the protagonist's name (Fouad is a common Arabic name, but "Fouad" also means "heart") and the cinematic apparatus itself. The film is as much about the act of filming as it is about its subject; Soueid's camera is sometimes confrontational, sometimes tender, and the dynamic between filmmaker and subject becomes part of the film's texture. This reflexivity aligns with phenomenological film theory: the viewer is made aware of the act of looking and the ethical responsibilities it entails.

**Key Scene:** El Kurdi's bedroom dance sequences are the film's most powerful moments. Alone, she dances to Arabic pop music, her body moving in ways that are both sensual and defiant. These scenes, as described by the National Museum of Norway, show a "Syrian trans woman and belly dancer" claiming space for herself in the privacy of her home (https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/exhibitions-and-events/national-museum/arrangementer/2022/11/trap-filmserie/cinema-fouad). The dance becomes a form of what Steinbock calls "shimmering"—a moment of trans becoming that is both visible and fleeting.

### 4.3 *Warsha* (Dania Bdeir, 2022, Lebanon/France)

*Warsha* (Arabic for "workshop") is a 15-minute short film directed by Dania Bdeir that follows Mohammad, a Syrian migrant worker in Beirut who volunteers to operate one of the city's tallest and most dangerous construction cranes. Isolated in the crane's cabin, he sheds his work clothes and performs a dance of gender expression, using the safety of height to explore his trans identity.

**Trans Theory Lens:** *Warsha* offers a radical reimagining of trans cinematic space. The crane becomes a metaphor for both the danger and the liberation of trans existence: high above the city, Mohammad is invisible to the social gaze that polices gender on the ground. The film stages a kind of "trans phenomenology" (Keegan, 2016) in which the vertical axis—ascending, looking down—becomes a way of reorienting the trans body in space. The film is also notable for casting Khansa, a multi-disciplinary artist who "redefines masculinity in the Middle East" (https://film-fest-report.com/sundance-2022-warsha-interview-dania-bdeir).

**Film Theory Lens:** The film's visual language is strikingly cinematic. The opening scenes establish the gritty, dangerous reality of Beirut's construction sites, with migrant workers labouring under precarious conditions. As Mohammad climbs the crane, the camera follows him, creating a sense of vertigo and anticipation. The climactic scene—a dance performed in the crane's cabin—uses the shimmering lights of Beirut as a backdrop, creating what Bdeir describes as a space where Mohammad can "break out of gender norms and express himself truly" (https://film-fest-report.com/sundance-2022-warsha-interview-dania-bdeir). The film's use of sound—the industrial noise of the construction site giving way to music—further underscores the movement from repression to expression.

**Key Scene:** The transformation scene is wordless. Mohammad removes his work vest and hard hat, revealing a red dress underneath. He begins to dance, his movements tentative at first, then increasingly confident. The camera stays close, capturing the joy and terror of this act of self-disclosure. This scene exemplifies what Halberstam calls "the transgender gaze"—a way of looking that does not objectify but rather affirms the subject's own self-perception. The film won the Short Film Jury Award at the 2022 Sundance Film Festival and was shortlisted for the Oscars (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsha).

## 5. Beyond the Three Films: A Broader Survey

### 5.1 *Circumstance* (Maryam Keshavarz, 2011, Iran/USA)

Though primarily a lesbian romance, *Circumstance* is essential context for understanding MENA queer cinema. The film, which won the Sundance Audience Award, depicts two teenage girls in Tehran navigating desire under the watchful eye of the morality police. The film was shot in Lebanon because Keshavarz was barred from filming in Iran, and its release resulted in the director being banned from returning to Iran (https://epgn.com/2011/09/08/15436643-troubling-circumstance-film-delves-into-iranian-society-repression). The film's underground party scenes, with their mix of drugs, music, and gender play, create a space of queer possibility that resonates with trans themes.

### 5.2 *In Between* (Maysaloun Hamoud, 2016, Israel/France)

*In Between* (Arabic: *Bar Bahar*) follows three Palestinian women sharing an apartment in Tel Aviv. While not explicitly trans, the film's exploration of gender performance, religious identity, and sexual freedom is directly relevant. The character of Salma, a lesbian DJ who hides her sexuality from her conservative Christian family, enacts a form of gender passing that parallels trans experience. The film's title—*In Between*—captures the liminal space that queer and trans people occupy in MENA societies (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Between_(2016_film)).

### 5.3 *The Window* (2022) and *Dress Up* (2022)

These short films, featured in the Arab Film and Media Institute's queer Arab film list, represent the new wave of MENA trans filmmaking. Both films explore trans identity in the Arab diaspora, addressing themes of family rejection, transition, and self-acceptance. As noted in the AFMI's 2026 Pride Month roundup, these films are part of a growing ecosystem of trans cultural production that includes feature films, documentaries, and video art (https://arabfilminstitute.org/queer-arab-films-to-watch-during-pride-month).

### 5.4 *Measures of Distance* (Mona Hatoum, 1988)

Though not strictly a trans film, Mona Hatoum's experimental video *Measures of Distance* is a crucial precursor to MENA trans and queer filmmaking. The film uses letters from Hatoum's mother in Beirut, overlaid on images of her mother's body in the shower, to explore the relationship between intimacy, exile, and the female body. The work's use of Arabic script as a visual veil and its engagement with the politics of looking prefigure many of the aesthetic strategies used by later trans filmmakers (https://www.moma.org/collection/works/118590).

## 6. Core Concepts in MENA Trans Cinema

### 6.1 Exile and Mobility

A recurring theme in MENA trans cinema is the need to move—to leave home, to cross borders, to seek safety in another country. *Facing Mirrors* ends with Eddie's departure for Germany; *Cinema Fouad* depicts Khaled's displacement from Syria to Lebanon; *Warsha* shows a Syrian migrant worker in Beirut. This geography of exile reflects the real conditions of trans people in the region, many of whom are forced to flee due to family rejection or state violence.

### 6.2 The Body as Site of Resistance

MENA trans films consistently foreground the body as a site of both vulnerability and resistance. The dance sequences in *Cinema Fouad* and *Warsha* are acts of bodily reclamation, while the car accident in *Facing Mirrors* dramatises the body's exposure to violence. These films refuse the "wrong body" narrative that has dominated Western trans cinema (critiqued by Halberstam and others), instead presenting trans embodiment as a dynamic, relational process.

### 6.3 The Domestic and the Public

The films oscillate between the domestic space (the bedroom in *Cinema Fouad*, the taxi in *Facing Mirrors*, the crane cabin in *Warsha*) and the public sphere (the street, the construction site, the city). These spaces are gendered and policed, but the films show how trans subjects carve out moments of freedom within them. The concept of "queer phenomenology" (Ahmed, 2006) is useful here: the films ask how trans bodies are "oriented" in space and how they can "reorient" themselves.

### 6.4 The Role of the Camera

The camera in MENA trans cinema is never neutral. In *Cinema Fouad*, the filmmaker's presence is palpable; in *Warsha*, the camera moves with the protagonist, creating a sense of embodied empathy; in *Facing Mirrors*, the camera observes from the taxi's interior, creating a space of intimacy. This reflexivity aligns with the "trans gaze" as theorised by Halberstam: a way of looking that acknowledges the politics of representation.

## 7. Conclusion: The Future of MENA Trans Cinema

MENA trans cinema is at a crucial juncture. The growing visibility of trans filmmakers and subjects in the region—despite legal and social obstacles—is producing a body of work that is both aesthetically innovative and politically urgent. The forthcoming *Handbook of Trans Cinema* includes dedicated chapters on "Trans Cinema from Algeria," "Trans Cinema from Lebanon," and other national contexts, signalling the institutional recognition of this field (http://wikicfp.com/cfp/servlet/event.showcfp?eventid=187726).

At the same time, the region's trans filmmakers face significant challenges: censorship, funding constraints, and the risk of personal harm. The fact that *Facing Mirrors* could be made in Iran, that *Cinema Fouad* could be screened in Beirut, and that *Warsha* could win at Sundance, is testament to the resilience of trans artists and their allies.

Future scholarship should attend to the following: (1) the relationship between MENA trans cinema and the global festival circuit, which often shapes the conditions of production and reception; (2) the role of digital platforms in distributing trans films that cannot be shown in traditional cinemas; (3) the intersection of trans identity with other markers of difference, including class, religion, and nationality; and (4) the development of region-specific theoretical frameworks that do not simply apply Western trans studies concepts uncritically.

As this paper has argued, MENA trans cinema offers a unique vantage point on the relationship between gender, visual culture, and power. It demands that we rethink the categories of trans theory and film theory from the perspective of the Global South, where the stakes of gender variance are often life and death. The films discussed here—*Facing Mirrors*, *Cinema Fouad*, *Warsha*—are not merely regional examples of a global phenomenon. They are works that challenge the very terms of the debate, insisting that trans identity is not a Western import but a lived reality with deep roots in the cultures of the Middle East and North Africa.
