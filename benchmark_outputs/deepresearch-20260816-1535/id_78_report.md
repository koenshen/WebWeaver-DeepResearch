

# Parkinson's Disease: A Comprehensive Guide for Families — Stages, Warning Signs, and Post-DBS Care

## Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that affects more than 10 million people worldwide. It is the second most common degenerative neurological condition after Alzheimer's disease. While the disease course varies significantly from person to person, understanding the typical stages, recognizing critical warning signs, and knowing how to support a loved one—especially after advanced treatments like Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)—can dramatically improve quality of life and safety.

---

## Part I: Health Warning Signs Across the Stages of Parkinson's Disease

The most widely used clinical framework for staging PD is the **Hoehn and Yahr scale**, originally published in 1967 and subsequently modified. This scale classifies disease severity from Stage 1 (mildest) to Stage 5 (most advanced). In addition, clinicians use the **Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS)** to track both motor and non-motor symptoms.

### Stage 1 — Early-Stage / Mild

**Motor Signs:**
- Tremor on one side of the body (often a subtle resting tremor in a finger, thumb, hand, or chin)
- Mild rigidity or stiffness on one side
- One arm may swing less freely when walking
- Slight changes in posture or walking pattern
- Reduced facial expression (hypomimia / "masked face") that may be mistaken for depression
- Micrographia (handwriting becomes smaller and more cramped)

**Non-Motor Signs (often precede motor symptoms by years):**
- Loss of sense of smell (hyposmia or anosmia)
- REM sleep behavior disorder (acting out dreams, thrashing, speaking during sleep)
- Constipation and other digestive changes
- Subtle mood changes including mild depression or anxiety
- Fatigue

**What family members may notice first:** Changes in arm swing, softer voice, decreased facial expression, smaller handwriting, and a subtle change in walking pattern (shuffling or reduced stride). These are often noticed by family members before the patient is aware of them.

*Source: Healthline, "5 Stages of Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.healthline.com/health/parkinsons/stages)  
*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "10 Early Signs"* (https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/10-early-signs)

### Stage 2 — Moderate

**Motor Signs:**
- Symptoms appear on both sides of the body (though one side may still be more affected)
- Tremor, rigidity, and trembling become more noticeable
- Changes in facial expression become more apparent
- Muscle stiffness prolongs task completion
- Walking difficulties may develop or increase
- Posture may start to stoop or lean forward
- Speech difficulties may emerge (soft or slurred speech)

**Key Distinction from Stage 1:** Balance is NOT yet impaired. Most people can still live independently, though daily tasks take longer.

*Source: Healthline, "5 Stages of Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.healthline.com/health/parkinsons/stages)

### Stage 3 — Mid-Stage (Major Turning Point)

**Motor Signs:**
- **Loss of balance** is the hallmark symptom (unsteadiness when turning or when pushed from standing)
- Significant slowing of movement (bradykinesia)
- Decreased reflexes
- **Falls become more common** — this is a major safety concern
- Functional disability is mild to moderate
- Daily tasks can still be completed with assistance

**Non-Motor Signs:**
- More pronounced mood changes and anxiety
- Increasing difficulty with fine motor tasks
- Potential for medication-related fluctuations ("on-off" periods)

**This is the stage where falls pose the greatest new risk.** Families should begin implementing fall prevention measures.

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "Stages of Parkinson's"* (https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/what-is-parkinsons/stages)  
*Source: Healthline, "5 Stages of Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.healthline.com/health/parkinsons/stages)

### Stage 4 — Advanced Stage

**Motor Signs:**
- Severe and incapacitating symptoms
- Can stand without assistance, but movement requires a walker or other assistive device
- Significant decrease in movement and reaction times
- Medication response often becomes less effective
- **Increasing loss of independence** — many people cannot live alone safely

**Non-Motor Signs:**
- Cognitive changes (memory difficulties, slowed thinking, planning problems)
- Mood disorders (anxiety, depression, apathy)
- Sleep disorders (insomnia, vivid dreams)
- Pain and sensory disturbances
- Fatigue
- Speech and swallowing problems begin to worsen
- Up to 50% of people at Stages 4 and 5 experience confusion, hallucinations, and delusions

**Critical Safety Concern:** Living alone at this stage is often dangerous due to fall risk and the inability to complete daily tasks without assistance.

*Source: Healthline, "5 Stages of Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.healthline.com/health/parkinsons/stages)  
*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "Stages of Parkinson's"* (https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/what-is-parkinsons/stages)

### Stage 5 — Most Advanced / End-Stage

**Motor Signs:**
- Advanced stiffness in the legs causes "freezing" upon standing, making it impossible to stand or walk
- Person is bedridden or confined to a wheelchair unless aided
- **Around-the-clock care is required** for all activities
- Medications may no longer control symptoms well

**Non-Motor Signs:**
- Severe cognitive decline / dementia
- Significant swallowing difficulties (dysphagia) leading to choking risk and aspiration pneumonia
- Speech may become unintelligible
- Urinary incontinence
- Psychosis and hallucinations (often medication-related)
- Weight loss and diminished appetite

**Life-Threatening Complications:**
- Aspiration pneumonia (from swallowing difficulties)
- Injuries from falls (fractures, head trauma)
- Malnutrition and dehydration
- Infections (UTIs, bedsores)

**Note:** Parkinson's disease itself is not fatal, but its complications — particularly aspiration pneumonia, falls, and infections — can be.

*Source: Healthline, "5 Stages of Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.healthline.com/health/parkinsons/stages)  
*Source: Verywell Health, "End-Stage Parkinson's Disease: Hospice Criteria"* (https://www.verywellhealth.com/end-stage-parkinson-s-disease-hospice-criteria-5205423)

## Part II: Specific Signs That Should Alert Family Members to Intervene or Seek Medical Advice

### Early-Stage Red Flags (Seek Initial Diagnosis)

If you observe **any combination of the following** in a loved one, schedule an appointment with a neurologist (preferably a movement disorder specialist):

1. **Resting tremor** in a finger, thumb, hand, or chin
2. **Stiffness or rigidity** that does not resolve with movement, often presenting as shoulder or hip pain
3. **Bradykinesia** — slowing of movement, difficulty initiating movements
4. **Reduced arm swing** on one side while walking
5. **Micrographia** — progressive shrinking of handwriting
6. **Hypomimia** — reduced facial expression (masked face)
7. **Hypophonia** — soft, monotone, or slurred speech
8. **Loss of sense of smell** (hyposmia)
9. **REM sleep behavior disorder** — acting out dreams, thrashing, speaking during sleep
10. **Unexplained constipation, depression, or anxiety**

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "10 Early Signs"* (https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/10-early-signs)  
*Source: Neurology Institute of Huntsville, "Early Signs of Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.neurologyinstituteofhuntsville.com/early-signs-of-parkinsons-disease)

### Mid-Stage Red Flags (Require Urgent Medical Attention)

Contact your neurologist or seek medical care if:

- **First fall occurs** — this marks a major progression milestone and requires a fall risk assessment
- **Frequent falls** — more than one fall in a short period
- **Freezing of gait** — feet suddenly feel "stuck to the floor"
- **Worsening "on-off" fluctuations** — medication doses wear off unpredictably
- **Onset of dyskinesia** — uncontrolled, involuntary writhing movements
- **Significant changes in mood or behavior** — new depression, anxiety, or apathy
- **Hallucinations or delusions** — even if mild, these require immediate medical evaluation (often medication-related)

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "Stages of Parkinson's"* (https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/what-is-parkinsons/stages)

### Advanced-Stage Red Flags (Require Immediate Medical Evaluation or Emergency Care)

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room if:**

- **Sudden confusion or delirium** — often signals a urinary tract infection (UTI) or other infection, which can severely worsen PD symptoms
- **Signs of aspiration** — coughing or choking while eating/drinking, followed by fever, shortness of breath, or chest congestion (possible aspiration pneumonia)
- **Inability to take medications** — vomiting, severe dysphagia, or altered mental status preventing medication intake
- **Head injury from a fall** — especially if on blood thinners or if there is loss of consciousness, confusion, or vomiting
- **Suicidal ideation** — depression is common in PD; any talk of self-harm requires immediate intervention
- **Severe hallucinations or psychosis** — frightening or dangerous hallucinations require medical intervention
- **Sudden worsening of mobility** — rapid onset of symptoms (over days rather than months) may indicate an infection, medication issue, or other acute problem

**Contact your neurologist or primary care provider promptly if:**

- **Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)** — coughing, choking, drooling, or food pocketing in the mouth; this requires a swallowing evaluation
- **Unintentional weight loss** — 5% or more of body weight in 6-12 months
- **Repeated infections** — especially UTIs or pneumonia
- **Increasing confusion or dementia** — difficulty participating in medical care decisions
- **Worsening of PD symptoms during hospitalization** — people with PD are at high risk for complications during hospital stays, including missed medications and adverse drug reactions

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "Hospitalization and Parkinson's"* (https://www.parkinson.org/sites/default/files/documents/care-partner-guide-senior-living-facility.pdf)  
*Source: APDA, "How People with Parkinson's Disease Can Minimize Risks During Hospitalization"* (https://www.apdaparkinson.org/article/hospitalization-risks-for-people-with-parkinsons)  
*Source: Stanford Medicine, "Hospitalization in Parkinson's Disease"* (https://med.stanford.edu/parkinsons/caregiver-corner/hospitalization.html)

### When to Consider Hospice Care

The Parkinson's Foundation identifies the following signs that may indicate eligibility for hospice:

- Trouble breathing or needing oxygen while resting
- Frequent falls or needing help to get out of a bed or chair
- Difficulty eating or drinking, leading to weight loss
- Recurrent hospitalizations or significant decline after a single hospital stay
- Advanced dementia
- Weight loss and/or diminished appetite
- Dysphagia for liquids or secretions

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "Understanding Hospice Care in Parkinson's"* (https://www.parkinson.org/library/fact-sheets/hospice)  
*Source: PMC, "Palliative Care of End Stage Parkinsonism"* (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10448122)

## Part III: Daily Life Adjustments and Support Strategies for Patients After Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) Surgery

Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) is an advanced surgical treatment for Parkinson's disease that involves implanting electrodes in specific brain regions (typically the subthalamic nucleus [STN] or globus pallidus interna [GPi]) connected to a pacemaker-like device (implanted pulse generator or IPG) placed under the skin near the collarbone. DBS can significantly improve motor symptoms, reduce medication needs, and enhance quality of life — but it requires careful post-surgical management.

### Immediate Post-Surgery Recovery (First 2-4 Weeks)

**Hospital Stay:**
- Patients typically spend 1-2 days in the hospital for monitoring
- Post-surgical pain is usually mild and managed with oral medication
- Swelling around the eyes and tenderness near incisions are common and may last 1-2 weeks
- The surgical incisions are covered with surgical glue; they can get wet but should not be soaked (no baths, pools, or hot tubs for at least one month)

**Activity Restrictions:**
- **Do not lift, push, or pull anything heavier than 8-10 pounds** (about the weight of a gallon of milk)
- **Avoid strenuous activity for the first 2-4 weeks**
- **Do not drive** until you can fully turn your neck, are off narcotic medication, and your surgeon says it is safe
- **Do not fly in an airplane for at least 3 weeks**
- Gentle neck stretching (side bends, turns, flex/extend for 10 seconds each, a few times a day) can help prevent "bowstringing" — tightness in the neck along the wires due to scarring

**Wound Care:**
- Keep incisions clean and dry
- Monitor for signs of infection: redness, swelling, warmth, drainage, or fever
- Contact your DBS team immediately if any signs of infection appear

*Source: DBSandMe, "What to Expect After DBS Surgery"* (https://www.dbsandme.com/en/support-and-resources/articles/what-to-expect-after-dbs-surgery.html)  
*Source: Mian Neurosurgery, "DBS Patient Guide"* (https://mian-neurosurgery.com/dbs-patient-guide)  
*Source: OHSU, "What to Expect After DBS Surgery"* (https://www.ohsu.edu/brain-institute/what-expect-after-dbs-surgery)

### DBS Programming and Medication Management

**Initial Programming:**
- The DBS device is typically turned on about 1 month after surgery
- The initial programming session can take several hours
- **Multiple visits are required** over the first few months to fine-tune settings
- Temporary sensations during programming may include tingling, mild facial pulling, or visual changes — these usually resolve when stimulation is adjusted
- Some symptoms (like tremor) may improve immediately; others (like dystonia) may take more time

**Medication Management:**
- **Do not change medications unless directed by your neurologist**
- DBS is not a cure; most patients will continue to take some PD medications
- In some cases, medication doses can be reduced by approximately 30% with DBS
- **Maintain a strict medication schedule** — do not skip doses, take extra doses, or take doses at unusual times
- DBS works best when combined with medication, exercise, and other therapies

**Long-Term Management:**
- Regular follow-up appointments are needed to monitor the device and adjust settings
- The IPG battery lasts 3-5 years (non-rechargeable) or up to 15 years (rechargeable)
- DBS does not help with all symptoms — it does not improve speech, balance, cognition, swallowing, or non-motor symptoms like depression or anxiety

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)"* (https://www.parkinson.org/living-with-parkinsons/treatment/surgical-treatment-options/deep-brain-stimulation)  
*Source: Boston Scientific, "Life After DBS Surgery"* (https://www.brain-stimulation.co.uk/what-is-dbs/how-is-life-with-dbs.html)  
*Source: OHSU, "DBS for Parkinson's"* (https://www.ohsu.edu/brain-institute/dbs-parkinsons)

### Daily Life Adjustments for Optimal Comfort and Well-Being

#### 1. Establish a Consistent Routine
- Keep a steady daily routine — consistent wake times, meal times, medication times, and bedtimes
- Set a reasonable bedtime and stick with it
- If sleep is difficult, melatonin can be effective and is available over-the-counter (consult your doctor first)

#### 2. Exercise and Physical Activity
- **Exercise is critically important** — there is strong evidence that it may slow disease progression and improve symptoms
- Walking is an excellent way to begin rehabilitation after DBS surgery
- Gradually increase activity as cleared by your surgeon
- Consider physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy to maximize DBS benefits
- Recommended activities: walking, stretching, balance exercises, tai chi, yoga, cycling, swimming

#### 3. Fall Prevention
- **Fall risk is significantly elevated in the weeks after DBS surgery**
- Remove tripping hazards (loose rugs, clutter, cords)
- Install grab bars in bathrooms and near stairs
- Ensure adequate lighting, especially at night
- Use assistive devices (canes, walkers) as recommended
- Keep floors dry and non-slip

#### 4. Diet and Nutrition
- Follow a heart-healthy diet (Mediterranean diet is often recommended)
- Stay well-hydrated
- For swallowing difficulties: softer foods, thickened liquids, and upright positioning during meals
- Avoid large protein-rich meals close to medication times, as protein can interfere with levodopa absorption
- Consult a dietitian for personalized guidance

#### 5. Sleep Hygiene
- Stick to a consistent sleep schedule
- Create a comfortable, dark, quiet sleep environment
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol close to bedtime
- Address any sleep disorders (insomnia, RBD, sleep apnea) with your healthcare team

#### 6. Emotional and Mental Health Support
- Depression and anxiety are common in PD — do not hesitate to seek professional mental health support
- Join a DBS support group or Parkinson's support group
- Consider counseling or therapy for both patient and caregivers
- Discuss any mood changes, apathy, or cognitive concerns with the DBS team

#### 7. Communication and Social Engagement
- Maintain social connections — isolation worsens outcomes
- If speech becomes soft or slurred, speech therapy can help (the Lee Silverman Voice Treatment (LSVT) program is evidence-based)
- Use communication aids if needed (amplification devices, writing tools)

#### 8. Driving Safety
- Do not drive until cleared by your surgeon and neurologist
- Factors that affect driving safety: ability to turn the neck, reaction time, medication side effects (drowsiness), and cognitive changes
- Be honest about driving abilities — driving assessments may be helpful

*Source: Mian Neurosurgery, "DBS Patient Guide"* (https://mian-neurosurgery.com/dbs-patient-guide)  
*Source: Hopkins Medicine, "Fighting Parkinson's Disease with Exercise and Diet"* (https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/parkinsons-disease/fighting-parkinson-disease-with-exercise-and-diet)  
*Source: Mass General Brigham, "Parkinson's Disease Diet"* (https://www.massgeneralbrigham.org/en/about/newsroom/articles/parkinsons-disease-diet)

### What Family Members and Care Partners Can Do

**During the Immediate Post-Surgery Period:**
- Assist with wound care monitoring
- Ensure medication adherence
- Help with transportation to programming appointments
- Be patient during the adjustment period — symptom improvement may take weeks to months
- Watch for signs of complications: infection, persistent pain, worsening symptoms

**Long-Term Support Strategies:**
- **Create a "hospitalization kit"** with the Parkinson's Foundation Aware in Care kit, including a medication form listing dosage and timing of each drug, and a letter from the movement disorder specialist highlighting the importance of timely PD medication
- **Keep a "red flags" list** for care facility staff (increased agitation, sudden confusion, frequent falls, or any acute change in status)
- **Be the advocate** — ensure hospital staff understand the critical importance of giving PD medications on time (delays can cause severe symptom worsening)
- **Encourage independence** — do not take over tasks the person can still do themselves; this preserves dignity and function
- **Communicate openly** — have difficult conversations early about driving cessation, safety, and future care needs
- **Seek caregiver support** — caregiving for PD is associated with significant burden; support groups, respite care, and counseling are essential

*Source: Parkinson's Foundation, "PD Care Partner Guide"* (https://www.parkinson.org/sites/default/files/documents/care-partner-guide-senior-living-facility.pdf)  
*Source: Hopkins Medicine, "The Parkinson's Caregiver: 7 Ways to Help Your Loved One"* (https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/parkinsons-disease/the-parkinsons-caregiver-7-ways-to-help-your-loved-one)  
*Source: VA Caregiver Support Program, "Caregiving Tips - Parkinson's Disease"* (https://www.caregiver.va.gov/Tips_by_Diagnosis/Parkinsons.asp)

### When to Call the DBS Team

Contact your DBS neurology nurse or doctor if you experience:

- **Signs of infection** at incision sites (redness, swelling, warmth, drainage, fever)
- **Sudden worsening of symptoms** that does not resolve with medication
- **New or worsening speech problems** (slurred speech, difficulty finding words)
- **Visual changes** (double vision, blurred vision)
- **Persistent numbness or tingling** in the face or limbs
- **Muscle tightness** of the face or arm that does not resolve when stimulation is adjusted
- **Any side effects from stimulation** (speech changes, visual changes, numbness/tingling, muscle tightness)
- **Device-related concerns** (unusual sensations near the IPG, changes in battery status)

*Source: DBSandMe, "What to Expect After DBS Surgery"* (https://www.dbsandme.com/en/support-and-resources/articles/what-to-expect-after-dbs-surgery.html)

## Summary of Key Take-Home Messages

| **Stage** | **Primary Warning Signs for Families** | **Action Required** |
|-----------|----------------------------------------|---------------------|
| **Stage 1-2** | Tremor, stiffness, reduced arm swing, masked face, micrographia, soft voice, sleep disturbances, loss of smell | Schedule neurological evaluation |
| **Stage 3** | First fall, loss of balance, freezing of gait, increased bradykinesia | Fall risk assessment, home safety modifications, medication review |
| **Stage 4** | Inability to live alone, need for walker, cognitive decline, hallucinations, swallowing difficulties | Consider 24/7 care, advanced care planning, speech/swallow evaluation |
| **Stage 5** | Bedridden, unable to swallow, recurrent infections, significant weight loss, dementia | Consider hospice evaluation, palliative care, comfort-focused care |

**Post-DBS, families should focus on:** medication adherence, fall prevention, regular programming appointments, gentle exercise, maintaining independence, and seeking prompt medical attention for any signs of infection or complications.
