
# Chub Mackerel (Scomber japonicus) Across the Pacific Rim: Price Dynamics, Biological Variation, and Oceanographic Drivers

## Executive Summary

Chub mackerel (*Scomber japonicus*), also known as Pacific mackerel, is a small pelagic species of immense ecological and economic importance across the Pacific Rim. This report synthesizes findings from fisheries science, market intelligence, and oceanographic research to establish a holistic understanding of how marine environmental variability drives biological changes in the stock, which in turn propagate through price formation and market dynamics in major aquatic markets. The species is currently in an overfished state across much of its range, with interannual variations in body size and condition increasingly linked to climate drivers such as the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), and secular warming trends.

## Part I: Price Dynamics in Major Pacific Rim Aquatic Markets

### 1.1 Japan: The World's Largest Consumer Market

Japan is both a major harvester and the world's most important consumer market for chub mackerel. Wholesale prices at Japan's major central markets (including the Tokyo Metropolitan Central Wholesale Market, now relocated to Toyosu) have shown significant upward momentum in recent years.

Source: Tridge Frozen Mackerel Wholesale Price Summary (https://www.tridge.com/market-overview/frozen-mackerel)

Japan's domestic landed price in the Sanriku region was approximately **JPY 103/kg (USD 0.77/kg)** in November 2022, but the market strongly favors larger fish (>500 g). The shortage of large fish has been a persistent price driver. In 2022, Japan exported 119,319 MT of frozen mackerel at an average price of about **JPY 150/kg (USD 1.12/kg)**, with main markets in Vietnam, Thailand, and Egypt (https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/supply-trade/japan-a-leading-producer-of-mackerel-is-also-norway-s-most-important-market).

By 2026, a sharp price surge was reported in Japanese media. Domestic mackerel prices rose from approximately **¥8,500 to ¥12,000 per unit** (a ~41% increase) over a short span, driven by reduced Norwegian supply due to stricter quota regulations and the lingering effects of the Middle East geopolitical situation on shipping costs (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DtfPdZAtrEQ).

As of August 2026, the mackerel price in China was **USD 4.09/kg (CNY 28.69)**, down 12.9% year-on-year. The decline reflects a combination of robust domestic supply and softer global demand. Chinese wholesale prices for chub mackerel (FOB Ningbo) range from approximately **¥8.84/kg to ¥14.52/kg (USD 1.22–2.00/kg)** depending on size grade, with larger fish (50+ pcs/kg) commanding a premium (https://yorso.com/catalog/fish/mackerel/scomber-japonicus-pacific-chub-mackerel).

### 1.3 South Korea

South Korea's chub mackerel market has been under significant pressure. Wholesale prices for frozen mackerel in Korea ranged from **$2.97 to $6.65 USD/kg** over the past four weeks (as of mid-2026), with notable regional variation across Anyang-si, Seoul, Ansan-si, Daedeok-gu, and Busan. Import prices have been declining, from a range of $2.30–9.74/kg in 2023 to $2.30–8.80/kg in 2024 (https://dir.tridge.com/prices/frozen-mackerel/KR).

A critical market dynamic is the **declining competitiveness of domestic chub mackerel** due to a rising prevalence of smaller-sized fish (≤250 g). Korean consumers have increasingly shifted preference toward imported Norwegian Atlantic mackerel, which offers larger fillets and higher fat content—a trend that directly links biological deterioration of the local stock to market displacement (Jang et al., 2024, cited in Frontiers in Marine Science, 2026).

### 1.4 Peru and the Eastern Pacific

Peru's chub mackerel fishery is a significant contributor to the country's small-pelagic landings, averaging **60,000 tonnes annually** for the period 2010–2019, though with high interannual fluctuation (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0079661121001567). 

Export prices for Peruvian chub mackerel (CFR) were reported at approximately **$1,450/MT for 300–500g sea-frozen product**, with land-frozen product about $200/MT less (https://www.facebook.com/groups/886828398016284/posts/25065949609677492). The Selina Wamucii price database shows Peru's frozen mackerel (HS 030374) at reference prices updated through July 2026 (https://www.selinawamucii.com/insights/prices/peru/mackerel).

### 1.5 United States West Coast

The U.S. West Coast fishery for Pacific mackerel operates under precautionary harvest guidelines of **80,000–100,000 metric tons**. In 2021, California landings of Pacific mackerel totaled **1,906,752 lbs with an ex-vessel value of $481,349**—an average of approximately **$0.25/lb ($0.55/kg)** (https://nrm.dfg.ca.gov/FileHandler.ashx?DocumentID=198970). This is considerably lower than wholesale prices in Asia, reflecting the product's primary use as a lower-value protein source for canning and bait on the U.S. market.

### 1.6 Global Market Context

The global mackerel market was valued at approximately **USD 13.48 billion in 2025** and is forecast to reach **USD 17.45 billion by 2030**, growing at a CAGR of 5.31% (https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/mackerel-market). Chub mackerel held approximately **29.4% of the species share** in 2025, with Japan, China, South Korea, and the United States as the key harvest and consuming nations.

## Part II: Interannual Variations in Weight and Length

### 2.1 Baseline Biological Parameters

Chub mackerel exhibit a maximum length of **64 cm TL** and a maximum weight of **2.9 kg**, though common length is approximately **30 cm fork length** (https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Scomber-japonicus.html). The Bayesian length-weight relationship derived from global data is:

**W = 0.00741 × L³·⁰⁵** (cm total length, grams)

### 2.2 Recent Interannual Variation (2016–2021): Northwest Pacific

A comprehensive analysis of 2,686 chub mackerel specimens collected from the Northwest Pacific (2016–2020) revealed significant interannual heterogeneity in size and condition (https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/10/2/301):

- **Fork length range**: 40–294 mm (mean: 181.91 mm)
- **Body weight range**: 0.8–311.8 g (mean: 73.27 g)
- **Length-weight relationship**: W = (1.41 × 10⁻⁶) × FL³·³⁷
- **Regional difference**: Specimens from the high seas near Japan (mean FL ~220 mm) were substantially larger than those from the Zhejiang coastal area (~150 mm)

**Year-specific LWR parameters:**

| Year | a (×10⁻⁶) | b | Dominant FL Group |
|------|-----------|---|-------------------|
| 2016 | 2.96 | 3.37 | 200–210 mm |
| 2017 | 2.87 | 3.36 | 150–160 mm |
| 2018 | 3.64 | 3.31 | 200–210 mm |
| 2019 | 3.13 | 3.34 | 210–220 mm |
| 2020 | 3.58 | 3.35 | 210–220 mm |

**Relative condition factor** analysis showed that fish from 2017 and 2020 had the lowest body condition compared to historical baselines (pre-2006). The condition factor declined to below 1.0 for all years except when compared to the 2006–2016 baseline, indicating a **long-term decline in fish body condition** since the early years of the fishery.

### 2.3 Long-Term Trend in the Korean Fishery (2015–2024)

Data from the Busan Cooperative Fish Market, which handles approximately 90% of Korean domestic large purse seine chub mackerel landings, reveals a **dramatic and alarming shift toward smaller fish** (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1814806/full):

| Year | Proportion of Catch ≤250 g |
|------|---------------------------|
| 2015 | 65% |
| 2024 | 88% |

The 250 g threshold corresponds to an approximate total length of **29.7 cm** (using the species-specific LWR: W = 0.0025 × FL³·⁴⁶⁹⁶). This is significantly **below the length at 50% maturity (L₅₀ = 31.4 cm TL)**, indicating that the majority of the current catch consists of **immature, pre-spawning individuals**—a direct driver of recruitment overfishing.

### 2.4 Eastern Pacific (Peru)

Growth parameters for the Peruvian stock (1996–1998) showed asymptotic fork lengths of **40.2–42.2 cm** and growth coefficients (K) of **0.38–0.39/year**. The growth rate slows by approximately **50% during the cold season** relative to the warm season in the Gulf of Guayaquil region (https://worldfishcenter.org/publication/growth-and-length-weight-parameters-pacific-mackerel-scomber-japonicus-gulf-guayaquil).

### 2.5 Density-Dependent and Temperature-Driven Variation

The seminal work of Watanabe and Yatsu (2004) demonstrated that **interannual variation in length-at-age** of chub mackerel in the Kuroshio-Oyashio transition zone is driven by two primary factors:
- **Density dependence**: Higher population densities lead to slower growth due to competition for food resources
- **Sea surface temperature (SST)**: Warmer conditions during the growing season enhance growth rates, but extreme temperatures during spawning reduce survival

Source: https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/content/effects-density-dependence-and-sea-surface-temperature-interannual-variation-length-age

## Part III: Oceanographic Drivers and Climate Linkages

### 3.1 Sea Surface Temperature (SST)

SST is the single most critical environmental factor for chub mackerel at all life stages:

- **Spawning**: Optimal SST = **18°C**; suitable range = **15–22°C** (March–June spawning period)
- **Larval survival**: Tightly coupled to SST within the optimal window
- **Feeding and growth**: Optimal temperature ≈ **25°C**; observed in the high seas near Japan during summer

Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2022.996626/full

A Temperature Suitability Index (TSI) was developed to quantify spawning habitat quality:

**TSI = (1/√(2π)σ) × e^[-(t-μ)²/(2σ²)]** where μ = 18°C, σ = 2.0°C

### 3.2 ENSO and Climate Mode Forcing

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) exerts powerful interannual control over chub mackerel dynamics:

- **El Niño events**: Warm anomalies in the equatorial Pacific propagate to the Kuroshio region, altering spawning conditions. The 1997/98 extreme El Niño caused a **~48% decline** in Taiwanese mackerel purse-seine landings, with estimated revenue losses of **USD 6.2 million** (https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/e6c87962-c4f7-443c-b9e3-57d94e7c0470/content).

- **La Niña events**: Cooler conditions in the Peruvian Current System shift the ecological niche of chub mackerel. In the northern Peruvian Current System, 95% of chub mackerel records (1997–2018) occurred within SST of **16.12–26.15°C**, SSS of **34.35–35.41 PSU**, and chlorophyll-a of **0.22–4.99 mg/m³** (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0079661121001567).

- **Multi-decadal modes**: The Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), Southern Oscillation Index (SOI), Arctic Oscillation (AOI), and Monsoon Index (MOI) all show significant correlations with chub mackerel TSI and stock abundance. The Aleutian Low and Siberian High intensity modulate winter-spring temperature regimes that affect recruitment success 1–2 years later.

### 3.3 Secular Warming Trends

Korean waters have warmed by **1.58°C over the past 57 years (1968–2024)**—a rate far exceeding the global average. This warming is a potential confounding factor in stock assessment models and may be driving shifts in spawning grounds northward (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1814806/full).

Climate projection models (RCP 4.5 and 8.5) suggest that under the high-emission scenario, chub mackerel habitat in the Peruvian Current System could contract by **~47%** of current hotspot areas from 2040–2050 to 2090–2100, with habitats becoming increasingly coastal (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0079661121001567).

### 3.4 Chlorophyll and Primary Productivity

Chlorophyll-a concentration (CHL) is a secondary but significant driver. While SST has a clear fixed effect on CPUE, the relationship between log(CHL) and CPUE is more variable, showing random fluctuations at the monthly scale. However, regions with high primary productivity (e.g., the Zhejiang coastal area influenced by continental runoff) support chub mackerel with better body condition than the oligotrophic high seas (https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/9/8/323).

## Part IV: Synthesis — The Causal Chain from Ocean to Market

### 4.1 The Fishery Economics–Environment Linkage

The evidence assembled in this report supports a clear causal chain:

**Oceanographic forcing → Biological response → Catch composition → Market price → Fishery economics**

1. **ENSO and climate variability** alter SST and primary productivity in spawning and feeding grounds.

2. **Temperature anomalies** affect spawning success (TSI), larval survival, and subsequent recruitment 1–2 years later.

3. **Recruitment variability** determines the size and age structure of the fishable stock. Prolonged warm episodes or excessive fishing pressure shift the population toward smaller, younger individuals.

4. **Smaller fish** (≤250 g, below L₅₀ = 31.4 cm) have lower fillet yield (50–52% vs. 55–57% for Atlantic mackerel), lower fat content, and lower market value. In Korea, this has led to consumer preference shifts toward imported Norwegian mackerel, reducing competitiveness of the domestic fishery.

5. **Price formation** is driven by size-grade premiums. In Japan, the scarcity of large fish (>500 g) has pushed wholesale prices above $7.00/kg, while in China, the export unit value declined 12.9% YoY due to supply-side pressure from abundant small fish.

### 4.2 Stock Status as the Ultimate Constraint

Both the CMSY++ and Bayesian State-Space (BSS) stock assessment models consistently classify the chub mackerel stock as **"Overfished"** as of 2024:

| Model | B₂₀₂₄ (tons) | B/BMSY | Status |
|-------|-------------|--------|--------|
| CMSY++ | 779,191 | 0.63 | Overfished |
| BSS | 549,884 | 0.59 | Overfished |

Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1814806/full

In response, Japan reduced its Pacific coast catch quota by **60% to 139,000 tonnes** for 2025—approximately half the estimated MSY (https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276). This regulatory tightening will further constrain supply and is expected to maintain upward pressure on wholesale prices in the Japanese market.

### 4.3 Market Segmentation by End-Use

The biological quality of chub mackerel directly influences its market channel:

| Size Grade | Fat Content | Typical End-Use | Price Premium |
|-----------|------------|----------------|--------------|
| >500 g WR | 5–10% lipid | Sashimi, grilled, simmered (Japan, Korea) | High |
| 300–500 g WR | 3–7% lipid | Canned loins, breaded portions | Medium |
| <300 g WR | 1–4% lipid | Canning, fishmeal, bait | Low |

Source: https://www.easyfish.net/en/atlantic-vs-pacific-chub-mackerel

The fat content premium that drives Atlantic mackerel pricing (≥18% fat commands +$150–200/MT) is **rarely applicable to chub mackerel**, which typically has 1–10% lipid. Instead, chub mackerel pricing is dominated by **size grade** and **yield percentage**.

## Part V: Conclusions and Research Implications

### 5.1 Key Findings

1. **Price dynamics** across Pacific Rim markets show a clear hierarchy: Japan (highest wholesale prices, $7.00/kg), China (export unit value ~$4.09/kg, declining), South Korea (volatile, $2.97–$6.65/kg), and the U.S. West Coast (lowest, ~$0.25–$0.55/kg ex-vessel).

2. **Interannual variation in body size** is severe: the proportion of small fish (≤250 g) in the Korean catch rose from 65% (2015) to 88% (2024), signaling a population increasingly dominated by immature individuals.

3. **Oceanographic forcing** via ENSO, PDO, and long-term warming directly modulates recruitment, growth, and distribution. The 1.58°C warming of Korean waters over 57 years is a leading indicator of habitat change.

4. **The stock is overfished** (B/BMSY = 0.59–0.63), and management responses (e.g., Japan's 60% quota cut) will have immediate and lagged effects on market supply and price.

### 5.2 Research Recommendations

To further establish direct correlations between marine biological resources, aquatic markets, fishery economics, and the marine environment, future research should:

- **Integrate satellite-derived oceanographic data** (SST, CHL, SSH) with ex-vessel price time series at the fleet level to quantify the price elasticity of environmental variability.
- **Develop size-structured bioeconomic models** that link the TSI (spawning temperature suitability) and recruitment to market composition and price formation with a 1–3 year lag.
- **Conduct consumer preference studies** across Japan, Korea, and China to quantify the willingness-to-pay premium for larger, wild-caught chub mackerel versus imported alternatives.
- **Establish a Pacific Rim price index** for chub mackerel by size grade, standardized to a common product form (e.g., HGT 20 kg, 300–500 g), to enable cross-market comparison and price transmission analysis.
