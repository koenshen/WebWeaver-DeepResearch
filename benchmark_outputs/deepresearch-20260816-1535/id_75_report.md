

# Modulating Plasma Metal Ion Concentrations as a Preventive or Therapeutic Strategy Against Cardiovascular Diseases: Evidence and Interventions

## Executive Summary

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of death globally, and interest has grown in whether modulating plasma metal ion concentrations—via supplementation, chelation, or removal—could serve as preventive or therapeutic strategies. The evidence is decidedly mixed. While **potassium supplementation** (via salt substitutes) has the strongest clinical support for reducing stroke and cardiovascular events, and **iron removal** (phlebotomy/chelation) is well-established for treating iron-overload cardiomyopathy, most other interventions (magnesium, calcium, selenium, zinc, copper, chromium) lack definitive proof of benefit from large randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Observational studies frequently show U-shaped associations between metal levels and CVD risk, implying that both deficiency and excess are harmful. The failure of the TACT2 trial to replicate the earlier TACT findings on EDTA chelation underscores the scientific uncertainty in this field.

---

## 1. Conceptual Framework: Why Metal Ions Matter in Cardiovascular Health

Metal ions play essential roles in cardiovascular physiology—maintaining ion channel function, regulating vascular tone, serving as enzyme cofactors, and modulating oxidative stress and inflammation. Exogenous metal ions (Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Sr²⁺, Mn²⁺, Co²⁺) can protect the cardiovascular system through antioxidant mechanisms, promotion of angiogenesis, and regulation of ion channels and pH [1]. Conversely, toxic heavy metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury) promote atherosclerosis through oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation [2].

Crucially, for many essential metals the relationship with CVD risk is **U-shaped**: both deficient and excessive levels increase risk. This complexity means that "more is not always better," and indiscriminate supplementation can be harmful.

## 2. Supplementation Strategies: The Evidence

### 2.1 Potassium: The Strongest Clinical Evidence

Potassium supplementation represents the most successful metal-ion intervention to date. The landmark **Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS)**—a cluster-randomized trial of 20,995 Chinese adults with prior stroke or hypertension aged ≥60—demonstrated that replacing regular salt (100% NaCl) with a salt substitute (75% NaCl + 25% KCl) over ~4.7 years reduced:

- **Stroke risk by 14%** (rate ratio 0.86; 95% CI 0.77–0.96; p=0.006)
- **Major cardiovascular events by 13%** (rate ratio 0.87; 95% CI 0.80–0.94; p<0.001)
- **All-cause mortality by 12%** (rate ratio 0.88; 95% CI 0.82–0.95; p<0.001)

with no significant increase in hyperkalemia-related serious adverse events [3].

A subsequent analysis estimated that **potassium supplementation drove roughly three-quarters or more of the blood-pressure-lowering effect** observed in SSaSS, highlighting the independent importance of potassium beyond sodium reduction [4]. The WHO recommends increasing potassium intake to at least 90 mmol/day (3510 mg/day) from food to reduce blood pressure and CVD risk [5]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (n=32,073) confirmed that salt substitutes significantly reduce systolic BP (mean difference −4.80 mmHg) and all-cause mortality (HR 0.88) [6].

**Clinical status:** Potassium supplementation via salt substitutes is an evidence-based, low-cost, population-level intervention supported by major guideline bodies.

### 2.2 Magnesium: Modest Blood Pressure Effects, Uncertain Outcomes

Magnesium plays critical roles in vascular tone, cardiac conduction, and blood pressure regulation. A 2025 meta-analysis of 38 RCTs (n=2,709) found magnesium supplementation reduced:

- Systolic blood pressure by **−2.81 mmHg** (95% CI −4.32 to −1.29)
- Diastolic blood pressure by **−2.05 mmHg** (95% CI −3.23 to −0.88)

with greater effects in populations with hypertension and hypomagnesemia [7].

Epidemiological evidence is supportive: a meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies found that each 100 mg/day increment in dietary magnesium was associated with a 7% lower risk of stroke, 22% lower risk of heart failure, 19% lower risk of type 2 diabetes, and 10% lower risk of all-cause mortality [8]. A dose-response meta-analysis of 19 prospective studies (1,168,756 participants) found dietary magnesium associated with lower all-cause mortality, but **not with CVD mortality** specifically [9].

In the acute MI setting, the LIMIT-2 trial (2,316 patients) found intravenous magnesium sulfate reduced early mortality by 24% and left ventricular failure by 25% [10]. However, the larger ISIS-4 trial (58,050 patients) found no mortality benefit, possibly because magnesium was given later after reperfusion. The Magnesium in Acute Myocardial Infarction meta-analysis ultimately showed no survival benefit in modern reperfusion-era trials.

**Clinical status:** Magnesium supplementation lowers blood pressure modestly and improves endothelial function, but there is no definitive evidence that it reduces hard cardiovascular outcomes in the general population. It is most reasonable in individuals with documented deficiency or hypomagnesemia.

### 2.3 Calcium: Safety Concerns but No Proven Hazard

Calcium supplements have been widely used for bone health, but concerns about cardiovascular safety have been raised. A 2023 meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials (n=55,438) found that calcium supplementation (mean dose 1 g/day), with or without vitamin D, was **not significantly associated with any excess risk** of myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease death, any CHD, stroke, or all-cause mortality [11]. Risk ratios ranged from 1.01 to 1.15 for calcium alone, and from 1.02 to 1.09 for calcium plus vitamin D—none statistically significant.

However, the authors acknowledged the analysis could not exclude small absolute excess risks of 0.3–0.5% per year for CHD or stroke. Earlier meta-analyses (e.g., 2021, pooling 13 trials) had suggested a potential 16% increased risk of CHD with calcium supplementation, particularly in trials with low risk of bias [12]. A Mendelian randomization study found a 1 SD higher genetically-predicted plasma calcium was associated with a 25% higher risk of CHD (OR 1.25; 95% CI 1.08–1.45) [11].

**Clinical status:** For individuals with low bone density and low baseline CVD risk, calcium supplements appear safe. However, they should not be used for cardiovascular prevention, and the balance of evidence suggests caution in high-CVD-risk populations.

### 2.4 Selenium: U-Shaped Risk, No Proven Supplementation Benefit

Selenium is an essential component of antioxidant selenoproteins. Observational studies consistently show a **U-shaped relationship** between selenium status and CVD mortality—both low and high levels are associated with increased risk. In a study of 929 hypertensive patients from NHANES 2003–2004, serum selenium showed a U-shaped association with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with the lowest mortality at approximately 130–136 μg/L [13].

A Cochrane systematic review of selenium supplementation for CVD prevention (12 trials, n=19,715) found **no statistically significant effect** on all-cause mortality (RR 0.97), CVD mortality (RR 0.97), or non-fatal CVD events (RR 0.96) [14]. Notably, the SELECT trial even raised concerns about increased type 2 diabetes risk with selenium supplementation.

However, a 2025 meta-analysis of 20 cohort studies found that each 1-SD increase in selenium biomarker concentration was associated with 11% lower cardiovascular mortality (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.84–0.94), suggesting selenium status matters for CVD risk in observational settings [15]. The Finnish nationwide selenium fertilization program, which raised serum selenium levels, did not reduce CVD rates, suggesting selenium supplementation may not confer benefit in already-sufficient populations.

**Clinical status:** Selenium supplementation is not recommended for CVD prevention in selenium-replete populations. Correction of true deficiency (e.g., Keshan disease prevention) is the only clear indication.

### 2.5 Zinc: Promising but Limited Evidence

Zinc is essential for cardiac antioxidant defense (superoxide dismutase), nitric oxide signaling, and regulation of inflammatory cytokines. Zinc deficiency is common in heart failure patients and is associated with worse outcomes. A systematic review found that heart failure patients frequently have zinc deficiency, and two small randomized double-blind trials evaluating micronutrient supplementation including zinc found **improvement in echocardiographic findings** compared with placebo [16].

A landmark case report described a 24-year-old woman with anorexia nervosa and severe cardiomyopathy (LVEF 0.10) whose cardiac function recovered to LVEF 0.60 after zinc supplementation, establishing zinc deficiency as a reversible cause of heart failure [17]. A pilot RCT (NCT00696410) of zinc acetate (50 mg three times daily for 10 months) in nonischemic cardiomyopathy was completed in 2011, but definitive large-scale results remain lacking [18].

A 2023 meta-analysis of zinc supplementation and CVD risk factors (GRADE-assessed) found improvements in several surrogate markers, but hard cardiovascular outcomes were not evaluated [19]. Preclinical work suggests zinc supplementation reduces oxidative stress and myocardial inflammatory signaling [1].

**Clinical status:** Zinc supplementation appears reasonable in patients with documented zinc deficiency (common in heart failure, elderly, and malnourished populations), but there is insufficient evidence to recommend routine zinc supplementation for CVD prevention in zinc-replete individuals.

### 2.6 Copper: Deficiency and Excess Both Problematic

Copper is critical for mitochondrial respiration (cytochrome c oxidase), antioxidant defense (SOD1), and connective tissue integrity. Epidemiological data show **U-shaped associations** between serum copper and cardiovascular mortality—both extremes linked to elevated risk [20].

Copper deficiency has been proposed as a contributor to ischemic heart disease. Small trials of copper supplementation (2 mg/day) in adults with moderate hypercholesterolemia increased erythrocyte SOD1 activity and improved markers of lipoprotein oxidation resistance [21]. A review of >60 publications involving >2,500 patients with copper malnutrition reported >1,000 patients benefited from copper supplementation [22]. Conversely, copper overload increases oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and contributes to "cuproptosis"—a newly recognized form of copper-induced cell death in the myocardium.

**Clinical status:** Copper supplementation may benefit individuals with true deficiency, but routine supplementation is not recommended due to the narrow therapeutic window and the dual risks of deficiency and toxicity. Emerging research on copper chelation and copper chaperone modulation (e.g., ATOX1 targeting) remains at experimental stages [20].

### 2.7 Chromium: Emerging but Unproven

Chromium supplementation has been proposed to improve insulin sensitivity, glucose metabolism, and inflammatory markers relevant to CVD. A 2021 meta-analysis found chromium supplementation significantly reduced inflammatory biomarkers (hs-CRP, TNF-α, IL-6)—key CVD risk factors [23]. However, effects on lipids and hard clinical outcomes remain inconsistent, and no large RCT has evaluated chromium's effect on cardiovascular events.

**Clinical status:** Not recommended for CVD prevention pending definitive trials.

## 3. Chelation and Removal Strategies: Removing Excess Metals

### 3.1 Iron Removal: The Best-Established Metal-Modulating Therapy

**Hereditary hemochromatosis and transfusional iron overload** are the clearest examples where modulating metal ion concentrations is an effective, guideline-recommended cardiovascular therapy. Iron overload causes cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and heart failure through free radical generation.

**Therapeutic phlebotomy** is first-line therapy for non-anemic patients with iron overload. Weekly removal of 1 unit (450–500 mL) of blood until serum ferritin reaches 10–20 μg/L, followed by maintenance below 50 μg/L, has been shown to:

- Lower myocardial iron content
- Improve left ventricular diameter, fractional shortening, LVEF, and left ventricular mass
- Prevent progression to cardiomyopathy [24]

**Iron chelation therapy** is used for anemic patients or those with severe heart failure who cannot tolerate phlebotomy. Three FDA-approved chelators exist:

- **Deferoxamine** (subcutaneous/IV): reduces myocardial iron by ~24%, delays/reverses cardiac hemochromatosis, improves LV function, and improves survival in thalassemia patients [24]
- **Deferiprone** (oral): retrospective and prospective data suggest superior cardiac iron removal compared with deferoxamine, with better cardiac disease-free survival (p=0.003 in one 5-year analysis) [25]
- **Deferasirox** (oral): effectively lowers serum ferritin and cardiac/liver iron

Cardiac MRI T2* values guide treatment intensity: T2* <10 ms indicates high heart-failure risk (47% of thalassemia patients with T2* <6 ms developed heart failure within 1 year) [24].

**Clinical status:** Well-established, life-saving therapy for iron-overload cardiomyopathy—the strongest evidence that metal modulation can treat cardiovascular disease.

### 3.2 EDTA Chelation for Heavy Metals (Lead, Cadmium): TACT vs. TACT2

The hypothesis that chelation of toxic metals (lead, cadmium) could reduce atherosclerotic CVD arose from the observation that these metals promote oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis [26].

**The original TACT trial** (n=1,708 post-MI patients) reported that 40 weekly infusions of a disodium EDTA-based chelation regimen reduced the composite primary endpoint (death, reinfarction, stroke, revascularization, hospitalization for angina) by 18% (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.69–0.99; p=0.035), with an even larger 41% reduction (HR 0.59) in patients with diabetes [27]. EDTA infusions increased lead excretion by 3,830% and cadmium by 514%, confirming effective mobilization of toxic metals [26].

**The confirmatory TACT2 trial** (n=1,000 post-MI patients with diabetes) was specifically designed to replicate the promising diabetic subgroup finding. Despite achieving a **61% reduction in blood lead levels**, EDTA chelation produced **no significant reduction** in cardiovascular events, cardiovascular death, MI, or stroke compared with placebo (35.6% vs. 35.7% event rates) [28, 29]. The trial was definitively negative.

**Interpretation:** The TACT2 result effectively does not support EDTA chelation as a cardiovascular therapy in contemporary US/Canadian patients, likely because population blood lead levels have declined substantially since the original TACT era. The hypothesis that toxic metal reduction could benefit CVD remains mechanistically plausible in heavily exposed populations, but requires further research. Mayo Clinic and FDA guidance explicitly state chelation is not a proven heart disease treatment [30].

### 3.3 Copper Chelation: Experimental

Given the U-shaped risk for copper and the role of copper overload in oxidative cardiac injury, copper chelators and "cuproptosis" modulators are being investigated preclinically for cardiomyopathy, heart failure, and atherosclerosis. Mitochondria-directed copper depletion, copper ionophores, and dual iron/copper chelation approaches remain in early experimental stages [20].

## 4. Other Metals of Interest

### 4.1 Manganese
Manganese is a cofactor for mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), which is essential for cardiac antioxidant defense. Observational studies show manganese deficiency is associated with increased CVD risk, and supplementation improved markers of vascular inflammation (ICAM-1, MCP-1) in animal models. However, human trials of manganese supplementation for cardiovascular outcomes are lacking, and manganese excess is neurotoxic [31].

### 4.2 Cobalt
Cobalt ions promote angiogenesis by mimicking hypoxia (stabilizing HIF-1α, upregulating VEGF). This has generated interest in cobalt-based biomaterials and local delivery systems for cardiac regeneration. However, systemic cobalt is cardiotoxic—elevated cobalt from metal implants is associated with cardiomyopathy and heart failure. No clinical trials of cobalt supplementation for CVD exist [1, 32].

### 4.3 Strontium
Strontium ions may regulate vascular calcification and pH in ischemic myocardium, but clinical evidence for cardiovascular benefit is absent [1].

## 5. Overall Assessment: Is Modulating Plasma Metal Ions a Viable Strategy?

### 5.1 Where the Evidence is Strong

| Intervention | Evidence Strength | Clinical Recommendation Status |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium (salt substitutes) | **Strong** — Large RCT (SSaSS), meta-analyses, WHO support | Recommended for hypertension/stroke prevention |
| Iron removal (phlebotomy/chelation) | **Strong** — Established therapy for iron overload | Guideline-recommended for hemochromatosis/transfusion iron overload |
| Magnesium (IV in AMI, oral for BP) | **Moderate** — Positive BP meta-analyses, mixed AMI results | Reasonable for hypomagnesemia/hypertension; not routine for CVD prevention |
| Calcium | **Moderate (safety)** — No significant hazard shown, small risks not excluded | Not recommended for CVD prevention; safe for bone health in low-risk patients |

### 5.2 Where Evidence is Weak or Absent

| Intervention | Evidence Strength | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Selenium supplementation | **Weak** — Observational U-shape, null RCTs | Not recommended for CVD prevention |
| Zinc supplementation | **Weak-Moderate** — Deficiency correction beneficial, no large outcome trials | Reasonable for documented deficiency |
| Copper supplementation | **Weak** — Deficiency correction may help, toxicity risk | Not routinely recommended |
| Chromium | **Weak** — Surrogate marker improvements only | Not recommended |
| Manganese, cobalt, strontium | **Preclinical only** | Experimental |

### 5.3 Key Challenges and Caveats

1. **U-shaped relationships**: For selenium, copper, and manganese, both deficiency and excess increase CVD risk. Supplementation in replete individuals may cause harm. Personalizing therapy requires reliable biomarkers of metal status.

2. **Observational vs. interventional divergence**: Selenium and magnesium show strong epidemiological associations with lower CVD risk, yet supplementation trials fail to show benefit—suggesting confounding or that supplementation cannot correct long-standing tissue-level deficiencies.

3. **The TACT/TACT2 lesson**: A positive subgroup finding in one trial (TACT diabetic subgroup) was not confirmed in a dedicated trial (TACT2), highlighting the dangers of over-interpreting secondary analyses and the need for confirmatory trials.

4. **Population-specific effects**: Metal interventions may benefit specific populations (iron chelation in thalassemia, potassium in high-sodium consumers, zinc in deficient elderly) without generalizing to the broader population.

5. **Safety**: Metal supplements are not benign. Calcium may carry small CVD risks, selenium may increase diabetes risk, and excess copper/manganese are toxic.

---

## 6. Future Directions

- **Personalized medicine approaches** using plasma metal profiles to identify individuals with deficiencies or toxic metal burdens who may benefit from targeted intervention [33]
- **Nanoparticle-based local delivery** of metal ions (magnesium, zinc, cobalt) to ischemic myocardium or vascular lesions, currently in preclinical development [1]
- **Triple iron chelation regimens** (deferoxamine + deferasirox + deferiprone) for severe cardiac iron overload [34]
- **Large, adequately powered RCTs** in populations with documented metal deficiencies or elevated toxic metal levels, rather than unselected general populations
- **Investigation of metal chaperones and metal-regulatory proteins** (ATOX1, CTR1, ferroportin, hepcidin) as novel therapeutic targets [20]

---

## Conclusion

Modulating plasma metal ion concentrations **can** be an effective preventive or therapeutic strategy against cardiovascular disease, but only in specific, evidence-supported contexts. **Potassium supplementation** (via salt substitutes) has the strongest evidence for reducing stroke and cardiovascular mortality. **Iron removal** through phlebotomy or chelation is a proven, life-saving therapy for iron-overload cardiomyopathy. For most other metals—magnesium, calcium, selenium, zinc, copper, chromium—supplementation shows promising surrogate-marker effects but lacks definitive evidence for reducing hard cardiovascular outcomes, and U-shaped risk curves mean that indiscriminate supplementation can be harmful. The failure of TACT2 reinforces that metal modulation is not a "one-size-fits-all" strategy but must be targeted, personalized, and evidence-based.
