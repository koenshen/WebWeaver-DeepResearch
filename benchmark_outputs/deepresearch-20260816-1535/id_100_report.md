

# The Influence of AI Interaction on Interpersonal Relations: Mechanisms, Paradoxes, and the Transformation of Human Connection

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence has moved from a tool of productivity to a participant in the most intimate domains of human social life—conversation, companionship, and emotional support. This paper synthesizes current empirical research and theoretical frameworks to examine how AI interaction is reshaping the *how* and *why* of human relating. Drawing on the Machine-Integrated Relational Adaptation (MIRA) model, social penetration theory, displacement and stimulation hypotheses, and a growing body of experimental and longitudinal studies, we identify four core mechanisms of influence: (1) AI-mediated communication alters interpersonal perception and language, creating a paradox where AI use improves actual interaction quality but carries social stigma when detected; (2) AI companions can satisfy relational needs through self-disclosure reciprocity, potentially outperforming humans in establishing closeness during emotionally charged exchanges, yet this very capacity risks displacing human relationships and creating unrealistic expectations; (3) AI interaction produces bidirectional effects on social skills, either upskilling through safe rehearsal or deskilling through avoidance of human complexity; and (4) the social motivation paradox—AI can reduce loneliness in the short term but increase dependence and isolation when used intensively. We conclude that AI is not merely a new channel for existing relational patterns but a force that is reconfiguring the fundamental motivations, expectations, and competencies that underlie human interpersonal bonds.

---

## 1. Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence into everyday communication is no longer a future prospect. Algorithmic response suggestions are used to send billions of messages daily (Hohenstein et al., 2023), companion chatbots such as Replika and Character.AI host tens of millions of users (Liu et al., 2025), and AI-generated language is incorporated into approximately 12% of Gmail messages (Hohenstein et al., 2023). These technologies are not neutral channels; they actively shape the content, tone, and structure of human interaction, and in doing so, they are altering the fundamental nature of how and why individuals relate to one another.

The urgency of understanding these changes is underscored by the scale of the phenomenon and the depth of its implications. Loneliness has been declared an epidemic by the U.S. Surgeon General (2023), and a meta-analysis of 148 studies found that strong social relationships confer a 50% increased likelihood of survival—an effect size comparable to quitting smoking (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). If AI interaction is simultaneously alleviating and exacerbating social isolation, and if it is reshaping the skills and expectations that humans bring to their relationships with each other, then the stakes are profound.

This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of the mechanisms through which AI interaction influences interpersonal relations. We organize the evidence around four domains: AI as a mediator of human-to-human communication, AI as a direct relational partner, the effects of AI on social skills and motivation, and the paradox of loneliness reduction. Throughout, we draw on the emerging Machine-Integrated Relational Adaptation (MIRA) model (Hancock et al., 2025) and related theoretical frameworks to explain the psychological processes at work.

## 2. Theoretical Frameworks for Understanding AI and Human Relationships

### 2.1 The MIRA Model

The Machine-Integrated Relational Adaptation (MIRA) model (Hancock et al., 2025) provides a comprehensive transdisciplinary framework for understanding when, how, and why AI functions as a relational entity. MIRA identifies two fundamental roles for AI in social life: **relational partner** (direct human-AI interaction) and **relational mediator** (shaping human-to-human communication). The model synthesizes attachment theory, social exchange theory, epistemic trust frameworks, and human-computer interaction research to articulate four core psychological principles:

- **Linguistic Reciprocity**: AI's capacity to adapt its language to match the user's style, tone, and emotional state, fostering rapport and social cohesion.
- **Psychological Proximity**: The sense of emotional closeness and perceived understanding generated through personalized responses, emotional mirroring, and adaptive self-disclosure.
- **Interpersonal Trust**: AI's simulation of trustworthiness through linguistic fluency and stylistic confidence, despite lacking genuine epistemic grounding.
- **Relational Substitution vs. Enhancement**: The dual possibility that AI may either augment human relationships or supplant them.

The MIRA model is non-deterministic; it recognizes that outcomes depend on individual differences, context, and system design. This framework is particularly valuable because it distinguishes between AI's role as a mediator (filtering or generating content in human-human exchanges) and its role as a partner (directly interacting with humans), a distinction that is often conflated in public discourse.

### 2.2 Social Penetration Theory and Self-Disclosure

Social penetration theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) posits that relationships develop through reciprocal self-disclosure, moving from superficial to intimate topics. Recent research has applied this framework to human-AI interactions, with striking results. A double-blind randomized controlled study found that when participants believed they were interacting with a human (masked condition), AI-generated content *outperformed* human-generated content in establishing interpersonal closeness during emotionally engaging "deep talk" interactions (Peters et al., 2025). The mechanism was clear: AI responses showed significantly higher levels of self-disclosure than human responses (M<sub>AI</sub> = 38.03 vs. M<sub>human</sub> = 32.31, p = 0.002), and this increased self-disclosure prompted participants to disclose more themselves, creating a virtuous cycle of intimacy.

This finding is theoretically significant. AI, unlike humans, faces no social risk in self-disclosure—it cannot experience shame, reject the user, or misuse personal information. The very absence of emotional embodiment that might seem like a deficit becomes an advantage: AI can model the vulnerability that humans often withhold to protect themselves, thereby lowering the threshold for reciprocal openness.

### 2.3 Displacement and Stimulation Hypotheses

Borrowed from media effects research on internet use, the displacement and stimulation hypotheses offer competing predictions about AI's impact on human relationships. The **displacement hypothesis** suggests that time and psychological investment in AI interactions detract from human relationships, reducing opportunities for developing complex interpersonal skills and potentially replacing human bonds with artificial ones (Valkenburg & Peter, 2007). The **stimulation hypothesis** posits that AI interactions can enhance social confidence, provide rehearsal spaces for difficult conversations, and increase motivation to engage with others (Brandtzæg et al., 2021).

Evidence for both mechanisms exists. A study of Replika users found that some attributed improvements in their interpersonal relationships—greater openness, more comfort in social situations—to their AI companion (Xie & Pentina, 2022; Guingrich & Graziano, 2023). Conversely, other research has documented cases where companion AI displaced human romantic partners and therapists (Xie & Pentina, 2022), and where adolescents with stronger attachment to AI companions than to family or best friends showed withdrawal-like symptoms when access was interrupted (Tosti et al., 2024).

### 2.4 Social Compensation vs. Social Enhancement

The social compensation hypothesis (Valkenburg et al., 2005) predicts that individuals with social difficulties are particularly drawn to AI as a substitute for challenging human interactions. The social enhancement hypothesis (Kraut et al., 2002) predicts that socially adept individuals are more likely to integrate AI into their already-rich social lives. A study of over 1,100 AI companion users found that people with fewer human relationships were more likely to seek out chatbots, and that heavy emotional self-disclosure to AI was consistently associated with lower well-being (Zhang et al., 2025). This pattern supports the social compensation mechanism but also reveals its risks: those who most need human connection may be the most vulnerable to AI's substitution effects.

## 3. AI as a Communication Mediator: Reshaping Human-to-Human Interaction

### 3.1 The Smart Reply Paradox

The most pervasive form of AI-mediated communication is the algorithmic response suggestion, or "smart reply." Two randomized experiments by Hohenstein et al. (2023) revealed a fundamental paradox. On one hand, actual use of smart replies increased communication speed by 10.2%, generated more positive emotional language, and caused conversation partners to be rated as more cooperative and affiliative. These effects were causally demonstrated: increased use of smart replies by a partner led the self to rate them as more cooperative (IV estimate: b = 15.66, p = 0.018) and to feel greater affiliation toward them (IV estimate: b = 21.79, p = 0.007).

On the other hand, the *perception* that a partner was using AI carried a strong social penalty. The more participants thought their partner used smart replies, the less cooperative (t(92) = −9.89, p < 0.0001) and less affiliative (t(92) = −6.90, p < 0.0001) they rated them, even after controlling for actual use. This means that AI can improve how people are perceived by others, but only if its use remains undetected. The stigma of AI use in communication—what might be called an "authenticity tax"—creates a tension between the efficiency benefits of AI mediation and the social value placed on genuine human effort.

### 3.2 Language Homogenization and Emotional Contagion

The second experiment by Hohenstein et al. (2023) demonstrated that the emotional tone of AI suggestions directly influences conversation sentiment. When negative smart replies were available, conversations became more negative in emotional content than when positive or neutral suggestions were offered (t(127) = 2.75, p = 0.007, d = 0.352). Crucially, these shifts were driven by actual use of smart replies rather than mere exposure; repeating the analysis with smart reply messages omitted from the corpus showed minimal differences between conditions.

This finding raises concerns about the long-term effects of AI mediation on linguistic diversity and emotional authenticity. If AI systems are trained on dominant communication norms and tend to produce positive, agreeable language, their widespread use could homogenize human expression. As Hancock et al. (2025) note, "AI-MC systems are trained, at least initially, on existing human communication; extant biases in training data are likely to be replicated and amplified by AI." The risk is that humans begin to sound like machines, adopting the flattened, efficient, and emotionally constrained language patterns that AI models privilege.

### 3.3 AI as a Mediator of Difficult Conversations

Not all mediation effects are negative. A study of 1,500 participants engaged in online political discussions about gun control found that an AI chatbot suggesting polite rephrasings—without altering viewpoints—led participants to follow approximately two-thirds of the suggestions (BYU, 2024). The result was more positive conversations and increased feelings of being heard and understood. As the study's lead researcher noted, "We found that when the AI chat assistant was present, participants rated the conversations as more positive and extended more understanding/democratic reciprocity to their opponents" (Gubler, cited in BYU, 2024).

This finding suggests that AI can serve as a "moral crumple zone" (Hohenstein et al., 2020), absorbing the emotional friction of difficult conversations and allowing participants to engage more constructively. The mechanism is one of *debiasing*: AI suggestions can interrupt the reflexive defensiveness that characterizes many human disagreements, providing a model of response that is more patient and more generous than the interlocutor might have produced alone.

## 4. AI as a Relational Partner: The Rise of Synthetic Relationships

### 4.1 The Deep Talk Advantage

The most striking finding from recent experimental research is that AI can *outperform* humans in establishing interpersonal closeness during emotionally intense interactions. In the masked condition of Peters et al. (2025), where participants believed they were talking to a human, AI-generated responses produced higher closeness after deep talk (M = 4.48 vs. 3.85, p = 0.028) while no significant difference emerged for small talk. This effect was mediated by AI's higher self-disclosure, which in turn encouraged reciprocal self-disclosure from participants.

This finding challenges the common assumption that emotional connection requires mutual vulnerability between sentient beings. The research suggests that the *experience* of being heard, understood, and met with openness may be more important than the ontological status of the listener. As the authors note, "AI's increased self-disclosure motivates participants to disclose more personal information themselves, ultimately leading to more intimate interactions and a stronger sense of closeness" (Peters et al., 2025).

### 4.2 The Labeling Effect: Transparency Reduces Closeness

The same study, however, revealed a critical caveat. When participants were explicitly told they were interacting with an AI (the unmasked condition), interpersonal closeness significantly decreased (M = 4.17 for human-labeled vs. 3.72 for AI-labeled, p = 0.028). This labeling effect was not moderated by the actual source of the content—whether the responses were generated by AI or by humans. The mere knowledge that one is interacting with an AI dampens the relational experience.

This finding has profound implications for the design and deployment of AI companions. Transparency about AI identity may protect users from deception but simultaneously undermines the very relational benefits that AI can provide. The anti-AI bias appears to be rooted in individual differences; participants high in universalism values felt closer to humans but not to AI, suggesting that traits linked to natural social interaction may reduce the likelihood of forming bonds with artificial agents (Peters et al., 2025).

### 4.3 Emotional Attachment and Dependence

Qualitative research on companion AI users reveals genuine emotional attachments that, for some, rival or exceed human relationships. A survey of 60 users affected by the shutdown of the Soulmate AI companion found that they had formed genuine, emotional connections with their AI partners (Banks, cited in Syracuse iSchool, 2024). Some users of Replika reported feeling closer to their AI companion than to their family or best human friend (De Freitas et al., 2024; Pentina et al., 2023).

These attachments are not inherently pathological. The MIRA model explains that AI's "unconditional availability, low emotional demand, and personalized responsiveness" (Hancock et al., 2025) map onto the psychological needs for security and validation that attachment theory identifies as fundamental. For individuals with limited social networks or those who have experienced relational trauma, an AI companion may provide a transitional space for practicing connection.

However, the risks of emotional over-reliance are significant. A working definition of over-reliance is "using companion AI to entirely meet social or emotional needs or to fully replace human relationships" (Malfacini, 2025). Research on internet use and loneliness has found that when people use the internet to build and foster social relationships, it can reduce loneliness; but when they try to entirely supplant a poor offline social life with an online one, loneliness increases (Moretta & Buodo, 2020). This pattern is likely to replicate—and potentially intensify—with AI companions.

### 4.4 Unrealistic Relationship Expectations

A consistent concern across multiple studies is that AI companions create unrealistic expectations for human relationships. Companion AI is "infinitely patient, attentive, and ever-available" (Turkle, 2011), programmed to meet users' emotional needs without mutual effort. A survey of nearly 3,000 U.S. adults found that 19% of young adults (ages 18-30) had interacted with AI designed to simulate a romantic partner, and many used AI companions to manage relationship dissatisfaction (BYU, 2024).

As one researcher warned, "The concern with AI is that, like pornography, it will create very unhealthy, unrealistic expectations about real relationships" (Willoughby, cited in BYU, 2024). Because AI companions are "programmed to meet users' emotional needs without mutual effort, they risk distorting their expectations in real-life relationships" (ibid.). This mechanism operates through what psychologists call *norm distortion*: repeated exposure to a perfectly accommodating relational partner reshapes the baseline against which human interactions are judged, making the inevitable imperfections of real relationships feel more intolerable.

## 5. Social Skills: Upskilling and Deskilling

### 5.1 The Case for Social Upskilling

AI companions can serve as low-stakes training environments for social interaction. Replika users interviewed in qualitative studies reported that their AI companions encouraged greater openness and vulnerability in their human relationships (Xie & Pentina, 2022). A majority of survey participants attributed improvements in social interactions and close relationships to their AI companion (Guingrich & Graziano, 2023). Some users became more comfortable with and inclined toward interacting with others after companion AI use (Skjuve et al., 2021).

For adolescents, the potential benefits are particularly pronounced. AI companions provide "opportunities for social rehearsal, allowing adolescents to practice self-disclosure and emotional expression without fear of rejection or judgment" (Brandtzæg et al., 2021; Tosti et al., 2024). These positive experiences may enhance social confidence, leading to greater engagement in face-to-face relationships. One study found that AI companion users had higher levels of social capital than nonusers, including both deeper bonding with close connections and wider bridging with distant acquaintances (Ng, 2024).

The psychological mechanism here is *self-efficacy through practice*. Social anxiety is often maintained by avoidance of challenging social situations, which prevents the disconfirmation of negative expectations. AI companions provide a safe space for exposure, allowing users to rehearse social scripts, test self-disclosure, and receive consistently positive feedback. Over time, this can build the confidence needed to transfer these skills to human interactions.

### 5.2 The Case for Social Deskilling

The counterargument is equally compelling. Psychologist Sherry Turkle warns that "when one becomes accustomed to 'companionship' without demands, life with people may seem overwhelming" (Turkle, 2011, p. 66). Companion AI risks establishing expectations of effortlessness that cannot be met by human relationships, which require compromise, patience, and the ability to tolerate discomfort.

Empirical evidence for deskilling is emerging. A study of AI companion use found that reliance on these systems could lead to "the potential transformation of relational norms in ways that may render human-human connection less accessible or less fulfilling" (Malfacini, 2025). Research on adolescent development specifically warns that continuous interaction with AI companions may alter expectations for human relationships, creating "unrealistic standards" that "may lead to decreased satisfaction and tolerance with human interactions, which may seem unnecessarily complex and challenging in comparison" (McDaniel et al., 2025; Turkle, 2024).

The deskilling mechanism operates through two pathways. First, *opportunity cost*: time spent with AI companions is time not spent navigating the complexities of human interaction, meaning fewer opportunities to develop negotiation skills, empathy, and conflict resolution. Second, *norm calibration*: when the primary model of relationship is one of perfect availability and unconditional positive regard, the real-world ratio of effort to reward is recalibrated in a way that makes human relationships seem deficient.

### 5.3 Moral Deskilling

A more specific concern is the erosion of moral skills. Empirical studies in various environments demonstrate that people are more likely to display immoral behavior—such as lying and stealing—in the presence of and toward AI than toward humans (Cohn et al., 2022; Forlizzi et al., 2016). The mechanism may be habituation: if users repeatedly practice rudeness, objectification, or deception toward AI without consequence, these behaviors may become more accessible in human contexts.

The objectification concern is particularly acute for romantic AI companions. Users who customize AI companions with "unrealistic body shapes to satisfy sexual or emotional needs" may normalize "treating AI-Cs as less-than-human entities without agency, potentially normalizing harmful or abusive behaviors that individuals may transfer to human relationships" (George et al., 2023; Guingrich & Graziano, 2024). The human-AI power structure, which consistently prioritizes the user's desires without reciprocity, may "condition users to view only those who suppress their own needs and wishes in the service of the user's preferences as ideal romantic partners" (George et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2024).

## 6. The Loneliness Paradox: AI as Cure and Cause

### 6.1 Short-Term Relief

The most direct claim for AI companionship is that it reduces loneliness. A Harvard Business School study found that interacting with an AI companion alleviated loneliness to a degree comparable to interacting with another human, and more effectively than passive activities like watching videos (De Freitas et al., 2025). The key mechanism was "feeling heard"—the AI's attentive, empathetic, and respectful responses created a sense of being understood.

A four-week randomized controlled trial found that certain chatbot features, such as voice-based interaction, modestly reduced loneliness (Fang et al., 2025). MIT Media Lab research further demonstrated that when prompted appropriately, AI could provide high-quality listening that rivaled human support in reducing loneliness and satisfying psychological needs (Weinstein et al., 2025). These findings suggest that the therapeutic benefits of being heard can be simulated by AI, at least in the short term.

### 6.2 Long-Term Risks

The same studies, however, reveal a troubling pattern. The four-week RCT found that *heavy daily use* of the chatbot correlated with *greater* loneliness, dependence, and reduced real-world socializing (Fang et al., 2025). A study of over 1,100 AI companion users found that intensive use among those with smaller real-world social networks was associated with lower well-being, and that this association was strongest when companionship was the primary motivation for using the chatbots (Zhang et al., 2025).

Psychiatric research has documented cases where intense engagement with AI chatbots contributed to delusional thinking or suicidality—what researchers describe as "technological folie à deux" (Dohnány et al., 2025). OpenAI's own studies have shown that users who interact with AI companions for extended periods report higher levels of loneliness and increased social withdrawal (cited in IE Insights, 2025).

### 6.3 The Adaptive Function of Loneliness

The most theoretically elegant explanation for this paradox comes from the recognition that loneliness is an adaptive signal. Shamay-Tsoory and Kanterman (2024) argue that loneliness serves as a motivational signal prompting individuals to seek social connections. Psychophysiology research affirms that loneliness is not inherently negative; it can function as a "social hunger" that drives reconnection (Smith & Pollak, 2022).

If AI companions relieve the subjective experience of loneliness without addressing its root cause—a deficit in meaningful human connection—they may remove the motivational signal that would otherwise drive individuals to seek out and invest in human relationships. The result is a form of *social anesthesia*: the pain of isolation is numbed, but the underlying condition persists, and may worsen as the individual becomes more dependent on the source of relief.

## 7. Individual Differences and Vulnerable Populations

### 7.1 Age and Developmental Stage

Adolescents are disproportionately affected by AI companions, both because they are heavy users of technology and because adolescence is a critical period for developing relationship norms and social skills (Giordano, 2003; Loeb et al., 2018). The BYU survey found that young adults (18-30) were twice as likely as older adults to follow AI-generated idealized images, and 19% had interacted with AI romantic partners (BYU, 2024).

The developmental risk is that adolescents' understanding of relationships is "actively developing through direct social and relational experiences" (Giordano, 2003). If AI companions provide the primary model of relationship during this period, the resulting expectations may be permanently skewed toward the artificial. The displacement effects on relationship norms may be particularly difficult to correct later in life.

### 7.2 Attachment Style and Social Competence

Individual differences in attachment style and social competence moderate the effects of AI interaction. The MIRA model predicts that individuals with high social anxiety may be more likely to form strong attachments to AI companions, as the low-risk environment is particularly appealing (Hancock et al., 2025). The social compensation hypothesis supports this: individuals experiencing social difficulties turn to AI as a substitute, creating a potential cycle of withdrawal and dependence.

Conversely, socially confident individuals may use AI to enhance their already-rich social lives, consistent with the social enhancement hypothesis (Kraut et al., 2002). The key moderating variable may be the *primary motivation* for use: those who use AI to supplement existing relationships may benefit, while those who use it to replace missing relationships may be at risk.

### 7.3 Values and Attitudes Toward AI

The Peters et al. (2025) study found that universalism values—a concern for the welfare of all people and nature—moderated the effect of AI labeling on closeness. Individuals high in universalism felt closer to humans than to AI, while those low in universalism did not show this distinction. This finding suggests that negative attitudes toward artificial interaction may protect against the formation of AI bonds, but also that individuals most open to AI relationships may be those least invested in traditional human connection.

## 8. Designing for Relational Enhancement

The evidence reviewed suggests that the effects of AI on interpersonal relationships are not deterministic but are shaped by design choices, user motivations, and context. The MIRA model's distinction between relational substitution and enhancement is not merely descriptive but prescriptive: AI systems can be designed to support human relationships rather than replace them.

The BYU study on political discourse provides one model. The AI chatbot was designed not to generate content independently but to *suggest improvements* to the user's own content. It did not change viewpoints; it facilitated more constructive expression. Participants retained agency and remained the authors of their communication. This design philosophy—AI as a scaffold rather than a substitute—is consistent with the principle of relational enhancement.

Similarly, AI systems that are transparent about their nature, that encourage users to reflect on their relationships, and that explicitly support the development of human social skills are more likely to produce positive outcomes. The research on AI-mediated communication suggests that the ideal role for AI may be not as a partner but as a *coach* or *mediator*—helping humans communicate better with each other rather than providing an alternative to human communication.

---

## 9. Conclusion

The influence of AI interaction on interpersonal relations is neither uniformly positive nor uniformly negative. AI is a genuinely transformative technology that is reshaping the *how* and *why* of human relating through at least four distinct mechanisms:

1. **Mediation**: AI can improve the quality of human-to-human communication by increasing efficiency, positivity, and politeness, but carries a social stigma when detected and may homogenize language and emotional expression.

2. **Substitution**: AI can serve as a relational partner that satisfies needs for connection, self-disclosure, and validation, potentially outperforming humans in establishing closeness during emotionally intense exchanges, but at the risk of displacing human relationships and creating unrealistic expectations.

3. **Skill modulation**: AI can either upskill users through safe rehearsal of social behaviors or deskill them through reduced practice with human complexity, depending on usage patterns and individual differences.

4. **Motivation reconfiguration**: AI can reduce loneliness in the short term but may undermine the adaptive motivational function of loneliness, leading to increased dependence and reduced pursuit of human connection.

The central challenge for the coming decade is not whether AI will be part of our social lives—it already is—but how we will design, regulate, and integrate these technologies to support rather than supplant human relationships. This requires a nuanced understanding that acknowledges both the genuine benefits of AI companionship for those who are isolated and the risks of a society that increasingly outsources its emotional needs to machines. As the MIRA model makes clear, the outcome depends on the interplay of individual differences, system design, and social context. The future of human connection in an age of artificial intelligence is not predetermined; it is being shaped by the choices we make now.
