

# The Politics of the Past: How Historical Narratives Are Reinterpreted Through Contemporary Political and Social Lenses

## 1. Introduction

The past is never static. It is a resource, a battlefield, and a mirror in which societies see not only what they were but what they wish to become. In recent decades, the study of historical memory has moved from the margins to the centre of scholarly and public debate, as struggles over monuments, curricula, public holidays, and legal frameworks have made visible the profoundly political nature of how we remember. This report analyses how historical narratives are being reinterpreted through contemporary political and social lenses, focusing on three interconnected processes: the **ideologisation of history** (the deliberate infusion of historical interpretation with ideological content), the **instrumentalisation of the past** (the use of historical narratives for present political, social, or economic ends), and the **reclamation of silenced narratives** (efforts by marginalised groups to recover stories that dominant historiography has obscured). It also examines how commemorative practices shape historiography itself and how historical memory serves current agendas, drawing on theoretical frameworks from memory studies, postcolonial theory, and transitional justice.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations: Collective Memory, *Lieux de Mémoire*, and Silencing the Past

### 2.1 Maurice Halbwachs and Collective Memory

The modern study of memory and politics begins with the French sociologist Maurice Halbwachs, who in the 1920s and 1930s developed the concept of **collective memory**. Halbwachs argued that memory is never purely individual; it is always shaped by the social frameworks—family, religion, class, nation—within which individuals are situated. "One never remembers alone," he wrote; collective memory is always selective, and different groups produce different memories of the same events. Halbwachs’s work established that the past is continually reconstructed in the present, a foundational insight for all subsequent memory studies (Halbwachs 1925/1992; see also Coser 1992). This theory remains central to understanding why historical narratives are so fiercely contested: they are constitutive of identity and belonging.

### 2.2 Pierre Nora and *Lieux de Mémoire*

The French historian Pierre Nora extended Halbwachs’s insights in his monumental seven-volume work *Les Lieux de Mémoire* (1984–1992). Nora distinguished between **memory** (a living, organic, continuously evolving bond with the past) and **history** (a critical, intellectual reconstruction). He argued that in modern societies, genuine *milieux de mémoire* (environments of memory) have largely disappeared, replaced by deliberate *lieux de mémoire* (sites of memory)—places, objects, or concepts where memory crystallises and is consciously preserved. These range from monuments and museums to flags, anthems, and even colours. Nora’s work demonstrates that commemorative practices are not neutral; they are political acts that define which pasts are deemed worthy of remembrance (Nora 1989; Wikipedia). The concept of *lieux de mémoire* has been widely adopted to analyse how states and communities construct identity through selective memorialisation.

### 2.3 Michel-Rolph Trouillot and the Silencing of the Past

Perhaps the most powerful critical framework for understanding the politics of historical production comes from the Haitian anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot. In *Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History* (1995), Trouillot examines how power operates at every stage of historical production—from the creation of sources to the making of narratives and the eventual interpretation. He identifies four moments of silencing: the moment of fact creation (what is recorded), the moment of fact assembly (what is archived), the moment of fact retrieval (what is made accessible), and the moment of retrospective significance (what is deemed important). His most famous example is the **Haitian Revolution** (1791–1804), the only successful slave revolt in history, which was systematically **silenced** in Western historiography because it was "unthinkable" within the racial and colonial frameworks of the time. For Trouillot, the "unthinkable" is not that which cannot be thought, but that which cannot be admitted into the dominant narrative. His work provides a crucial lens for understanding why certain histories are marginalised and how power determines what is remembered and what is forgotten (Trouillot 1995; Wikipedia).

## 3. Ideologisation of History: The Weaponization of the Past

The ideologisation of history occurs when historical interpretation is deliberately shaped to serve a particular political ideology. This is distinct from legitimate historical revision, which updates narratives in light of new evidence. Ideologisation often involves the **selective use of evidence**, the **glorification or demonisation of historical figures**, and the **imposition of a single, state-sanctioned narrative** on a complex past.

### 3.1 Far-Right Memory Politics

A defining feature of the contemporary far-right globally is a revisionist engagement with the past. According to a 2025 policy brief from the Berghof Foundation's Global Learning Hub for Transitional Justice and Reconciliation, far-right actors across the world are engaged in memory politics to legitimise their ideologies, undermine democratic institutions, and fuel division. The brief, based on case studies from **Chile, South Africa, South Korea, and Lithuania**, finds that far-right actors challenge established historiography and remembrance culture, promoting distorted historical narratives that fuel hate speech, xenophobia, racism, and political polarisation (Berghof Foundation 2025). In Chile, for example, far-right groups have gathered to commemorate the 1973 coup, seeking to rehabilitate the Pinochet dictatorship as a necessary bulwark against communism. In Lithuania, nationalist actors have sought to downplay the collaboration of some Lithuanians in the Holocaust while emphasising Soviet crimes. These cases illustrate a common pattern: the **instrumentalisation of historical grievances** to construct a narrative of victimhood and justify exclusionary policies.

### 3.2 The Rehabilitation of Authoritarian Figures

In many countries, the ideologisation of history involves the rehabilitation of authoritarian leaders. In **Russia**, the memory of Joseph Stalin has undergone a significant transformation. A March 2019 Levada Center survey found that 51% of Russians held positive attitudes toward Stalin and 70% believed he had played a beneficial role for the country (Soroka 2021). New monuments to Stalin have been erected, including one in Volgograd in 2023, placed not far from an existing memorial to victims of political repression. This is part of a broader "Great Patriotic War" narrative that reinforces the legitimacy of the current Russian state and its military actions (PONARS Eurasia 2024). In **Eastern Europe**, the removal of Soviet-era monuments has become a central feature of post-communist memory politics, with countries like Estonia, Poland, and Ukraine dismantling symbols of Soviet occupation while Russia constructs new ones to counter these narratives (PONARS Eurasia 2024).

### 3.3 Scholarly Perspectives

The scholarly literature on the ideologisation of history emphasises the dangers of what the European Parliament's 2015 study calls "the ideological instrumentalisation and moralisation of the past." The study argues that when historical memory is intertwined with moral categorisation, it "serves more as a cause and hotbed for new conflict than a means for a durable settling of the past." It warns that the "nationalisation and political instrumentalisation of memory hamper not only critical involvement with one’s own history, but also present and future" (European Parliament 2015). A 2023 study in *Práticas da História* notes that the new far right has recognised history as "a key terrain of symbolic and ideological struggle," systematically delegitimising scholarly historical knowledge and portraying academic historians as "leftist ideologues" (Brandi et al. 2023).

## 4. The Instrumentalisation of the Past: Memory Laws and Mnemonic Security

If ideologisation refers to the *content* of historical narratives, instrumentalisation refers to the *mechanisms* by which these narratives are deployed. One of the most significant instruments for shaping historical memory is **law**.

### 4.1 Memory Laws: A Global Overview

Memory laws are legal rules that govern state-approved narratives regarding historical events. They can take many forms: **genocide denial statutes**, **truth and reconciliation frameworks**, **heroization or criminalization of historical figures**, and **heritage protection laws**. According to a 2025 narrative review published in *Interdisciplinary Studies in Society, Law, and Politics*, memory laws serve multiple functions: "promoting national unity, facilitating reconciliation, or entrenching dominant political narratives." The study, which analysed case studies from Eastern Europe, Africa, Latin America, and Asia, found that memory laws are particularly prominent in post-conflict and ideologically divided societies, where they can be used either to foster unity or to exert political control (Li & Wei 2025).

### 4.2 Holocaust Denial Legislation

The earliest and most widespread form of memory law concerns the **Holocaust**. As of 2025, 17 EU Member States have criminalised Holocaust denial, and the 2008 EU Framework Decision on racism and xenophobia requires member states to criminalise the denial, gross minimisation, approval, or justification of genocide and crimes against humanity (European Parliament 2021). These laws are justified as necessary to protect historical truth, combat antisemitism, and preserve the memory of victims. However, they also raise fundamental questions about freedom of expression, academic freedom, and the role of the state in defining historical truth. The European Court of Human Rights has consistently upheld Holocaust denial bans as compatible with the European Convention on Human Rights, arguing that denial constitutes an abuse of rights (European Parliament 2021).

### 4.3 Memory Laws in Eastern Europe

In Eastern Europe, memory laws have taken a different trajectory. As the MEMOCRACY project (2021–2025) documented, post-Soviet states have enacted laws that focus on **denial of communist crimes**, often mirroring Holocaust denial legislation but applied to Soviet-era atrocities. In **Poland**, the Law and Justice (PiS) party introduced legislation criminalising the attribution of Nazi crimes to the Polish state, alongside laws requiring the dismantling of Communist-era monuments. These laws have been criticised for stifling free historical debate, particularly regarding Polish involvement in the Holocaust (Illiberalism.org 2023). In **Russia**, memory laws have been used to criminalise the "rehabilitation of Nazism" and to project the Soviet memory of WWII onto neighbouring countries, signalling what scholars call "mnemonic security"—the use of legal frameworks to defend a state's identity in times of crisis (Verfassungsblog 2025).

### 4.4 Instrumentalisation in the Digital Age

The instrumentalisation of the past is increasingly conducted through digital media. A 2023 study on Russian disinformation notes that historical revisionism is a key tool of information manipulation, with narratives about the demolition of Soviet monuments in Ukraine used to justify aggression. The study concludes that "historical revisionism emerges as a manipulation tool at the service of authoritarianism, imperialist foreign policy goals, and the agendas in the domestic arena of political parties in the extremes of the ideological spectrum" (PMC 2023). The speed and reach of digital platforms amplify the effect of instrumentalised history, making it a potent weapon in contemporary geopolitical conflicts.

## 5. Reclaiming Silenced Narratives: Counter-Memory and History from Below

If the ideologisation and instrumentalisation of history represent the power of dominant groups to shape the past, the **reclamation of silenced narratives** represents the resistance of those who have been excluded. This section examines efforts to recover the stories of women, colonised peoples, Indigenous communities, and other marginalised groups.

### 5.1 Women's Voices in the Partition of India

The 1947 Partition of India, which led to the displacement of approximately 15 million people and the deaths of up to 2 million, is a textbook example of how official historiography can silence gendered experiences. For decades, the dominant narrative focused on high politics—the decisions of Nehru, Jinnah, and Mountbatten—while the experiences of women, who suffered widespread abduction, rape, and violence, were marginalised. Feminist historians and literary critics have worked to reclaim these silenced voices through fiction, oral history, and testimonies. As the scholar Urvashi Butalia documented in *The Other Side of Silence* (1998), women's oral narratives reveal a history that "politics hushed up," recovering women not as victims but as witnesses and storytellers of the nation's most traumatic birth. Works like Amrita Pritam's *Pinjar*, Bapsi Sidhwa's *Cracking India*, and Saadat Hasan Manto's short stories function as a "counter-archive," converting women's fractured voices into memory (Butalia 1998; UniversePG 2025). This is a powerful example of what subaltern studies scholar Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak called "the subaltern speaking"—or, more precisely, the struggle to create conditions in which the subaltern can be heard (Spivak 1988).

### 5.2 Postcolonial and Decolonial Historiography

The reclamation of silenced narratives is central to **postcolonial** and **decolonial** historiography. Scholars such as Ranajit Guha, Dipesh Chakrabarty, and the Subaltern Studies Group have challenged the elitism of colonial and nationalist historiography, arguing that history must be written from the perspective of the oppressed. In *Provincializing Europe* (2000), Chakrabarty argues that European historical categories are inadequate for understanding non-European experiences and that alternative frameworks are needed. The decolonisation of history education has become a global movement, with calls to "decolonise the curriculum" resonating from South Africa's #RhodesMustFall movement to debates in European universities (EuroClio 2021). The removal of the Cecil Rhodes statue at the University of Cape Town in 2015, and the subsequent campaign at Oxford University, symbolised a broader demand that the material and symbolic legacies of colonialism be confronted (Contested Histories 2021).

### 5.3 Indigenous Truth-Telling and Narrative Sovereignty

In settler-colonial states such as Canada, Australia, and the United States, the reclamation of Indigenous histories has been advanced through **truth commissions** and **narrative sovereignty** movements. The Canadian Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) documented the horrors of the Indian Residential School system, forcing a national reckoning with the country's colonial legacy. The Commission's work is a form of **public history** that uses "storylistening" and "storysharing" to shift collective memory (SSRN 2023). The concept of **narrative sovereignty**—the right of Indigenous peoples to control their own stories—has been central to these efforts. The Reclaiming Native Truth project, a national effort to counter discrimination and invisibility, found that "erasure and harmful stereotypes" are the primary mechanisms through which Native peoples are marginalised in the American historical imagination, and that changing these narratives is essential for achieving justice (IllumiNative 2022).

### 5.4 Counter-Memory and the Politics of the Archive

The French philosopher Michel Foucault introduced the concept of **counter-memory** to describe the ways in which marginalised groups create alternative histories that challenge official narratives. This concept has been operationalised in the work of activists and scholars who "read against the grain" of colonial archives, using oral tradition, performance, and art to recover suppressed pasts. The *Women Mobilizing Memory* project at Columbia University, for example, models an alternative to monumental commemorative practices by "giving voice to silenced memories and reclaiming collective memories that have been misrepresented in official narratives" (Columbia University n.d.). These efforts are not merely academic; they are forms of political intervention that reshape collective memory and contribute to struggles for justice, recognition, and equality.

## 6. Commemorative Practices and the Shaping of Historiography

Commemorative practices—monuments, memorials, public rituals, and national days—are among the most visible ways in which societies engage with the past. They are also among the most contested. As the political scientist John R. Gillis wrote in *Commemorations: The Politics of National Identity* (1994), "Memory is as central to modern politics as politics is central to modern memory." Commemorative practices do not simply reflect historical truth; they actively **shape historiography** by determining which events are remembered, how they are interpreted, and who is authorised to speak about them.

### 6.1 The Monument Wars

The decade following the 2015 Charleston church massacre saw an unprecedented wave of monument removals, recontextualisations, and debates across the United States. According to the Southern Poverty Law Center, 126 Confederate monuments had been removed from public ground as of July 2018, but hundreds remained. The debate over Confederate monuments crystallises the tensions between heritage and hate, history and memory. Opponents argue that these monuments, most of which were erected during the Jim Crow era (1890–1920), were intended to assert white supremacy and terrorise African Americans. Supporters claim they honour Southern heritage and military veterans. As Stanford historian James Grossman notes, "We cannot erase these histories simply by taking down the reminders" (AHA 2017). The controversy illustrates how **commemorative landscapes are never neutral**; they are "tools of power" that "continue to shape our collective memory" (Constitution Center 2017).

### 6.2 The Rhodes Must Fall Movement

The #RhodesMustFall movement, which began at the University of Cape Town in March 2015 and spread to Oxford University, represents a transnational challenge to colonial commemorative practices. The statue of Cecil Rhodes—a British imperialist, mining magnate, and architect of apartheid-era segregation—became a symbol of the ongoing legacies of colonialism in higher education. The movement's demand for the statue's removal was accompanied by calls to "decolonise the curriculum" and address institutional racism. The statue at UCT was removed on 9 April 2015, after weeks of protest. At Oxford, the campaign continues, with Oriel College voting to remove the statue in 2020 but facing legal and regulatory obstacles (Contested Histories 2021; Wikipedia). The movement demonstrates how **commemorative objects can become focal points for broader social movements**, forcing institutions to reckon with their historical entanglements.

### 6.3 National Days and Invented Traditions

National days are among the most powerful commemorative practices, as they are occasions for the state to project a unified narrative of the past. However, these days are often contested by groups who feel excluded or misrepresented. In **Australia**, Australia Day (26 January) commemorates the arrival of the First Fleet in 1788, but for many Indigenous Australians, it is "Invasion Day" or "Survival Day," a day of mourning. The growing movement to change the date reflects a fundamental challenge to the settler-colonial narrative of the nation's origins. Similarly, in the **United States**, the celebration of Columbus Day has been increasingly replaced by Indigenous Peoples' Day, reflecting a shift in historical consciousness shaped by decades of Indigenous activism. These examples illustrate how **commemorative practices are sites of ongoing negotiation** over national identity and historical truth.

### 6.4 Museums as Sites of Contested Memory

Museums, particularly national museums, are critical arenas for the shaping of historical narratives. The **National Museum of African American History and Culture** in Washington, D.C., opened in 2016, represents a major institutional effort to centre African American experiences in the American story. However, it has also been subject to political attacks, with claims that it "focuses too much on the negatives—including slavery—of American history" (Yale Macmillan Center 2023). In **Europe**, the *House of European History* in Brussels attempts to present a transnational narrative, but it has been criticised for glossing over colonial violence and Eurocentrism. The role of museums in divided societies is particularly fraught; as a 2024 study in *Ethnopolitics* notes, museums "may exacerbate conflict through the presentation of contentious narratives" (Tandfonline 2024). The tension between commemorative justice and national cohesion is a central challenge for public history.

## 7. Historical Memory and Contemporary Political Agendas

### 7.1 Memory as a Tool of Political Polarisation

Historical memory is a powerful driver of contemporary political polarisation. A 2023 study in the *International Journal of Historical Studies and Civic Affairs* found that "selective remembering, symbolic representations, and power dynamics are key elements influencing contemporary political narratives" globally, with examples from the USA, Canada, Europe, and Africa (Nantongo 2023). In the United States, partisan divisions over the meaning of the Civil War, the legacy of slavery, and the role of Confederate monuments are central to the culture wars. In Europe, divergent memories of World War II, the Holocaust, and communism underpin political cleavages between East and West, as well as between left and right. The rise of populist and far-right parties across Europe has been accompanied by a deliberate strategy of **memory revisionism**, aimed at replacing post-war narratives of guilt and responsibility with narratives of national pride and victimhood (Yale Review of International Studies 2023).

### 7.2 Transitional Justice and the Politics of Memory

In post-conflict societies, the management of historical memory is a critical component of **transitional justice**. The South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), established in 1995, is perhaps the most famous example. The TRC sought to balance truth-finding, amnesty, reparations, and reconciliation. It provided a public platform for victims and perpetrators to tell their stories, and its sessions were broadcast nationally, creating a shared archive of the apartheid era. However, the TRC has been criticised for failing to deliver adequate reparations, for privileging symbolic reconciliation over material justice, and for creating a "middle road" that left many victims feeling betrayed (ICTJ 2023; Cambridge University Press n.d.). The TRC's legacy illustrates the **tensions inherent in any attempt to use history for political healing**: whose truth is told, who gets to speak, and who is held accountable?

### 7.3 Memory and Geopolitics: The Russia-Ukraine War

The ongoing war in Ukraine is, in part, a war over memory. The Russian government has justified its invasion by claiming that Ukraine is a "Nazi" state, invoking the memory of World War II and the Soviet narrative of the "Great Patriotic War." Ukraine, in turn, has accelerated its own memory politics, removing Soviet-era monuments, renaming streets, and promoting a national narrative that emphasises Soviet repression and the Holodomor (the 1932–33 famine) as genocide. The destruction of Ukrainian cultural heritage by Russian forces—including museums, archives, and monuments—is a deliberate act of **mnemonicide**, the killing of memory. As the PONARS Eurasia memo notes, "framings of the past help inform actions and attitudes in the present," and the memory war has implications for public opinion about the conflict (PONARS Eurasia 2024).

### 7.4 The Future of Historical Memory

The digital age is transforming how historical memory is produced, disseminated, and contested. Social media platforms enable the rapid spread of both accurate historical knowledge and dangerous disinformation. They also provide space for marginalised groups to share their own histories, bypassing traditional gatekeepers. However, the algorithms that govern these platforms often amplify polarising content, including revisionist and denialist narratives. The challenge for the future is to develop what the Berghof Foundation calls "inclusive and critical engagement with history" while protecting democratic institutions from the "abuses of the past for political gains" (Berghof Foundation 2025).

## 8. Conclusion

Historical narratives are never simply about the past. They are fundamental to the construction of identity, the legitimation of power, and the pursuit of justice. This report has examined three interconnected processes through which the past is reinterpreted in the present. The **ideologisation of history** is evident in the rise of far-right memory politics, the rehabilitation of authoritarian figures, and the weaponization of historical grievances. The **instrumentalisation of the past** operates through memory laws, educational curricula, and digital disinformation, shaping what can be said and remembered. The **reclamation of silenced narratives** represents a vital counter-movement, as women, colonised peoples, Indigenous communities, and other marginalised groups insist on their right to be heard.

Commemorative practices—monuments, museums, national days—are the arenas in which these struggles are most visibly played out. They are not neutral; they are deeply political, and their meanings are always contested. The work of scholars such as Trouillot, Nora, and Halbwachs provides essential tools for understanding how power operates in the production of history. But that understanding is not enough. As the world becomes more polarised, and as historical memory is increasingly weaponised, the need for critical, inclusive, and democratic engagement with the past has never been more urgent.

The past is not a foreign country; it is a living part of the present. How we remember it will shape the future we inhabit.
