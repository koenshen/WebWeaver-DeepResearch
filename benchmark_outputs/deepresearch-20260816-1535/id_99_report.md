

# Light-Based Therapies in Aesthetic Medicine: Current Applications and Scientific Advances for Photoaging, Skin Brightening, and Hyperpigmentation

## 1. Introduction

Light-based therapies — including lasers, intense pulsed light (IPL), and light-emitting diodes (LEDs) — have become cornerstone modalities in aesthetic dermatology. Their ability to selectively target chromophores such as melanin, hemoglobin, and water enables precise treatment of photoaging, uneven skin tone, and hyperpigmentary disorders including melasma, solar lentigines (age spots), and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH). Over the past five years, technological innovations have significantly expanded the safety, efficacy, and applicability of these treatments across all skin types. This report synthesizes the latest clinical evidence and mechanistic understanding of these modalities as of mid-2026.

---

## 2. Laser Therapies

### 2.1 Picosecond Lasers

Picosecond (ps) lasers deliver energy in pulses measured in trillionths of a second (300–900 ps), producing predominantly photoacoustic (mechanical) disruption of pigment rather than photothermal damage. This property makes them particularly attractive for treating hyperpigmentation with minimal collateral thermal injury.

**Mechanism:** The ultra-short pulse duration generates intense tensile stress that fragments melanosomes into fine particles, which are then cleared by the lymphatic system. Because the pulse is shorter than the thermal relaxation time of melanosomes (~300 ps), heat diffusion to surrounding tissue is minimized.

**Clinical applications in hyperpigmentation and melasma:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies (26 treatment arms) published in December 2025 found that laser therapy significantly improved Melasma Area and Severity Index (MASI) scores, with a pooled standardized mean difference of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.65–1.11, p < 0.00001). The review included both picosecond and Q-switched modalities and concluded that effect sizes were moderate-to-large with rare, mild side effects [[1]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jocd.70602).
- A 2025 study specifically evaluating low-fluence 730-nm picosecond laser in Chinese melasma patients confirmed its safety and efficacy [[2]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12696807/).
- Comparative data show that picosecond lasers achieve faster and better melasma clearance than traditional nanosecond Q-switched lasers, with fewer sessions required [[3]](https://www.jkslms.or.kr/journal/view.html?doi=10.25289%2FML.2020.9.2.166). The "pico-toning" technique — using low-fluence 1064-nm picosecond laser — is now widely adopted in Asian populations for its favorable safety profile.
- However, cautious use is warranted: a 2025 case series reported hypopigmentation following picosecond laser treatment for melasma in a small subset of patients, underscoring the importance of appropriate patient selection and parameter optimization [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41243347).

**Photorejuvenation and skin brightening:** Beyond pigment clearance, picosecond lasers equipped with fractional handpieces (e.g., 532-nm and 1064-nm diffractive lenses) stimulate neocollagenesis and neoelastogenesis, improving skin texture, fine lines, and overall luminosity. The holographic fractional handpiece creates micro-injury zones that trigger regenerative repair without significant epidermal disruption [[5]](https://www.thepmfajournal.com/education/how-i-do-it/post/how-i-do-it-treatment-of-pigmentation-and-melasma).

### 2.2 Fractional Lasers (Ablative and Non-ablative)

Fractional photothermolysis — the creation of microscopic columns of thermal injury (microthermal zones, MTZs) surrounded by spared tissue — has revolutionized skin rejuvenation by enabling rapid healing with reduced downtime.

**Mechanism:** Non-ablative fractional lasers (e.g., 1550-nm erbium-doped fiber, 1540-nm, 1440-nm) coagulate dermal columns while preserving the stratum corneum. Ablative fractional lasers (e.g., CO₂ at 10,600 nm, Er:YAG at 2940 nm) vaporize columns of epidermal and dermal tissue, creating stronger remodeling signals. In both cases, the spared interlesional tissue provides a reservoir of viable keratinocytes and fibroblasts that accelerate re-epithelialization and collagen synthesis.

**Clinical advances in photoaging:**
- A 2024 comprehensive review in the *Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology* concluded that fractional lasers have significantly advanced skin rejuvenation by delivering targeted energy in a pixel pattern, which speeds healing and reduces complications vs. traditional ablative lasers [[6]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jocd.16514).
- Hybrid fractional lasers that combine ablative and non-ablative wavelengths in a single device offer comprehensive rejuvenation with reduced downtime and high patient satisfaction [[7]](https://www.dermatologytimes.com/view/laser-advancements-transform-skin-rejuvenation).
- A notable recent finding suggests that fractional laser treatment can reverse nearly 84% of age-related epigenetic changes in the skin, supporting healthier cellular function at the genomic level [[8]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DaSdBruJJ5L) (preliminary report; formal peer-reviewed data pending).

**Melasma considerations:** Fractional lasers are used cautiously in melasma due to the risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, especially in darker skin types. However, when combined with tranexamic acid injections or topical protocols, some studies show synergistic benefit. The 2024 Latin American consensus on melasma recommends fractional lasers as a second-line procedural option [[9]](https://reference.medscape.com/cc2/p10/treatment-melasma-guideline-2026a1000hzp).

### 2.3 Q-Switched Lasers

Q-switched (QS) lasers (nanosecond domain: 5–100 ns) remain a workhorse for pigmentary disorders. The 1064-nm QS Nd:YAG laser is particularly valued for its deep penetration and relative safety in darker skin types.

- The "laser toning" technique (low-fluence, multiple-pass 1064-nm QS Nd:YAG) is a mainstay for dermal melasma and photoaging-related dyschromia in Asian patients, though it carries a risk of punctate leukoderma with excessive treatments.
- Newer 675-nm lasers have shown promise in melasma treatment with 1-year follow-up data, and a 2025 study confirmed their efficacy in dark skin types (Fitzpatrick IV–VI) [[10]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12783984/).

## 3. Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) Therapy

IPL devices use broadband flashlamps (typically 500–1200 nm) with bandpass filters to deliver non-coherent, polychromatic light. Unlike lasers, IPL emits multiple wavelengths simultaneously, allowing simultaneous targeting of melanin and hemoglobin.

**Mechanism:** Selective photothermolysis of melanin (for pigmented lesions) and oxyhemoglobin (for vascular lesions). The broad spectrum means that absorption by competing chromophores is higher, necessitating careful patient selection.

**Applications in photoaging and dyschromia:**
- IPL is FDA-approved for treating benign pigmented and vascular lesions, hair removal, and signs of photoaging [[11]](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK580525).
- For solar lentigines and diffuse photodamage, IPL provides a "broad refresh" with minimal downtime. Treated spots often darken temporarily before shedding over 7–14 days.
- A recent case series in the *Australasian Journal of Dermatology* (2026) highlighted serious pigmentary complications (PIH and hypopigmentation) in skin of color treated with IPL in cosmetic clinics, emphasizing that device choice, energy settings, and operator experience are critical for safety [[12]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DWN4jzbADta).

**Limitations in melasma:** IPL is generally **contraindicated for melasma** because the broadband, heat-based energy can worsen the condition. The heat can stimulate melanocyte activity and trigger rebound hyperpigmentation. This is a key differentiator from picosecond lasers, which use photoacoustic rather than photothermal mechanisms [[13]](https://www.kraussdermatology.com/blog/ipl-vs-laser-for-hyperpigmentation-whats-the-difference).

**IPL vs. Laser:** Comparative reviews consistently find that lasers offer greater precision and deeper penetration for targeted pigment lesions, while IPL covers larger areas faster. For stubborn dark spots, laser treatments are more effective; for diffuse redness and mild pigmentation, IPL may suffice [[14]](https://www.kraussdermatology.com/blog/ipl-vs-laser-for-hyperpigmentation-whats-the-difference).

## 4. LED Photobiomodulation (Low-Level Light Therapy)

Photobiomodulation (PBM) — also known as low-level laser/light therapy (LLLT) — uses red (600–700 nm) and near-infrared (NIR, 700–1100 nm) light to modulate cellular function without thermal injury.

**Mechanism:**
- Photons are absorbed by cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, leading to dissociation of nitric oxide (NO), increased mitochondrial membrane potential, and upregulation of ATP synthesis.
- Downstream effects include activation of transcription factors (e.g., NF-κB, AP-1), release of growth factors (TGF-β1, VEGF, bFGF), reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS), and modulation of inflammatory cytokines.
- Red light (600–700 nm) penetrates to the dermis and stimulates fibroblast proliferation and collagen/elastin production. NIR light penetrates deeper, improving dermal circulation and reducing inflammatory mediators [[15]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11049838/).

**Clinical evidence for photoaging and skin rejuvenation:**
- A 2024 study by Shurrab et al. (n=30) demonstrated that 660-nm LLLT (15.6 mW/cm², 3 J/cm², 12 sessions) significantly improved melasma severity, skin elasticity, and wrinkle depth, with no adverse effects or re-pigmentation [[16]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38817003/).
- A 2023 review in *Photochemistry and Photobiology* confirmed that red light (600–700 nm) at doses of 15–20 J/cm² increases skin elasticity, density, and reduces roughness and pore diameter [[17]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/photonics/articles/10.3389/fphot.2024.1460722/full).
- Clinical trials of LED arrays (590 nm, 0.1 J/cm², 8 sessions over 4 weeks) showed that over 90% of patients improved by at least one Fitzpatrick photoaging category, and 65% demonstrated global improvement in facial texture and fine lines [[18]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4126803).

**Role in skin brightening:** PBM does not directly destroy melanin; instead, it improves skin brightness indirectly through enhanced cellular metabolism, reduced inflammation, and improved microcirculation, resulting in a more even, radiant complexion. It is often used as a stand-alone treatment or in combination with other modalities (e.g., post-laser recovery) to accelerate healing and enhance outcomes.

**Safety and tolerability:** PBM is extremely safe, with virtually no downtime, and is suitable for all skin types, including those with melasma, where heat-based therapies are risky. This makes it a valuable option for patients with sensitive skin or darker phototypes.

## 5. Comparative Analysis and Safety Considerations

| Modality | Mechanism | Best Indications | Skin Type Suitability | Risk Profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Picosecond laser** | Photoacoustic fragmentation | Melasma, lentigines, PIH, tattoo removal, rejuvenation | All types (including IV–VI) with appropriate settings | Low risk of PIH; rare hypopigmentation |
| **Fractional non-ablative (1550 nm)** | Thermal coagulation of MTZs | Photoaging, rhytides, scars, texture | II–IV; cautious in V–VI | Moderate; transient erythema/edema |
| **Fractional ablative (CO₂)** | Vaporization of MTZs | Severe photoaging, deep rhytides, scars | I–III; higher risk in darker skin | Higher downtime; risk of PIH, infection |
| **Q-switched Nd:YAG** | Nanosecond photothermal/photomechanical | Melasma (toning), lentigines, nevus of Ota | III–V (1064 nm); I–III (532 nm) | Risk of punctate leukoderma with over-treatment |
| **IPL** | Broadband photothermal | Diffuse photoaging, lentigines, telangiectasias | I–III; **not recommended** for melasma or skin of color | Risk of PIH/hypopigmentation in darker skin |
| **LED (PBM)** | Mitochondrial photobiomodulation | Photoaging, inflammation, post-procedure recovery, mild dyschromia | All types | Negligible; no downtime |

**Key safety messages emerging from recent literature:**
- **Melasma is a chronic, relapsing condition** that requires multimodal management including rigorous photoprotection (SPF50+ with iron oxide), topical agents, and careful procedural selection. Lasers should be viewed as supportive tools, not first-line monotherapy [[19]](https://www.sebastianpodlipnik.com/en/melasma-treatment).
- **Skin of color (Fitzpatrick IV–VI)** is at higher risk of both PIH and hypopigmentation from IPL and some laser treatments. Picosecond and low-fluence Q-switched lasers are preferred in this population [[20]](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/13/7/2116).
- **Combination therapy** is a dominant trend in 2025–2026: laser + microneedling, laser + exosomes, laser + radiofrequency, and laser + topical tranexamic acid or cysteamine are frequently reported to enhance outcomes and reduce side effects [[21]](https://thebluffs.com/laser-treatments/laser-skin-treatments-in-2025-innovations-and-what-to-expect).

## 6. Recent Advances and Future Directions

1. **Novel 675-nm laser wavelength** for melasma: Early studies show significant improvement in mixed melasma in dark skin types with 1-year sustained results [[10]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12783984/).

2. **Picosecond laser with fractional diffractive lens array:** This technology enables both pigment fragmentation and collagen induction in a single pass, addressing dyschromia and texture simultaneously.

3. **Organic LED (OLED) for skin rejuvenation:** A 2024 study in *Yonsei Medical Journal* explored the safety and efficacy of OLED-based phototherapy for wound healing and rejuvenation, representing a new frontier in flexible, wearable light therapy [[22]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/photonics/articles/10.3389/fphot.2024.1460722/full).

4. **At-home LED devices:** The market for consumer-grade PBM devices has grown rapidly, with increasing evidence supporting their efficacy for maintenance therapy. The FDA-cleared home-use market is projected to reach $2.6 billion by 2034 [[23]](https://dataintelo.com/report/low-level-laser-therapy-market).

5. **Epigenetic reversal by fractional laser:** Preliminary data suggesting that fractional CO₂ laser can reverse ~84% of age-related epigenetic changes opens a new paradigm for understanding laser rejuvenation at the molecular level [[8]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DaSdBruJJ5L).

6. **AI-guided parameter optimization:** Machine learning algorithms are being developed to predict optimal fluence, pulse duration, and wavelength for individual patients based on skin type, lesion characteristics, and genetic markers, minimizing side effects and maximizing efficacy.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Light-based therapies continue to evolve rapidly, offering increasingly safe, effective, and personalized options for photoaging, skin brightening, and hyperpigmentation. Picosecond lasers represent the leading edge for pigmentary disorders, combining superior efficacy with a favorable safety profile across skin types. Fractional lasers remain the gold standard for textural rejuvenation and photoaging, while LED photobiomodulation provides a gentle, non-invasive option suitable for all patients, including those with melasma. IPL, while still useful for specific indications, requires careful patient selection to avoid complications in darker skin. The integration of these modalities into comprehensive, multimodal treatment plans — alongside rigorous photoprotection and topical regimens — is the consensus approach for optimal outcomes.
