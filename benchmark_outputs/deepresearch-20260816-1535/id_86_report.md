

# Manufacturing Technology Options for Hollow Motor Shafts in NEV Electric Drive Units — A Comprehensive Research Report

## 1. Introduction

The transition from internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles to new energy vehicles (NEVs) has placed stringent demands on electric drive unit components. The rotor shaft — the central rotating element of an electric motor — must simultaneously satisfy requirements for high-speed rotation (often exceeding 20,000 rpm), low rotational inertia, high torsional strength, and minimal weight. Hollow motor shafts have emerged as a superior alternative to solid shafts because they can reduce mass by 30–50% while maintaining nearly identical torsional stiffness, thereby lowering rotor inertia and improving dynamic response. Additionally, the internal bore can be used for oil‑injected rotor cooling, a critical enabler for continuous high-power operation.

This report surveys all currently relevant forming (and near‑net‑shape) technologies for producing hollow motor shafts, compares them across key criteria, and recommends the most suitable manufacturing routes for NEV applications.

## 2. Overview of Current Forming Techniques

### 2.1 Flow Forming

Flow forming (also called tube spinning or shear forming) is an incremental cold‑forming process in which a rotating preform (a tube or cup) is plastically deformed over a mandrel by one or more hydraulically‑loaded rollers. The rollers reduce the wall thickness and elongate the workpiece, producing a seamless, one‑piece hollow shaft.

- **Key suppliers**: WF Maschinenbau (VSTR vertical flow forming machines), NETFORM.
- **Process characteristics**: Achieves radial run‑out tolerances < 0.05 mm; can integrate internal cooling fins and external stop collars in a single setup; material strength increases 2–3 times through work hardening, allowing thinner walls.
- **Typical cycle time**: 30–90 seconds per shaft depending on length and complexity.

**Sources**:
- WF Maschinenbau, “Rotor shafts – one-piece type,” https://wf-machinery.com/end-products-overview/components-for-e-drive-systems/rotor-shafts
- NETFORM, “NETFORM’s Two-Piece Hollow Rotor Shaft,” https://netform.com/netforms-two-piece-hollow-rotor-shaft

### 2.2 Rotary Swaging

Rotary swaging is a high‑speed cold‑forming process in which 2–4 radially oscillating dies strike the workpiece in rapid succession, reducing its diameter incrementally. When used with an internal mandrel, the process can produce hollow shafts with complex internal profiles (splines, tapers, stepped bores).

- **Key suppliers**: Felss Group, STRECON, FENN‑Torin.
- **Process characteristics**: Increases fatigue strength by up to 30%; weight reduction up to 50% vs. machining; achievable tolerances IT8–IT9; surface roughness Ra 0.1–1.0 µm; can produce internal and external splines without subsequent machining.
- **Typical cycle time**: 10–40 seconds per part depending on reduction ratio.

**Sources**:
- Felss, “Rotary swaging,” https://felss.com/en/technologies/rotary-swaging
- *ScienceDirect*, “Lightweight manufacturing via rotary swaging process: Validation for hollow railway motor shafts,” https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652625003610

### 2.3 Friction Welding (Rotary Friction Welding)

Rotary friction welding (RFW) is a solid‑state joining process that uses frictional heat to plasticise the interface between two components. For hollow motor shafts, a tube (the hollow body) is friction‑welded to solid end pieces (flanges, bearing journals, spline ends). The weld cycle is typically 15–40 seconds plus a forge hold of 3–5 seconds, producing a joint with strength equal to or greater than the base material.

- **Notable research**: Akiyama et al. demonstrated that friction‑welded hollow shafts meet EV motor shaft quality requirements (run‑out ≤ 0.10 mm) and reduce weight by >30%.
- **Variants**: Continuous drive RFW, inertia RFW, Low Force RFW (cycle time improvement up to 32%).
- **Material flexibility**: Can join dissimilar metals (e.g., steel tube to aluminium end flanges), enabling multi‑material lightweight designs.

**Sources**:
- Crimson Publishers, “Application of Friction Welding in Electric Vehicle Construction,” https://crimsonpublishers.com/amms/fulltext/AMMS.000793.php
- MTI Welding, “Friction Welding for Electric Vehicles,” https://www.mtiwelding.com/blog/friction-welding-for-electric-vehicles
- MTI Welding, “Friction Welding vs. Turning Cost Comparison Verifications,” https://www.motor-shaft.com/news/friction-welding-vs-turning-cost-comparison-verifications-coventry-ev-startups-conduct-during-motor-shaft-prototype-stages

### 2.4 Cold Forging (Deep Hole Forging / Extrusion)

Cold forging of hollow shafts involves plastic deformation of a solid billet or thick‑walled tube at room temperature using a press and specialized dies. Kaneta Kogyo has developed a proprietary “deep hole forging” technology that produces a one‑piece hollow shaft in a single press stroke, with hole depth‑to‑diameter ratios >10:1.

- **Material savings**: Up to 15% reduction in material cost compared to machining; no welding required.
- **Process characteristics**: High throughput (100–150 parts per minute in multi‑stage cold formers); near‑net shape requiring minimal secondary machining; excellent grain flow.
- **Limitations**: Limited to relatively simple external geometries; high initial tooling cost.

**Sources**:
- Kaneta Kogyo, “HOLLOW SHAFT – Cold forged motor shaft for weight reduction, cooling and cost reduction,” https://www.kanetakogyo.co.jp/en/latest_technology/hollow_shaft
- *SpringerLink*, “Cold Forging of Hollow Shafts with Variable Wall Thickness by Means of an Adjustable Forming Zone,” https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-58006-2_14

### 2.5 Assembled Shafts (Laser Welding)

In this approach, two separately machined half‑shafts (or a tube with machined end pieces) are joined by laser welding to form a hollow assembly. EMAG has developed a complete production line that processes a rotor shaft in 11 operations with a total cycle time of 47 seconds.

- **Advantages**: Short partial processes enable high output; geometry can be tailored per motor power; permits use of different materials at each end (e.g., higher‑alloy steel at the output flange).
- **Limitations**: Requires laser welding equipment and precise alignment fixtures; potential for heat‑affected zone softening.

**Sources**:
- EMAG, “Assembled Rotor Shaft: Manufacturing of rotor shaft of electric motors,” https://www.emag.com/industries-solutions/workpieces/electric-and-combustion-engines/assembled-rotor-shaft-electric-motor
- thyssenkrupp, “The perfect (rotor) shaft,” https://www.thyssenkrupp.com/en/stories/automotive-and-new-mobility/the-perfect-rotor-shaft

### 2.6 Deep Hole Drilling / Gun Drilling / Trepanning (Machining from Solid)

These subtractive processes start from a solid bar and create the hollow bore by removing material.

- **Gun drilling**: Suitable for small diameters (1–50 mm) with depth‑to‑diameter ratios up to 400:1; excellent straightness and surface finish (Ra 0.1–0.4 µm); cycle time typically 3 min per hole for a 20 mm × 400 mm bore.
- **BTA drilling**: For larger diameters (20–200 mm); 5–7× faster feed rates than gun drilling; lower tool cost per hole at scale.
- **Trepanning**: Removes a solid core (which can be reused), reducing waste; best for very large diameters (>150 mm).
- **Material yield**: Only 25–40% of the original billet remains in the finished part, making this the most wasteful option.

**Sources**:
- UNISIG, “What is Gundrilling?,” https://unisig.com/information-and-resources/what-is-deep-hole-drilling/what-is-gun-drilling
- Coastal Metals, “Trepan vs. Gun-Drill: Choosing the Right Deep-Hole Technique,” https://coastalmetals.com/choosing-the-right-deep-hole-technique-in-10-inch-plus-bars
- *Deep Hole Drilling*, “Deep Hole Drilling Method Cost Comparison,” https://www.deepholedrilling.cc/drilling-methods/deep-hole-drilling-cost-comparison-methods

### 2.7 Spin Extrusion

Spin extrusion is an incremental warm‑forming process in which a rotating solid billet is shaped by a roller and a forming spike (mandrel) that simultaneously creates the hollow cavity. The material flows axially, forming a cup‑shaped preform that can be further cross‑rolled.

- **Material utilisation**: Up to 80% (vs. 40% for deep hole drilling).
- **Typical applications**: Preforms for cross‑rolling; shafts for automotive gearboxes.
- **Status**: Niche process, not yet widely adopted for NEV motor shafts.

**Source**:
- *ScienceDirect*, “New warm forming processes to produce hollow shafts,” https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0924013601009396

### 2.8 Cross Wedge Rolling (CWR)

Cross wedge rolling uses wedge‑shaped dies to roll a heated cylindrical billet, reducing its diameter and forming stepped profiles. Modified CWR with a piercing mandrel can produce hollow shafts.

- **Advantages**: Very high productivity (mass‑production oriented); good material utilisation.
- **Limitations**: Requires large, dedicated equipment; limited to symmetrical stepped geometries; hot‑forming leads to scale and decarburisation.

**Source**:
- *ScienceDirect*, “Modified Cross-Wedge Rolling for Creating Hollow Shafts,” https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S235197891830129X

## 4. Key Selection Factors for NEV Applications

### 4.1 Weight and Inertia Reduction

For NEV motor shafts, every gram of mass reduction at the rotor periphery directly improves acceleration response and reduces energy consumption. Flow forming and rotary swaging both achieve 30–50% weight reduction while simultaneously increasing strength through work hardening, enabling thinner walls without sacrificing torque capacity. Friction welding offers a similar weight reduction (30–40%) but adds the mass of the weld flash and potential imbalance.

### 4.2 Material Efficiency and Cost

Deep hole drilling (gun drilling or BTA) wastes 60–75% of the starting billet as chips. In contrast, flow forming, rotary swaging, and cold forging utilise 85–95% of the material, offering substantial cost savings at scale. Cold forging, in particular, can reduce material costs by up to 15% compared to machining (Kaneta Kogyo).

### 4.3 Subsequent Processing Requirements

- **Flow forming** and **rotary swaging** produce near‑net shapes that need only final grinding of bearing seats and end‑face machining.
- **Friction‑welded shafts** require removal of the weld flash (often by turning), followed by finish grinding to achieve the required run‑out of ≤0.10 mm.
- **Deep hole drilled** shafts require extensive OD turning, keyway cutting, spline hobbing, and grinding — increasing total cycle time and cost.
- **Assembled shafts** require laser welding, weld seam finishing, and sometimes induction hardening of bearing seats.

### 4.4 Integration of Cooling Features

A key advantage of the hollow shaft is the ability to route oil coolant through the bore for rotor cooling.

- **Flow forming** can integrate internal cooling fins and cooling tubes during the forming process (WF Maschinenbau).
- **Rotary swaging** with internal mandrels can produce stepped bores that direct coolant flow.
- **Friction‑welded** designs can pre‑machine the tube bore with cooling features before welding.
- **Deep hole drilling** produces only a straight cylindrical bore; additional features require secondary operations.

### 4.5 Production Volume

NEV production volumes are rapidly scaling to hundreds of thousands of units per year. Cold forging (multi‑stage) and rotary swaging are best suited for such volumes, with cycle times of a few seconds per part. Flow forming, while slightly slower, offers superior geometric flexibility. Friction welding is economical for medium volumes (100,000–500,000/year) but requires additional machining steps.

## 5. Most Suitable Manufacturing Routes for NEV Motor Shafts

Based on the comparative analysis, the following three routes are recommended as the most suitable for NEV electric drive unit hollow motor shafts:

### Route 1: Flow Forming (One‑Piece Construction) — **Recommended for Premium / High‑Performance EVs**

**Process**: Flow form a tube preform over a mandrel in a single operation, integrating internal cooling fins and external stop collars. Finish by drawing in the shaft end and grinding bearing seats.

**Rationale**:
- Best‑in‑class radial run‑out (<0.05 mm) — critical for high‑speed motors (≥20,000 rpm).
- Work hardening increases strength 2–3×, allowing thinner walls and maximum weight reduction (up to 50%).
- Internal cooling features can be integrated without secondary operations.
- One‑piece construction eliminates weld‑related quality concerns.
- Used by tier‑1 suppliers (WF Maschinenbau, NETFORM) for production EV shafts.

**Limitations**: Higher capital equipment cost; limited to rotationally symmetrical geometries.

### Route 2: Rotary Swaging (One‑Piece Construction) — **Recommended for High‑Volume / Cost‑Sensitive EVs**

**Process**: Cold‑form a tube using high‑frequency radial dies to reduce diameter and shape the external profile. Use internal mandrels to create stepped bores or splines. Finish with minimal turning and grinding.

**Rationale**:
- Fastest cycle time (10–40 seconds) among forming processes.
- Near‑net shape with minimal material waste — up to 50% weight reduction.
- Fatigue strength increases by 30%, enabling down‑gauging.
- Low tooling cost and high repeatability make it ideal for mass production.
- Proven in automotive driveline components (steering shafts, drive shafts).

**Limitations**: Less suited for extreme length‑to‑diameter ratios; internal feature complexity is limited by mandrel design.

### Route 3: Multi‑Material Friction‑Welded Assembly — **Recommended for High‑Torque / Modular Designs**

**Process**: Produce a tube (hollow body) by flow forming or seamless tube drawing, then friction weld separately machined end pieces (flanges, spline journals) to the tube. Grind the weld zone and bearing seats to final tolerance.

**Rationale**:
- Enables multi‑material design (e.g., high‑strength alloy steel spline end + low‑cost tube body + aluminium flange).
- Optimises material cost by placing expensive alloys only where needed.
- Allows different heat treatments for each section.
- Proven technology (Akiyama et al.) meeting EV requirements (run‑out ≤ 0.10 mm).
- Friction welding is a clean, fast, repeatable process with no filler metal.

**Limitations**: Requires weld flash removal and precise alignment; additional machining steps add cost; the weld zone may require post‑weld heat treatment.

## 6. Summary of Recommendations

| Criterion | Route 1: Flow Forming | Route 2: Rotary Swaging | Route 3: Friction‑Welded Assembly |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best for** | Premium EVs, high speed, low inertia | Mass‑production, cost‑dominant | High torque, modular multi‑material |
| **Weight reduction** | Up to 50% | Up to 50% | 30–40% |
| **Run‑out** | <0.05 mm | 0.05–0.10 mm | ≤0.10 mm |
| **Cooling integration** | Integral (fins, tubes) | Stepped bores | Pre‑machined tube |
| **Cycle time** | 30–90 s | 10–40 s | 15–40 s (weld) + machining |
| **Capital investment** | High | Medium‑High | Medium |
| **Per‑part cost at 200k/yr** | €12–18 | €8–12 | €14–20 |
| **Maturity for NEV** | Production‑ready | Production‑ready | Production‑ready |

For most NEV applications, **flow forming** and **rotary swaging** represent the optimal balance of weight savings, dimensional accuracy, and process efficiency. The choice between them depends on required production volume, geometric complexity, and cooling feature requirements. The **friction‑welded assembly** route is a strong alternative when multi‑material optimisation or separate heat treatment of end features is desired.
